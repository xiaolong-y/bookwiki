Part IV, Chs. 15-18: Utility, MDPs, Multiagent Decisions, Probabilistic Programming

Ch. 15 — Making Simple Decisions

Maximum Expected Utility (MEU) (ch. 15.1)

For action a in evidence e, the expected utility is

EU(a∣e)=∑s′​P(Result(a)=s′∣a,e)U(s′).

The MEU principle: a rational agent should choose a∗=argmaxa​EU(a∣e).

Von Neumann-Morgenstern axioms (ch. 15.2)

Six axioms — orderability, transitivity, continuity, substitutability, monotonicity, decomposability — over preferences on lotteries imply the existence of a utility function U such that the agent’s preferences correspond to expected-utility maximization. Any rational agent must behave as if maximizing some utility function (representation theorem). This is the formal link between behavioural rationality and the standard model of ch. 1.

Value of Information (ch. 15.6)

For an evidence variable Ej​ to be observed,

VPIE​(Ej​)=(∑ejk​​P(Ej​=ejk​∣e)EU(αejk​​∣e,Ej​=ejk​))−EU(α∣e),

i.e. the expected gain from optimally responding to the new evidence minus the current best expected utility. VPI is non-negative and order-independent but not additive (ch. 15.6 properties).

Ch. 16 — Markov Decision Processes (the formal heart of sequential decision-making)

A Markov Decision Process (MDP) is the 4-tuple ⟨S,A,P,R⟩:

  • S = state space (with initial state s0​)
  • A = ACTIONS(s) per-state action set
  • P(s′∣s,a) = transition probabilities (Markov: depend only on s, not history)
  • R(s,a,s′) = reward function, bounded by ±Rmax​

A policy π:S→A assigns an action to each state. An optimal policy π∗ maximizes the expected sum of discounted rewards.

Utility of state sequences (ch. 16.1.1)

With additive discounted rewards and discount factor γ∈[0,1):

Uh​([s0​,a0​,s1​,a1​,…])=∑t=0∞​γtR(st​,at​,st+1​).

Russell justifies γ<1 on (i) human/animal preference-time data, (ii) economic interest-rate equivalence, (iii) implicit termination probability, and (iv) the stationarity of preferences theorem: discounted-additive is the unique utility-on-histories form satisfying stationary preferences.

Bellman equation (ch. 16.2, the eq. 16.5 of the textbook)

The utility U(s) of a state under the optimal policy satisfies

U(s)=maxa∈A(s)​∑s′​P(s′∣s,a)[R(s,a,s′)+γU(s′)].

The optimal action is

π∗(s)=argmaxa∈A(s)​∑s′​P(s′∣s,a)[R(s,a,s′)+γU(s′)].

Value iteration (ch. 16.2.1)

The Bellman update B applied to a utility vector:

Ui+1​(s)←maxa​∑s′​P(s′∣s,a)[R(s,a,s′)+γUi​(s′)].

Theorem (contraction): ∥BU−BU′∥∞​≤γ∥U−U′∥∞​. Hence B has a unique fixed point U∗; value iteration converges geometrically: error ≤γN⋅2Rmax​/(1−γ) after N iterations.

Policy-loss bound: if ∥Ui​−U∗∥∞​<ε, then ∥Uπi​−U∗∥∞​<2ε. In practice πi​ becomes optimal long before Ui​ converges (ch. 16.2.1, Fig. 16.8).

Policy iteration (ch. 16.2.2)

Alternates policy evaluation (solve ∣S∣ linear equations Uπ(s)=∑s′​P(s′∣s,π(s))[R(s,π(s),s′)+γUπ(s′)]) with policy improvement (π′(s)←argmaxa​…). Terminates in finitely many steps for finite MDPs.

POMDPs (ch. 16.4-16.5)

When the state is partially observable, the agent maintains a belief state b(s)=P(s∣percepts). The Bellman equation lifts to belief space:

U(b)=maxa​[r(b,a)+γ∑o​P(o∣b,a)U(b′)].

Exact POMDP planning is PSPACE-hard; modern approximations (point-based value iteration, online tree search) make medium-scale POMDPs tractable.

Ch. 17 — Multiagent Decisions

  • Non-cooperative game theory: Nash equilibrium is a strategy profile σ∗ such that no player gains by unilateral deviation. Pure-strategy NE need not exist (matching pennies); mixed-strategy NE always exist for finite games (Nash 1950).
  • Cooperative game theory: the Shapley value uniquely satisfies efficiency, symmetry, dummy-player, and additivity axioms; it is the “fair” payoff allocation.
  • Mechanism design / social choice: Arrow’s impossibility (no rank-aggregation rule satisfies universal-domain + non-dictatorship + Pareto + IIA) and Gibbard-Satterthorn (every non-dictatorial deterministic voting rule with ≥3 alternatives is manipulable).
  • Assistance games (Hadfield-Menell et al. 2017, ch. 17.5): a 2-player game where the AI is uncertain about the human’s reward function θ and the human’s actions are evidence about θ. The optimal AI policy in an assistance game defers to the human, asks clarifying questions, and avoids irreversible actions — this is the formal model behind the beneficial-machines pivot of ch. 1.1.5.

Ch. 18 — Probabilistic Programming

Lifts ch. 13 to first-order probabilistic languages (BLOG, Church, Pyro, PyMC):

  • Relational probability models (RPMs) replace Bayes-net nodes with relational templates; a finite model corresponds to a possibly-large flat Bayes net.
  • Open-universe probability models (OUPMs) drop the closed-world assumption — the number of objects is itself uncertain. Used in citation matching, multi-target tracking, particle physics.
  • Programs as probability models: arbitrary stochastic programs define implicit joint distributions; inference is via importance sampling, MCMC over execution traces, or variational inference over program structure.

Why this is the most reusable part of the book

The MDP/Bellman framework (ch. 16) is the formal substrate for all of reinforcement learning (ch. 23), most of robotics (ch. 26), and the assistance-game line of alignment research (ch. 17.5, 28). If a reader can write down the Bellman equation from scratch and prove the contraction, they have the prerequisite for ~80% of the post-2010 AI safety literature.
