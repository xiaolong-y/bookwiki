Low-Hanging Fruit for Better (Global) Health?

Ch. 3 — Low-Hanging Fruit for Better (Global) Health?

The puzzle

Cheap, evidence-backed health technologies — chlorine bleach for water, ORS (sugar+salt+water) for diarrhea-dehydration, ITNs for malaria, deworming pills, basic immunization, exclusive breastfeeding, iodized salt, iron-fortified flour — exist and could save the bulk of the 9 million annual under-5 deaths. But uptake is dismally low: in Zambia, 98% of households know Chlorin treats water but only 10% use it ($0.18/month); in India, only ⅓ of under-5 diarrhea cases get ORS; <40% of world infants are exclusively breastfed; basic immunization rates in rural Udaipur are 5% before intervention.

The Sachs frame

Health-poverty trap: malaria-prevalent countries are 1/3 the per-capita income of malaria-free ones; sick adults are unproductive → countries can’t afford eradication → stuck. Eradication push pays for itself many times over (Bleakley/Lucas: malaria eradication in US South 1951 and Latin America → 50% lifetime income gain for malaria-free cohorts; smaller but positive in India, Paraguay, Sri Lanka). Bed-net cost-benefit: 14net,5−yearlife,30590 Kenyan adult income = $295 expected gain → dominates dramatically.

Why isn’t the market plucking the fruit?

  1. Government supply-side dysfunction. World Bank Absenteeism Survey (2002-03, 6 countries): 35% nurse absenteeism on average (43% in India). Udaipur: 56% of health-post visits found facility closed. Das & Hammer “3-3-3 rule” for Delhi private/public clinic visits: 3 minutes, 3 questions, 3 medicines, often injectable steroids+antibiotics. ⅔ of unqualified urban “doctors” had no medical degree.
  2. Demand-side puzzle. Even when nurses are present (Seva Mandir intervention raised from 40% to >60% attendance), patient flow does not increase. Even when free monthly immunization camps run with clockwork regularity in Udaipur, only 6% → 17% full-immunization conversion.
  3. Cure-not-prevention bias. Udaipur poor saw a doctor every 2 months on average; <¼ of visits to public facility, >½ to private (often unqualified), remainder to bhopas (traditional exorcists). Spending was on antibiotic shots and saline drips, not on cheap prevention.

Mechanisms (this chapter’s theoretical contribution)

“Free means worthless”? — refuted

Cohen-Dupas TAMTAM bed-net experiment in Kenya: demand collapses as price rises (almost everyone takes free; near-zero at PSI’s $0.75 price). But conditional on receiving a net at any subsidized level, 60-90% of households used it, with no usage difference by price paid. The Easterly “subsidies-as-wedding-veils” anecdote does not survive RCT replication. Subsidy is fine; the failure mode is somewhere else.

Faith and theory

Patients lack the high-school biology that would let them reject “medicine must enter the bloodstream” → they prefer injectables → they are over-medicated in unsterile conditions → drug-resistant bacterial strains and steroid-induced premature aging. Self-limiting illnesses (most outpatient visits) feed spurious causal attribution to whatever was prescribed. Immunization, which prevents events that may not have happened anyway, is structurally unteachable from individual experience.

Hope and the placebo function of healthcare

A Bengali doctor: the poor cannot afford treatment for chronic disease (chest pain, blood in urine), so they show up only for self-limiting conditions to receive any treatment that signals doing-something. This explains the bhopa/healer/preacher market — symptoms come and go, attribution is generous, hope is the consumed good.

Time inconsistency / nudge

Seva Mandir + the authors’ RCT: offering 2 lbs of dal (~$1.83 PPP) per immunization shot raised full-immunization rates from 6% to 38% — a 6× jump from a small immediate reward. Stainless-steel-plate completion bonus did less. Mechanism: the cost (couple of hours, mild post-shot fever) is immediate, the benefit (avoided measles) is future and probabilistic. Standard hyperbolic-discounting present-bias means a tiny immediate offset can flip the decision. Distributing dal also lowered cost-per-immunization because fixed-cost nurse time was used more efficiently.

”Don’t bribe people for what’s good for them” — argued against

Both right (markets-only) and left (paternalism-of-information) traditions oppose incentives. Authors disagree on two grounds: (a) “beliefs” the poor hold are mostly weak (the dal experiment shows: evil-eye fear isn’t strong enough to refuse 40 rupees), and (b) action does not follow intention without scaffolding. Apply Thaler-Sunstein Nudge: defaults, small immediate incentives, friction-reduction (Kremer’s free chlorine dispenser at the village well — turn-the-knob auto-dose; cheapest known intervention against diarrhea per RCT). The authors’ Seva Mandir iron-fortified-flour program failed because the default flipped: millers had no incentive to fortify unless asked, and the small “ask” cost killed adoption.

Policy implication

  • For prevention with positive externalities (immunization, sanitation, nets), subsidies should sometimes be greater than 100% — pay people to consume.
  • Default-design matters as much as price: piped chlorinated water > chlorine bottles, regardless of household income.
  • Government should NOT primarily run delivery (absenteeism / 3-3-3 problem) but should regulate, fund, and audit local NGOs / private providers.

Why this chapter is high-leverage

It introduces three behavioral mechanisms (faith-as-hope, time-inconsistency, default-as-equilibrium) that recur in chs. 4 (education uptake), 5 (contraception), 6 (insurance), 8 (savings). It also demonstrates the RCT-as-mechanism-discrimination method: the price-sensitivity-vs-usage decoupling refutes Easterly directly with empirical evidence.
