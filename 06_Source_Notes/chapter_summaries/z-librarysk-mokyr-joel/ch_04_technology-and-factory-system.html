Technology and the Factory System

Technology and the Factory System

Chapter 4 — Technology and the Factory System

Formal spine

  • Locus of production = the physical site where work is carried out. Independent of firm-vs-plant distinction (Coase/Williamson) and of ownership of capital.
  • Driver of locus: relative cost of moving people vs. moving information; relative complexity of competence required vs. capacity of an individual household to absorb it.
  • Four explanations of the factory (Mokyr surveys then synthesizes):
    1. Fixed costs / scale economies — large indivisible equipment (steam engines, puddling furnaces, silk-throwing mills) makes household-scale production infeasible.
    2. Information asymmetry — piece-wage monitoring breaks down when capital is shared, embezzlement of raw materials is easy, and quality is hard to verify in finished goods.
    3. Effort enhancement — Marglin/Clark: workers are placed under one roof to extract more time and effort.
    4. The division of knowledge (Mokyr’s added explanation) — when the competence required to operate best-practice technique exceeds what a single household can carry, knowledge must be specialized across workers and the factory becomes the natural locus of access.
  • Prediction: when knowledge can be transmitted electronically, large plants become less necessary (telecommuting, distributed work). The Industrial-Revolution factory was a specific solution to a specific access-cost problem.

Core argument

The chapter situates the rise of the factory inside the same Ω/λ framework. Pre-1780 manufacturing was an “industry without industrialists” (Crouzet) — most production was domestic, with merchant-entrepreneurs running putting-out networks. Large workshops existed (Lombe’s Derby silk mill, Crowley’s Stourbridge ironworks) but were exceptions and were typically blends with domestic outsourcing. The Industrial Revolution made factories the rule rather than the exception — by 1830s Manchester the average cotton mill had 400 workers and the domestic spinner had vanished.

Mokyr surveys the three standard explanations and argues each is partly right but insufficient on its own. Fixed costs and scale economies are the most obvious driver — Arkwright’s water frame, Cort’s puddling furnaces, and integrated cotton mills required equipment that did not fit in a worker’s cottage and that had to be operated continuously to amortize the capital. Information-asymmetry arguments (Lazear, Hart-Grossman) explain why piece-wage putting-out worked when products were homogeneous and quality was easily verified, and broke down when capital was shared and quality became multidimensional. Effort-enhancement arguments (Marglin’s Marxist version, Clark’s “self-coercion” inversion) capture the social-control aspect of factory discipline but cannot by themselves explain the timing.

The chapter’s distinctive contribution is the division-of-knowledge argument, drawn from Demsetz (1988) and formalized by Becker and Murphy (1992). As best-practice λ-techniques became more complex, the competence needed to operate them began to exceed what any single household could store. Specialization of knowledge becomes inevitable. Because knowledge transmission in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries depended overwhelmingly on face-to-face contact, sharing a chopped-up body of competence required workers to be physically near one another. The factory is, in this view, a knowledge commons: a building inside which marginal access cost between an unskilled worker and the firm’s accumulated technical knowledge is minimized, and inside which a foreman, a chemist, a machinist, a mechanic, an accountant, and a manager can each carry a manageable piece of the total competence and supply it to the others on demand.

This framing yields a falsifiable prediction. If large plants existed primarily to minimize knowledge-access costs in a world where face-to-face contact was the bottleneck transmission medium, then a sharp drop in those costs (electronic communication, broadband internet, video conferencing, distributed collaboration tools) should reduce the optimal plant size and revive distributed work. The chapter’s epigraph from Frances Cairncross (1997) — “in half a century’s time, it may well seem extraordinary that millions of people once trooped from one building to another each morning” — is exactly this prediction. Mokyr is careful not to over-commit, but the framework predicts the partial reversal of the Industrial-Revolution centralization that has played out in the IT and post-2020 era.

The chapter also catalogs the welfare consequences of the factory transition, often understated by income-per-capita statistics: loss of leisure-income choice (workers could no longer trade hours for income at the margin); loss of timing-of-leisure freedom; commuting costs; nonpecuniary disutility of dark-Satanic-mill conditions; collapse of joint household production; and the emergence of human-capital formation as a firm-level concern (factory-financed Sunday schools, indenture contracts, in-house training). Workers may have ended up worse off than under the old domestic system but better off than the only available alternative once the cottage industry’s competitive rents had been crushed.

Important examples

  • Lombe’s Derby silk throwing mill (1718, 300 workers, 5 stories) and Crowley’s Stourbridge ironworks (1682, 800 workers) as pre-IR factory exceptions.
  • Arkwright’s Cromford works (300 workers) and New Lanark mills (1,600 workers in 1815).
  • Cyfarthfa ironworks: 1,500 men in 1810, 5,000 in 1830.
  • Demsetz/Becker-Murphy formalization of the firm as a knowledge-coordinator solving the “more knowledge needed than any one person can hold” problem.
