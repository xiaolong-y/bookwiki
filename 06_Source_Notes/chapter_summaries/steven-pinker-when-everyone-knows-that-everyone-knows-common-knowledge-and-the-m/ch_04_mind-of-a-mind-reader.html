Chapter 4 — Reading the Mind of a Mind Reader: Bystanders, Bank Runs, Beauty Contests

The chapter’s question: how do humans implement common knowledge in a finite skull? Three formal models for representing common knowledge are presented, then tested against empirical cognitive psychology and applied to financial markets and bystander behavior.

Three formal representations.

  1. Iterated/recursive propositions — list “Amy knows X, Brad knows X, Amy knows that Brad knows X…” with infinite tail. The list cannot fit in a finite skull, but the ”…” tail can stand in for the infinite series.
  2. Reflexive/fixed-point representation — “(n): Amy and Brad know X, and Amy and Brad know n” as a self-referential proposition. Mathematically clean but cognitively unintuitive.
  3. Self-evident events — “Amy and Brad experienced a self-evident event that implies X” where a self-evident event (joint attention, public announcement, conspicuous public happening) is by definition known-to-all. Herbert Clark and others argue this is the most plausible psychological implementation.

Empirical cognitive limits. The Kinderman-Dunbar-Bentall study had college students answer questions about embedded mental states (“John thought that Penny knew what Sheila wanted to do” = third-order). Performance is solid up to fourth-order; falls off a cliff at fifth-order. The chapter notes that humans can “chunk” recurring patterns of embedded mental states into single semantic units (bluff, conceal from, deceive, doubt, humor, persuade, suspect) and into longer scripts (affair, scandal, argument); chunked patterns can effectively support more layers without exceeding working-memory.

Pinker’s research team’s experiments. Thomas, DeScioli, Haque, and Pinker (and later De Freitas, Lee, Lindner) ran online studies in which participants imagined being a butcher or baker (Stag Hunt structure) facing different states of knowledge about hot-dog prices.

  • Private knowledge (only the participant knows): ~20% attempt coordination
  • Public knowledge (loudspeaker announcement): ~80% attempt coordination
  • Intermediate orders (second/third-order): ~50% attempt coordination, often committing the “turtles all the way down” fallacy or having the higher-order knowledge collapse into lower-order in working memory

The De Freitas-Pinker follow-up tested whether participants infer that a public event entails any number of embedded knowledge states. They presented an 18-level-embedded sentence about a public car crash; participants overwhelmingly endorsed it as something Edward “has reason to believe.” Self-evidence is the cognitive substrate humans actually use.

Keynesian beauty contests. Keynes 1936 likened stock-market investing to a newspaper beauty contest where readers must pick the prettiest face anticipating what other readers will pick. “We have reached the third degree where we devote our intelligence to anticipating what average opinion expects the average opinion to be. And there are some, I believe, who practise the fourth, fifth and higher degrees.”

Mehta-Starmer-Sugden (replicating Schelling 1960) showed empirical recursive mentalizing: when paired participants must match each other’s choice, they shift from idiosyncratic favorites toward stereotypical responses (rose for flower, red for color, John for boy’s name, London for city, 1 for number). Rosemary Nagel’s “guess two-thirds of the average” experiment found people guess ~36 (one or two layers of recursion), not 0 (full common-knowledge convergence). In real-world newspaper-contest replications about 15% chose 0; the most successful contestants ran little experiments among friends to find the population-specific best response.

Bank runs and asset bubbles. Pinker treats these as Stag Hunts. Two equilibria — keep money in (best for everyone) or withdraw early (second-best); each depositor fears being last to withdraw. Hofstadter’s reverberant doubt names the phenomenon. Banks’ marble columns (and the Pete-Seeger-Weavers song “The Banks Are Made of Marble”) were common-knowledge generators of solvency. FDIC plaques replaced marble in the post-1933 era. FDR’s “the only thing we have to fear is fear itself” is “literally true: the fear of fear itself was the immediate cause of the bank runs.”

The 2023 Silicon Valley Bank collapse is the chapter’s recent case: Twitter-coordinated withdrawals generated common knowledge in hours. Matt Klein’s “more a case of a ‘bank-run by idiots’ than a ‘bank run by idiots’” captures the cascade dynamic. The 2020 toilet-paper hoarding (Wolfers’s analysis) is structurally identical and traces back to a 1973 Johnny Carson monologue joke that was false at the time but self-fulfilling within days; toilet-paper-as-emergency-commodity became a culturally installed focal point.

Meme stocks. GameStop 2021 (Keith Gill’s “Roaring Kitty”); May 2024 leaning-forward cartoon tweet tripled the stock in two days. Pinker’s framework: any conspicuous focal point that brings an asset into mass consciousness can ignite a Keynesian beauty contest, regardless of fundamentals. Closed-end mutual fund example: Caribbean Basin Fund (ticker CUBA) jumped from -10% NAV discount to +70% NAV premium when Obama announced relaxed Cuba relations, despite the fund holding no Cuban assets.

Inflationary spirals, recessions, currency runs. All exhibit the same common-expectation structure. Greenspan: “I’ve learned to mumble with great incoherence… If I seem unduly clear to you, you must have misunderstood what I said.” Alfred Kahn (Carter’s inflation czar, told to avoid the word depression): “We’re in danger of having the worst banana in 45 years”; switched to kumquat when banana producers protested. Central-bank communication is precisely-imprecise common-knowledge management.

Bystander apathy. The 1964 Kitty Genovese case (the 38 witnesses number was largely fabricated; only 6 actually witnessed the attack) inspired the Darley-Latané laboratory studies that found inactive-confederates suppress emergency response 80% to 30%. Diekmann reframed this as the Volunteer’s Dilemma: each bystander prefers someone-else-intervenes > self-intervenes > no-one-intervenes; the optimal mixed strategy depends on payoff ratios and the number of potential volunteers. The Pinker-team experiment manipulated state of knowledge: with private knowledge, volunteer rate is high; with second-order knowledge (you know someone else knows), shirk; third-order, volunteer; fourth-order, shirk; common knowledge produces the textbook bystander effect (more bystanders = less helping).

Joe Biden’s June 2024 dropout. Many of his advisors privately knew of his cognitive decline; voters told pollsters they were worried; but Biden, allies, and the Democratic Party supported his reelection bid as if all was normal. After the June 2024 debate, the percentage doubting his fitness rose only “by a few percentage points,” but the private knowledge became common knowledge, and within four weeks Biden bowed out. Pinker’s freshest illustration of the private-to-common-knowledge phase shift.
