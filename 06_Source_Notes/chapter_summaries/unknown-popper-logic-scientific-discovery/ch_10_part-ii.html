Part II — Some Structural Components of a Theory of Experience (chs. 3-10, sec. 12-85)

Spine

Eight chapters of formal-and-applied apparatus that operationalises the Part I programme. Chapters 3-7 build the formal layer (theories, falsifiability, basic statements, degrees of testability, simplicity); chapters 8-9 handle probability and quantum theory; chapter 10 closes the book with corroboration and “The Path of Science”. Popper’s case is not made by his rhetoric — it is made here.

Chapter 3, Theories (sec. 12-18)

Causal explanation = deducing a singular statement from a universal law plus initial conditions (the thread-and-weight example, sec. 12, p. 38). Popper avoids “the principle of causality” because it is unfalsifiable (sec. 12, p. 39). Strict universality (an “all-statement” about any place and time) is distinguished from numerical universality (a statement equivalent to a finite conjunction of singular statements; sec. 13). Universal vs. individual concepts (sec. 14); strictly universal vs. existential statements (sec. 15); theoretical systems as deductive closures (sec. 16); axiom-system interpretation (sec. 17); levels of universality and modus tollens (sec. 18).

Chapter 4, Falsifiability (sec. 19-24)

The conventionalist objection (Poincaré, Duhem) — a theory can always be rescued — is the central foil (sec. 19). Popper’s reply is methodological: the empirical method, as a convention, prohibits these rescues (sec. 20). The logical investigation of falsifiability (sec. 21) defines falsifiability as a property of a system of theory plus initial conditions: a system is falsifiable iff it forbids some logically possible singular statement. Falsifiability (a logical property of a statement) is sharply distinguished from falsification (a methodological act of refutation; sec. 22). Occurrences vs. events as the building blocks of falsifying basic statements (sec. 23). The internal consistency of the falsifiability criterion (sec. 24).

Chapter 5, The Problem of the Empirical Basis (sec. 25-30)

The Vienna Circle’s protocol sentences (Carnap, Neurath) treated as foundational singular statements. Popper rejects (sec. 25-27): perceptual experiences cannot logically justify statements; only statements can justify statements. Basic statements (sec. 28) are accepted by convention under the inter-subjective-testability rule. Fries’ trilemma (dogma vs. infinite regress vs. psychologism) is dissolved by accepting that tests do not bottom out in certainty but in conventionally-accepted singular statements that could in principle be tested further (sec. 29). Theory and experiment are mutually entangled — observation is theory-laden (sec. 30).

Chapter 6, Degrees of Testability (sec. 31-40)

The book’s most technical body chapter, and the source of the formal layer that distinguishes Popper from a generalised methodological-virtue advocate. Sec. 31-32: the programme — make falsifiability comparable. Sec. 33-34: the subclass relation between potential-falsifier classes — theory A is more falsifiable than theory B iff A’s class of forbidden statements properly includes B’s. Sec. 35: empirical content = the class of forbidden statements. Sec. 36: levels of universality (a strictly more general theory is more falsifiable) and degrees of precision (a more precise theory is more falsifiable). Sec. 37-40: logical ranges, dimension of a theory, set-of-curves dimension reduction. The complement of empirical content is logical probability — so high empirical content means low logical probability. The inductivist’s preference for “probable” theories is therefore inverted: the less probable a theory is, the more it says about the world.

Chapter 7, Simplicity (sec. 41-46)

Simplicity is reduced to degree of falsifiability. A “simpler” theory is one that forbids more, has higher empirical content, has lower logical probability. The aesthetic and pragmatic notions of simplicity (sec. 41) are rejected as conflating the empirical-content meaning with subjective preferences. Geometrical shape and functional form (sec. 44), the simplicity of Euclidean geometry (sec. 45), and conventionalism’s appeal to simplicity as an aesthetic preference (sec. 46) are addressed.

Chapter 8, Probability (sec. 47-72)

The longest chapter. Subjective vs. objective interpretations (sec. 48). The fundamental problem of the theory of chance (sec. 49) — how to ground probability statements in the world. Von Mises’ frequency theory (sec. 50). Popper’s own axiomatisation building on relative frequency in finite classes (sec. 52-56). Bernoulli’s theorem (sec. 60-63). The decidability of probability statements (sec. 65). The theory of range (sec. 72) generalising frequency. Mostly essential only for philosophy-of-probability specialists, but the technical machinery feeds directly into sec. 80 (probability of hypotheses) and into the new appendices *iv-*v on the formal calculus of probability.

Chapter 9, Some Observations on Quantum Theory (sec. 73-78)

Heisenberg’s programme (sec. 73). The statistical interpretation of quantum theory (sec. 74). Popper’s statistical re-interpretation of the uncertainty formulae (sec. 75) — the uncertainty relations are scatter relations about ensembles, not in-principle limits to single-system measurement. An attempt to eliminate metaphysical elements by inverting Heisenberg’s programme (sec. 76). Decisive experiments — experiments designed to choose between competing theories — proposed (sec. 77). Indeterminist metaphysics distinguished from indeterminist physics (sec. 78).

Chapter 10, Corroboration, or How a Theory Stands up to Tests (sec. 79-85)

The destination chapter. Sec. 79 (“Concerning the So-Called Verification of Hypotheses”) rejects the verification idea: theories are not made true by past predictive successes; they are made corroborated (provisionally trusted in proportion to severity-of-tests-passed). Sec. 80 is the technical core: probability-of-hypotheses is shown not to be reducible to probability-of-events (Reichenbach’s truth-frequency proposal fails; the construction either gives universal laws probability zero or makes their probability insensitive to falsification). Sec. 81 extends the criticism to inductive logic generally. Sec. 82 (“The Positive Theory of Corroboration”) builds the alternative: a theory’s degree of corroboration is a non-probabilistic report of severity-of-tests-passed, updated over time. Sec. 83 connects corroborability, testability, and logical probability — high corroborability requires high empirical content requires low logical probability. Sec. 84 distinguishes “true” from “corroborated”. Sec. 85 (“The Path of Science”) closes the book: science is quasi-inductive (it ascends from theories of lower universality to theories of higher universality, each new layer containing the corroborated portion of the old as a limiting case); science does not converge on truth and never claims to. “We do not know: we can only guess” (p. 278). The 1972 Addendum at the end of sec. 85 adds verisimilitude (truth-likeness) as the regulative ideal that corroboration tracks but does not measure.

Why Part II matters for the book

Part II contains the formal-technical-applied apparatus that distinguishes Popper from a methodological exhortation. Without it, falsifiability is a slogan; with it, falsifiability is a comparable, formally-defined property linked to empirical content, logical probability, simplicity, and (via sec. 80 and Apps. *vii-*ix) a non-probabilistic alternative to truth-frequency.

The chapters with the highest leverage:

  • Chapter 4 (sec. 19-24) — the formal investigation of falsifiability and the methodological reply to conventionalism.
  • Chapter 5 (sec. 25-30) — basic statements as conventions; the protocol-sentence dispute resolved.
  • Chapter 6 (sec. 31-40) — the comparison machinery (subclass relation, empirical content, dimension).
  • Chapter 10 (sec. 79-85) — corroboration and the path of science.

A reader with time for one chapter of Part II should read Chapter 10. With time for two, add Chapter 6. With time for three, add Chapter 4. Chapters 8 and 9 are essential only for philosophy-of-probability and quantum-foundations specialists.
