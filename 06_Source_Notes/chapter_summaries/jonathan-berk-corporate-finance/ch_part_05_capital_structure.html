Part 5 — Capital Structure (Chapters 14–17)

Formal spine. The Modigliani-Miller theorems and their progressive enrichment as frictions are introduced.

Chapter 14 — Capital Structure in a Perfect Market. The benchmark.

MM Proposition I (Ch. 14.2). Under three perfect-capital-market conditions — (1) investors and firms trade the same securities at competitive prices equal to PV of cash flows; (2) no taxes, transaction costs, or issuance costs; (3) financing decisions do not change the cash flows or reveal new information — the total value of a firm is independent of its capital structure:

VL​=VU​,

i.e. the market value of the levered firm VL​=E+D equals the market value of the otherwise-identical unlevered firm VU​. The proof is arbitrage via homemade leverage: any investor who prefers different leverage from the firm’s choice can borrow or lend on personal account at the same rate as the firm and replicate the alternative payoff. If VL​=VU​ for two firms with identical assets, an arbitrageur shorts the overvalued firm, longs the undervalued firm plus a homemade-leverage adjustment, and earns a riskless profit until prices converge — a direct application of the Law of One Price (Ch. 3).

MM Proposition II (Ch. 14.3). The cost of equity for a levered firm rises linearly with the debt-equity ratio:

rE​=rU​+ED​(rU​−rD​),

where rU​ is the unlevered cost of capital and rD​ is the cost of debt. The intuition: debt financing concentrates cash-flow risk on the residual equity claim, so equity-holders demand a higher expected return as leverage rises. The pretax WACC (i.e. the cost of capital of the firm’s assets) is invariant to capital structure:

rpretax-wacc​=E+DE​rE​+E+DD​rD​=rU​.

This is the “leverage just rebalances the slices of a fixed pie” intuition. Beta version of MM II: βU​=E+DE​βE​+E+DD​βD​ — the firm’s asset beta is a value-weighted average of equity and debt betas.

Chapter 15 — Debt and Taxes. First friction: corporate taxes.

  • Interest Tax Shield. Interest is deductible from taxable corporate income at marginal rate τC​. For a firm paying interest It​ in year t: Interest Tax Shieldt​=τC​×It​.
  • Levered firm value with permanent debt. PV of the tax shield is τC​×D (treating the tax shield as a perpetuity at rD​ on permanent debt D): VL​=VU​+τC​D. This is the MM with corporate taxes version of Proposition I. Implication: pure tax considerations push the firm toward 100% debt financing.
  • Personal taxes. Once we add personal taxes on interest income (rate τi​) and equity income (effective rate τe​ blending dividend and capital-gains rates), the effective tax advantage of debt is τ∗=1−1−τi​(1−τC​)(1−τe​)​, which can be substantially smaller than τC​ alone — the Miller (1977) personal-tax offset.
  • WACC with taxes (the formula students memorise): rwacc​=E+DE​rE​+E+DD​rD​(1−τC​), strictly less than the unlevered rU​ when there is debt, because debt’s tax shield reduces the firm’s effective cost of capital. Critical point flagged in Ch. 18: the WACC formula assumes the firm rebalances debt to a constant target debt-to-value ratio; if debt level is fixed instead (perpetual fixed face), use APV instead (Ch. 18).

Chapter 16 — Financial Distress, Managerial Incentives, and Information. The frictions that bound debt away from 100%.

  • Direct costs of bankruptcy (legal/administrative, ~3–4% of pre-distress firm value) and indirect costs (lost customers, suppliers, employees; fire-sale asset-liquidation discounts; managerial distraction).
  • Tradeoff theory of capital structure: the optimal D/V balances the marginal tax shield against the marginal expected-distress cost: VL​=VU​+τC​D−PV(distress costs).
  • Agency costs of debt. Asset substitution / risk-shifting (high-leverage equity-holders prefer riskier projects because of their convex payoff); debt overhang (Myers 1977 — equity-holders reject positive-NPV projects whose value largely accrues to debt-holders); cashing-out / milking (extracting value from a near-bankrupt firm).
  • Agency costs of equity. Free-cash-flow problem (Jensen 1986) — managers of low-leverage firms with abundant FCF over-invest; debt’s commitment to cash payouts is a discipline device.
  • Information / signaling. Pecking order (Myers-Majluf 1984): firms prefer internal funds → debt → equity, because adverse selection makes equity issuance look like bad news. Capital-structure signaling: leverage announcements reveal management’s belief about firm prospects.
  • Synthesis. The optimal capital structure is firm-specific and depends on industry-level distress technology, tangibility of assets, growth prospects, profitability, and tax-shield value.

Chapter 17 — Payout Policy. The dividend-irrelevance corollary.

  • MM payout-irrelevance (in perfect markets). With no taxes, transaction costs, or information differences, the firm’s choice between paying dividends, repurchasing shares, or retaining cash for future payout cannot change firm value. Investors who want different cash-flow timing can rebalance personally.
  • Tax preference. Dividends are taxed at ordinary income (or preferential dividend rate); capital gains are tax-deferred and may receive preferential rates → tax preference for repurchases over dividends in U.S. tax regimes for most of the 20th century.
  • Clientele effects. Investors with different tax circumstances self-sort into stocks with payout policies that suit them; the marginal investor’s tax rate determines the equilibrium payout-policy effect.
  • Signaling and stickiness of dividends. Empirically, managers smooth dividends (Lintner 1956) and treat dividend cuts as last-resort signals; dividend-payment-history is a costly signal of stable cash flow.
  • Repurchases. Three forms: open-market, fixed-price tender offer, Dutch auction; all three implement payout but differ in signalling and tax timing.

Why this Part matters. This is the heart of corporate finance — how firms should finance themselves. The MM theorems establish the frictionless benchmark; the next three chapters identify the four families of frictions (taxes, distress costs, agency costs, information asymmetry) that drive observed capital structures away from that benchmark. The reader who internalises this Part can reason from first principles about LBO capital structure (high tax shield + disciplined FCF → high-leverage optimum), startup capital structure (high distress cost + intangible assets → equity-dominated), and bank capital structure (regulatory minimum + safety-net subsidy → distorted equilibrium).
