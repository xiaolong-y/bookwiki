Ch.1 — Introduction — The four-layered paradox

post-war medicine has been astonishingly successful — antibiotics, transplants, hip replacements, IVF, chemotherapy curing childhood leukaemia — yet four discontents have grown alongside that success.

Ch.3 — DM2 1949: Cortisone — empiricism’s second pillar

If antibiotics defeat external enemies (bacteria), cortisone mobilises the body’s own healing machinery.

Ch.4 — DM3 1950: Streptomycin, Smoking, and Sir Austin Bradford Hill

Two events of 1950 jointly anchor a paradigm shift: (a) the streptomycin + PAS combination achieving “marked improvement” in 80% of TB patients, and (b) Doll and Bradford Hill’s case-control study proving smoking causes lung cancer.

Ch.6 — DM6 1955: Open-heart surgery — technology lit by the war

The “last frontier.” Until the 1950s the heart was technically off-limits — operations had to be done within the four-minute oxygen budget after circulatory arrest, ruling out repair of complex congenital defects or valves.

Ch.7 — DM7 1961: Charnley’s hip replacement — the squeak that started it

John Charnley, a Manchester orthopaedic surgeon, redesigned the artificial hip after a chance interaction with a patient whose Judet prosthesis squeaked so loudly his wife avoided being in the same room with him.

Ch.8 — DM8 1963: Kidney transplantation — the immunology accident

Surgical transplantation was anatomically simple — vascular anastomosis was a known technique.

Ch.9 — DM9 1964: Prevention of strokes — the one true preventive triumph

Le Fanu’s Rise pantheon includes one preventive intervention: lowering blood pressure to prevent stroke.

Ch.12 — DM12 1984: Helicobacter — the bacterium nobody saw
