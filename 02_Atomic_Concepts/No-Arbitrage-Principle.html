No-Arbitrage Principle

Definition

The no-arbitrage principle asserts that there exists no self-financing portfolio Π with: zero cost at t (Πt​=0); non-negative value at T in every state of the world a.s. (ΠT​≥0); and strictly positive value at T in at least one state with positive probability (P(ΠT​>0)>0). Equivalently — in the Law of One Price form used by Jonathan Berk and Peter DeMarzo - Corporate Finance — two portfolios that have identical cashflows in every state must trade at the same price in a competitive market. The two formulations are equivalent: each is provable from the other via construction of an arbitrage portfolio.

The principle’s operational content is the monotonicity theorem of Blyth (2014, ch. 6): if AT​≥BT​ a.s., then At​≥Bt​ for t≤T. With equality: if AT​=BT​ a.s., then At​=Bt​ — the replication corollary, which justifies pricing any derivative as the cost of any portfolio that exactly replicates its payoff.

Why it matters

No-arbitrage is the foundational axiom of modern derivatives pricing. Blyth’s analogy: it is to quantitative finance what the axiom of choice is to set theory — without it, ordering and bounds on derivative prices become impossible; with it, an enormous structure of replication-based pricing follows as theorem. Specifically, no-arbitrage delivers:

  • Forward / FRA / swap pricing without distributional assumptions about the underlying (Blyth chs. 2–4) — the conceptual foundation of the textbook’s anti-prediction message: “derivative pricing is largely unrelated to forecasting.”
  • Model-independent option bounds — put-call parity C−P=(F−K)Z, monotonicity in strike, convexity in strike, Lipschitz continuity, American = European call on no-dividend stock (Blyth ch. 7).
  • Risk-neutral probability — on a binomial tree, no-arbitrage ⇔ unique p∗=(r−d)/(u−d)∈(0,1) under which discounted expected payoff = replication price (Blyth ch. 8).
  • Fundamental theorem of asset pricing — no-arbitrage ⇔ ratios of asset prices to a numeraire are martingales under an equivalent measure (Blyth ch. 9; Harrison-Kreps 1979 in continuous time).
  • Bermudan / Bermudan-cancellable / cap-floor arbitrage bounds — model-independent inequalities derived purely from monotonicity (Blyth ch. 13).

Sources

  • Stephen Blyth - An Introduction to Quantitative Finance — Ch. 6 axiomatises and proves the monotonicity theorem; chs. 2–9 use it everywhere; cites Taliaferro and Blyth (2011) for the empirical 2008–2009 violation.
  • Jonathan Berk and Peter DeMarzo - Corporate Finance — Ch. 3 (Financial Decision Making and the Law of One Price): the textbook’s central organising axiom, used throughout Chs. 6–22 to derive NPV, bond pricing, MM, WACC, put-call parity, binomial / Black-Scholes pricing.
  • Roger Lowenstein - When Genius Failed — LTCM 1998: empirical case in which arbitrage appeared available (e.g., on-the-run / off-the-run Treasury spread, Russian GKO arbitrage, swap-spread arbitrage) but persisted longer than the Sharpe-ratio-maximising fund could finance, producing a liquidity-spiral collapse. Limits-to-arbitrage interpretation.

Conceptual tensions

  • Empirical violations under stress. Blyth Ch. 6’s two-Treasury yield divergence (Aug 2008–early 2009), Ch. 2’s October-2008 EUR-USD-forward arbitrage, Ch. 13’s 2009 Bermudan-floor bound violation, and the LTCM 1998 collapse are explicit cases where no-arbitrage failed to hold — sometimes for months, sometimes long enough to bankrupt sophisticated arbitrage funds. The principle is a useful baseline that breaks at the tails.
  • Limits-to-arbitrage literature. Shleifer-Vishny (1997), Gromb-Vayanos (2002), Brunnermeier-Pedersen (2009) systematise why arbitrage fails under stress: capital constraints on arbitrageurs, funding-liquidity / market-liquidity spirals, mark-to-market accounting amplifying small price moves. Blyth flags violations as anecdotes; modern theory makes them predictable.
  • Reflexivity (Soros). No-arbitrage assumes the underlying’s stochastic process is exogenous to derivative trading. Soros argues this is false: portfolio-insurance-induced delta hedging caused the 1987 crash; CDS-driven hedging accelerated the 2008 mortgage-bond collapse; derivative trading systematically affects fundamentals.
  • No-arbitrage vs market efficiency. The two are related but distinct: no-arbitrage is a statement about prices of related assets at the same time; market efficiency is a statement about expected returns across time. Blyth’s framing is no-arbitrage-as-axiom; Berk-DeMarzo’s framing is the same axiom but interpreted through the EMH equilibrium lens (Berk-DeMarzo Ch. 13).

Related concepts

  • Replicating Portfolio — the constructive technique that implements no-arbitrage pricing
  • Risk-Neutral Measure — the probability measure whose existence is equivalent to no-arbitrage (fundamental theorem)
  • Black-Scholes Formula — the canonical no-arbitrage-derived option price formula
  • Net Present Value (Formal) — NPV is itself a corollary of the no-arbitrage / Law-of-One-Price axiom (Berk-DeMarzo Ch. 3)
  • Risk Arbitrage Liquidity Spiral — the systematic failure mode of no-arbitrage under funding-liquidity stress
  • Efficient Market Hypothesis (Formal) — related but distinct: EMH is about expected returns, no-arbitrage is about same-time price relations

Research uses

For Yang’s research portfolio: no-arbitrage is the foundational axiom of any derivatives, structured-products, or quantitative-finance work. In blockchain-economics research, deviations from no-arbitrage in crypto markets (CEX-DEX price differentials, perpetual-futures funding-rate biases, stablecoin-redemption-arbitrage frictions) are themselves research findings — the magnitude and persistence of arbitrage failures measures market integration and capital-mobility constraints. In China political-economy research, the H-share / A-share / ADR pricing of dual-listed stocks shows persistent no-arbitrage violations driven by capital controls, with magnitudes that can be modelled as functions of regulatory tightness. In central-bank monetary-policy analysis, Blyth’s Ch. 6 stress-period violations are the conceptual frame for understanding why the Fed had to intervene in Treasury markets in March 2020 (to restore basis trades that had blown out). In quant-finance interview prep, no-arbitrage is the organising principle; any candidate who cannot articulate what arbitrage is, why it should not exist, and what happens when it does fails the interview.
