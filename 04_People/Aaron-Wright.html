Aaron Wright

Capsule

Aaron Wright is a US legal scholar, clinical law professor, and blockchain-legal-engineering practitioner. He is the co-author (with Primavera De Filippi) of Primavera De Filippi and Aaron Wright - Blockchain and the Law (Harvard University Press, 2018), and the co-founder of OpenLaw (now Tribute Labs), the smart-contract-and-natural-language hybrid contract platform that has been the implementation vehicle for many of the legal-engineering ideas in the book. He is a clinical professor of law at the Benjamin N. Cardozo School of Law (Yeshiva University), where he previously directed the Cardozo Tech Startup Clinic — the clinical program that gave Cardozo students hands-on exposure to the legal questions startups face. He is a co-founder (with De Filippi) of the Coalition for Automated Legal Applications (COALA), the practitioner-academic working group that drafts model legal infrastructure for DAOs. Why they matter

Where De Filippi’s positionality is European-legal-academic-plus-art-research, Wright’s is US-legal-clinical-plus-startup-practice. The combination across the two co-authors is what gives the book its unusual cross-jurisdictional and cross-register reach. Wright matters specifically for three reasons:

First, he is one of the few legal academics with operational depth in actually building the legal-engineering infrastructure the book analyzes. OpenLaw’s hybrid model — natural-language legal contracts paired with smart-contract executable clauses — is the implementation of the Smart Contract Doctrine argument in ch. 4 (and of the Ricardian Contract concept Werbach and others discuss). Reading the book without engaging OpenLaw’s actual implementation misses that the doctrinal claims are not abstract; they are claims someone has tried to build.

Second, he is the clinical-law-school anchor in the blockchain-legal-academic field. Where most legal-academic blockchain treatment is doctrinal-analytical (what should the law be?), Wright’s clinical-law training and Cardozo Tech Startup Clinic experience produce a more transactional-practical sensibility (what does the actual lawyer working with the actual startup actually need?). The book’s chs. 4-9 (smart contracts, securities, organizations, DAOs) reflect this orientation; the chapters are more attentive to practitioner-facing questions (formation, performance, dispute-resolution, liability) than a pure-doctrinal treatment would be.

Third, he is a DAO-legislation interlocutor with state legislatures. The Vermont BBLLC (2018), the Wyoming DAO LLC (2021), and the COALA Model Law are all part of an ecosystem Wright has been actively participating in. The book’s call for legislative recognition of DOs/DAOs as legal entities (ch. 8 p. 142) is not just an academic argument but a policy program with which Wright has been directly engaged. For Yang’s regulatory-architecture interests, Wright is one of the cleanest examples of an academic-practitioner whose ideas have been translated into actual statutory text.

Style and method

Wright’s voice — visible especially in the chapters most directly his (the contracts and organizations material, chs. 4, 8) — is practitioner-attentive, doctrinally careful, and analytically modest. Where some legal academics overstate doctrinal novelty, Wright is consistently careful to ground smart-contract analysis in existing contract doctrine (UCC Article 2, restatement-of-contracts, common-law formation rules) and treat the smart-contract layer as a new kind of performance rather than a wholly novel legal phenomenon. This is the right structural move: smart-contract formation is mostly conventional, but smart-contract performance (ex-ante self-executing rather than ex-post court-enforced) is genuinely new, and that is where the doctrinal innovation needs to happen.

A second methodological signature is transactional-comparative reasoning. The DAO chapter walks through three concrete legal challenges — limited liability, securities laws, non-regulatability — in the same systematic way a transactional lawyer would walk a client through deal structure. The Uber-as-DAO thought experiment (ch. 8 pp. 138-139) is the cleanest accessible illustration: take a familiar gig-economy company, restructure it as a token-and-smart-contract organization, ask what changes from a legal-doctrinal perspective. The thought experiment works because it makes abstract doctrine concrete via a familiar comparator.

A third signature is policy-program orientation. Where De Filippi’s chapters are more analytical-philosophical, Wright’s are more what-should-the-law-do? The book’s calls for legislative recognition (DAO legal entities, smart-contract performance rules, securities-law clarification) are written with knowledge of how state-level legislative processes actually work. The Vermont BBLLC and Wyoming DAO LLC have appeared piecemeal but inconsistently across jurisdictions — Wright’s policy program is intended to produce a more coherent statutory framework, and the unevenness of actual outcomes is partly the legislative-process limitation rather than the analytical limitation.

Best for

  • Practitioners working on smart-contract drafting, DAO formation, or crypto-startup compliance. Wright’s clinical orientation makes the book unusually useful for transactional questions; ch. 4’s contract-formation analysis and chs. 8-9’s DAO legal-status analysis are the cleanest practitioner-facing treatments in the 2018-2024 literature.
  • Anyone working on DAO legislation. Wright’s involvement in Vermont BBLLC and Wyoming DAO LLC means the book’s policy program has been partially translated into actual statutory text; reading it gives the analytical scaffolding for evaluating both existing statutes and proposed reforms.
  • Researchers studying legal-engineering hybrid systems. OpenLaw’s natural-language-plus-smart-contract architecture is the operational implementation of the book’s Smart Contract Doctrine argument; engaging both the book and the platform is the right way to evaluate the thesis empirically.

Less useful for

  • Pure doctrinal legal theory. Wright is more transactional than doctrinal; for deep doctrinal-philosophical analysis (Hart, Dworkin, Raz, the rule-of-law debates), his contribution is sketchy. Pair with rule-of-law theorists for that register.
  • Cypherpunk-original-position analysis. The book takes the cypherpunk genealogy seriously but does not deeply engage with the philosophical foundations (Hayek’s spontaneous-order theory, Rothbard’s anarcho-capitalism, the David Friedman-vs-Murray Rothbard debates). For that, pair with libertarian-political-theory sources.
  • Post-2022 DAO governance disputes. The book’s DAO-legal-status analysis is foundational but pre-dates the wave of DAO governance disputes (Solend’s emergency proposal 2022, the Convex / Curve wars 2021-2022, the OlympusDAO collapse). Frame remains accurate; empirical examples need supplementation.

How he changed my reading practice

Three durable lessons:

  1. Practitioner-attentiveness is a methodological discipline. Asking “what does the actual lawyer working with the actual startup actually need?” filters out a lot of doctrinal speculation that has no operational consequences. This carries cleanly to AI policy, platform regulation, and academic governance — the question is not “what should the rule be in principle?” but “what is the rule that practitioners can actually use?”
  2. Doctrinal continuity beats doctrinal novelty. Wright consistently grounds smart-contract analysis in existing contract law rather than declaring it sui generis. This is the right structural move because it forces the analysis to identify specifically what is new (performance), as distinct from what is borrowed (formation, interpretation, dispute-resolution).
  3. Policy programs and statutory text are empirical material, not just normative claims. Reading Vermont BBLLC and Wyoming DAO LLC against the book’s policy program is the right way to test whether the analytical framework actually translates to legislative outcomes. Several of the book’s recommendations have been picked up; several have been ignored. Tracking which-and-why is methodologically more productive than evaluating the framework on its own terms.

Relation to Yang’s research

For Yang’s blockchain-regulation research, Wright is the practitioner-clinical anchor in the De Filippi-Wright pairing. His contributions to the contracts and organizations chapters (chs. 4, 8) are the most directly useful for any specific transactional question — DAO formation, smart-contract drafting, securities-law application. Pair with CZ - Freedom of Money for the realized practitioner-side experience and with Kevin Werbach - The Blockchain and the New Architecture of Trust for the parallel academic frame.

For Yang’s regulatory-architecture interests, Wright’s policy-program orientation is methodologically instructive. The translation from analytical framework to statutory text (Vermont BBLLC, Wyoming DAO LLC) is a clean example of how academic legal-policy work can have actual legislative consequences. The unevenness of outcomes (some recommendations adopted, some not, with substantial cross-state variation) is a useful caution against assuming that good analysis automatically produces good legislation.

For Yang’s startup-and-VC interests intersecting with Sebastian Mallaby - The Power Law and the Marc Andreessen - The pmarca Blog Archives, Wright’s clinical-law-school perspective gives a less ideologically loaded view of the legal-engineering frontier than the VC-side advocacy literature. Reading Wright alongside Andreessen is a useful exercise in seeing how the same technological substrate looks from a clinical-academic versus venture-capital vantage.
