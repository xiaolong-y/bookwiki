Where Is My Flying Car? A Memoir of Future Past

Where Is My Flying Car? A Memoir of Future Past

J. Storrs Hall - Where Is My Flying Car

One-sentence thesis

The mid-century default future of rapidly improving physical technology — flying cars, fission-fuelled abundance, expanding aerospace, nanotech-enabled manufacturing, lunar industry — did not arrive because around 1970 the United States quietly switched off the Henry Adams Curve of 7%-a-year usable-energy growth and substituted a regulatory, liability-driven, status-anxious risk-suppression regime that taxed every iteration loop in atoms while leaving bits cheap; the absence of flying cars is therefore not a failure of physics, of demand, or of nerd imagination but a measurable policy artefact whose costs (Hall, citing Dawson and Seater, ch. 7) are on the order of 3–4× household income compounded over fifty years.

Why this book matters

This is the most empirically dense statement of the physical-technology stagnation thesis written by an engineer rather than an economist. Tyler Cowen and Robert Gordon argue stagnation in aggregate productivity terms; Peter Thiel argues it in venture-capital memos; Hall (a nanotechnology researcher with hands in molecular-machine design and a pilot’s licence) argues it at the level of specific machines that did not get built — Moulton Taylor’s Aerocar, Robert Fulton’s Airphibian, the Ford Nucleon, Drexler-Feynman atomically-precise manufacturing, Cessna’s piston-aircraft line — and traces each non-arrival to a specific calendar-time iteration tax. That makes the book unusually falsifiable for the genre: every one of his case studies is checkable against archival flight logs, FAA dockets, NRC permit timelines, and patent histories.

For a reader who is simultaneously following Sebastian Mallaby - The Power Law (why software-iteration loops scale beautifully under VC), Chris Miller - Chip War (how one atoms-domain — semiconductors — kept moving on a 50-year curve), and Nick Bostrom - Deep Utopia (what happens if we ever do break through to abundance), Hall is the missing diagnostic chapter: he documents the specific institutions (FDA, FAA, NRC, EPA, Department of Energy, product-liability tort regime, Chevron-deference administrative state) whose growth phase from 1970 to 2018 maps almost exactly onto the great-stagnation flatlines, and he gives the causal mechanism (Bayesian regulator incentive structure, Machiavelli effect on funded science, Peltzman risk-compensation, cost-disease in heavily-regulated sectors) by which absence of new bureaucracies is not the same as their counterfactual presence.

The book also matters because it directly funds the opposite prior of Bryan Caplan - The Myth of the Rational Voter: where Caplan argues that voter cognitive failure produces bad regulation, Hall argues that agency cognitive failure plus tort-litigation tail risk plus credentialed-expert turf protection produces it — voters are largely passive throughout. The two diagnoses point at different reform levers (jury reform and FDA scope-narrowing for Hall; political-economy redesign for Caplan), and reading them together sharpens the policy question of where the actual veto point sits.

Finally, Hall’s book is a methodological warning: it is one of the cleanest exhibits in the vault of an engineer’s-eye counterfactual style — he projects what the Aerocar industry would have become in 1975 had it followed the 1900–1950 automotive-adoption curve, and he uses that counterfactual as evidence. Reading him with Karl Popper - The Logic of Scientific Discovery in mind clarifies the epistemics: Hall’s claims are bold and falsifiable in Popper’s sense, not safe and unfalsifiable, and the reader’s job is to catalogue which of his counterfactuals an honest critic could in principle disprove.

Core argument

Hall’s argument runs through ten interlocking pieces, each anchored to a specific chapter band:

  1. The Henry Adams Curve is the unit of stagnation accounting. Hall borrows from Henry Adams’s autobiography the observation that usable energy per capita doubled roughly every decade from 1840 to 1900. Decomposed, the curve runs ≈3% population growth, ≈2% energy-efficiency growth, ≈2% per-capita energy-consumption growth, summing to ≈7%. From 1700 to 1970 the United States stayed on this curve. After 1970, per-capita energy consumption flatlined (ch. 3, Stagnation Point) while bits-domain technologies kept compounding. This single chart reframes “the Great Stagnation” as a physical-domain phenomenon, not a generalised civilisational malaise.

  2. The 1970–1980 inflection is over-determined but specific. Nixon’s price controls (1971), the OPEC embargo (1973), the establishment of the EPA (1970), OSHA (1970), the Consumer Product Safety Commission (1972), and the Department of Energy (1977) all cluster in one decade. Hall cites the Manufacturers Alliance for Productivity and Innovation report tracking cumulative federal regulations on manufacturers — an exponential curve with an inflection precisely in this window — and the Federal Register page count, which grew 121% under Nixon alone (ch. 7, Regulation). The energy flatline and the regulation explosion are not co-incidental.

  3. Cost disease is the macro signature of micro regulation. Health care 6×, primary education 3×, college tuition ≈10×, New York subway construction 600× per mile (ch. 3). These price increases are real, not inflation — they are what regulation costs the citizen at the till. Hall cites Dawson and Seater’s Journal of Economic Growth paper estimating that had the US held 1949 regulatory levels constant, median household income would be roughly 185,000insteadof53,000 — a counterfactual about 3.5× current. Even discounting this estimate by half preserves the qualitative claim. This is Hall’s most quantitatively grounded blow.

  4. The Machiavelli effect explains why scientific funding bureaucracies suppress disruptive sub-fields. Drawing the title from The Prince, ch. 6 (“there is nothing more difficult to take in hand … than to take the lead in the introduction of a new order of things”) and pairing it with Kuhn on normal science, Hall argues (ch. 5, The Machiavelli Effect) that the National Nanotechnology Initiative (Clinton, 2000, $500M) ended up not funding atomically-precise manufacturing of the Drexler-Feynman type but instead relabelling existing surface-and-materials science as “nanotechnology” while incumbent recipients (Smalley most prominently) attacked Drexler’s vision as infeasible. This is not a conspiracy: it is the predictable Bayesian behaviour of recipients of a fixed-pie funding allocation. Hall generalises the pattern across nutrition science (the Yudkin/Atkins suppression), behaviourist psychology (Miller’s testimony), and AI funding cycles. Falsifiability applies in reverse — incumbents successfully classify rival programs as unfalsifiable in order to deny them funding.

  5. The cold-fusion case is the textbook pathological failure mode of the same dynamic. Ch. 6 narrates the 1989 Pons-Fleischmann debacle: a real but poorly-understood low-energy nuclear reaction phenomenon (LENR) that the orthodox-physics establishment used as a cudgel to suppress all exotic-energy research, including phenomena that had survived independent replication. The lesson is not that cold fusion was real — Hall is careful — but that the cost of being wrong about it (career annihilation) was orders of magnitude higher than the cost of ignoring it, which is exactly the Machiavelli equilibrium.

  6. Forbidden-fruit chapters generalise the diagnosis to atoms-versus-bits in toto. The juxtaposition (ch. 7, opening epigraph by Thiel: “bits were unregulated and atoms were regulated. Starting a software company costs $100,000; getting a drug through the FDA costs about a billion”) reorganises the entire stagnation conversation around a binary cost asymmetry. The asymmetry is not natural law: it is the cumulative result of FDA-AS-1938, post-1962 drug-amendments, the Atomic Energy Act, FAA Part 23 certification, OSHA promulgation, Chevron deference (1984), and the post-1975 product-liability tort explosion — all of which apply to atoms-iteration loops and almost none of which apply to bits-iteration loops.

  7. Atomic-age fission was the keystone counterfactual that the regulatory regime explicitly killed. Hall (ch. 12, The Atomic Age) walks through the post-1971 collapse of US nuclear-plant construction permits: the curve looks like a mirror of private-piston-aircraft sales, both crashing in the late 1970s. The Department of Energy and NRC are fingered as proximate causes; the deep cause is the same liability-and-precaution culture. Asimov’s 1964 prediction that fission would supply “well over half the power needs of humanity” by 2014 was not an engineering miscalculation; it was a political-economy miscalculation. Hall reinforces this with ch. 13 (The Great Physics Stagnation): nuclear physics PhD output collapsed and key textbooks have not been updated since the 1960s. The discipline was organisationally starved, not exhausted.

  8. The Aerocar–Airphibian–Convair counterfactual is the book’s emotional core. Ch. 7 (Forbidden Fruit) reconstructs the engineering history of the four near-successful flying-car projects of the postwar period (Taylor Aerocar 1949; Fulton Airphibian 1946; ConvAirCar 1947; Hallock Road-A-Plane). Each was technically operable. Each foundered on FAA certification delays, product-liability exposure (Cessna explicitly cited liability when it ended piston production in 1986), and what Hall calls bureaucratic friction. He projects an alternate 1975 in which 20% of US households own a flying car — the same fraction as automobile penetration in 1925 — and computes that the resulting flying-car economy would have been roughly the size of the interstate-highway-induced economic boom of the 1950s, plausibly enough to have prevented the Great Stagnation outright.

  9. The Eloi diagnosis: cultural risk-aversion is downstream of bureaucratic risk-aversion. Ch. 8 (The Age of Aquarius) argues that the same generation that produced the regulatory explosion also produced a media and academic class culturally hostile to industrial confidence — Hall borrows H. G. Wells’s Eloi as a label for the resulting “soft” consumer of safety-state outputs. This part of the book is more cultural-criticism than empirical, but it links coherently to Reflexivity: pessimistic expectations alter investment and policy, which then produce flat reality, which justifies the original pessimism.

  10. Yet the same engineers were drawing what should have been built — and the designs work. Part III chapters (esp. ch. 16 Aeronautical Innovations, ch. 18 Metropolis, ch. 21 The Second Atomic Age, ch. 23 Engineers’ Dreams) walk through technically credible designs for ducted-fan VTOL, helical autopilot networks, atomic batteries, weather-control infrastructure, and a Lofstrom-style space pier that would put cargo into orbit at ≈10$/kg. Hall is not doing speculative fiction here — every design has a calculable physics envelope and a known precursor prototype. The argument is not that any specific machine will be built but that the continuous frontier of physically-realizable engineering has been culturally written off, which is the deeper civilisational defect.

The closing-region implication (ch. 24, Rocket to the Renaissance) is political-philosophical: if default futures are abandoned through choice architectures, then “lack of flying cars” is not a joke about inventors but a datum about civilisation’s risk-return frontier — and the frontier is negotiable, not given.

Each downward arrow is a chapter-level argumentative move. The central wedge — atoms regulated, bits unregulated — is the structural claim that organises everything else. Key concepts

  • Henry Adams Curve — the ≈7%/yr long-run trend in usable energy per capita from c. 1700–1970, drawn from Henry Adams’s autobiographical observation; Hall’s central diagnostic chart.
  • Great Stagnation (physical-domain emphasis) — Cowen/Thiel’s slowdown thesis specialised to atoms-iteration loops while bits keep compounding.
  • Falsifiability — Hall’s critique of the Smalley/NNI attack on Drexler hinges on the orthodoxy classifying atomically-precise manufacturing as unfalsifiable in order to deny funding; Popper’s demarcation criterion is the right tool to recover the symmetry.
  • Machiavelli Effect (Hall’s coinage) — the Bayesian-predictable behaviour of incumbent funded researchers when a disruptive sub-field threatens their funding allocation; named for The Prince (ch. 5).
  • Atoms-vs-bits regulatory asymmetry — Thiel’s binary observation operationalised as a 10,000× cost-of-iteration ratio between software companies and physical-product companies (ch. 7).
  • Cost disease (regulatory-explanation variant) — the secular real-price increases in heavily-regulated sectors (medicine, education, infrastructure, certification-bound aircraft) interpreted as iteration-loop tax, not as Baumol’s labour-productivity story.
  • Peltzman effect / risk compensation — the empirical observation that mandatory safety equipment elicits offsetting riskier behaviour; Hall uses it to argue benefits-of-regulation calculations are systematically overstated.
  • Reflexivity — Soros-Taleb feedback loop in which pessimistic expectations about technology alter investment and policy, producing flat outcomes that justify the pessimism.
  • Antifragility — Hall’s case for why iteration loops should be the unit of analysis: technologies that benefit from many cheap failures fragility under regulatory regimes that punish failures asymmetrically.
  • Skin in the Game — the principal-agent failure of regulators who bear no downside cost from over-regulation but enormous career risk from any approved-product disaster; Taleb-flavoured re-statement of Hall’s diagnosis.
  • Black Swan — every major regulatory expansion (FDA-1938 after Elixir Sulfanilamide; FAA tightening after Knute Rockne’s 1931 crash; product-liability after early-1970s media events) was a black-swan response, not a planned policy; the asymmetry is structural.
  • Reflexivity — Hall is implicitly Sorosian about regulation: regulator and regulated form a feedback loop in which expectations of strictness produce strictness, then justify themselves.

Key arguments created from this book

  • The Great Stagnation is structurally a regulation-induced atoms-iteration tax — Hall’s central empirical-causal claim.
  • Default futures are policy artefacts, not engineering achievements — Hall’s normative-philosophical implication.
  • Scientific funding bureaucracies systematically suppress disruptive sub-fields via the Machiavelli Effect — Hall’s contribution to the philosophy-of-science literature.

Best chapters/pages to actually read

Page numbers are unreliable in the EPUB extraction; cite by chapter title.

  • Ch. 3, The Graveyard of Dreams (esp. Stagnation Point section) — the Henry Adams Curve chart and the per-capita energy flatline. This single chapter is the empirical core of the book. Read first; without it, later chapters feel like polemic. Allocate 1 hour and re-derive the 7% decomposition (3% population + 2% efficiency + 2% per-capita) from Hall’s numbers.
  • Ch. 5, The Machiavelli Effect — the science-funding-pathology mechanism. The cleanest single statement of why incumbents block disruptive sub-fields. Read alongside Thomas S. Kuhn - The Structure of Scientific Revolutions for the normal-science framing.
  • Ch. 7, Forbidden Fruit — the Bureaucracy and Regulation sections. Hall’s quantitative case (Dawson-Seater, Djankov-McLiesh-Ramalho, MAPI, Federal Register page counts) and the Aerocar/Airphibian/ConvAirCar/Hallock Road-A-Plane case studies. This is the most policy-actionable chapter: extract specific FAA Part 23, OSHA, Chevron-deference, and product-liability levers and write a one-page memo on which would matter most if reformed.
  • Ch. 12, The Atomic Age — the fission counterfactual and the post-1971 nuclear-permit collapse. Important not because every reader cares about reactors but because fission is the cleanest case of a politically-suppressed atoms-domain technology. Hall’s energy-density numbers (Mt-of-coal-equivalent per kg of U-235) are correct; readers should verify them once and then trust Hall on subsequent magnitudes.
  • Ch. 6, Cold Fusion? — the LENR / Pons-Fleischmann case. Read it as an exhibit in how scientific orthodoxy responds to ambiguous heterodoxy, not as a position paper on cold fusion itself. Pair with Karl Popper - The Logic of Scientific Discovery on demarcation.
  • Ch. 21, The Second Atomic Age and ch. 23, Engineers’ Dreams (Space Pier section) — the forward-looking engineering chapters. Read these to recalibrate how much of the “post-Apollo civilisational ceiling” feeling is a function of what is on engineers’ drawing boards versus what regulators permit.
  • Skim — ch. 8 (Age of Aquarius) is more cultural-criticism than empirical; useful only for the Maslow-hierarchy framing. Ch. 16-19 are technically rich but redundant if you already accept the central thesis. Appendices C, D, E, F (regulatory tables, radiation, energy intensities, flying-car catalogue) are reference material, not narrative.

Important examples / cases

  • The Aerocar / Airphibian / ConvAirCar / Road-A-Plane (ch. 2, 7) — four functional postwar flying-car prototypes, all killed by certification cost, product liability, or both. Bruce Hallock was literally arrested under the Neutrality Act for failing to file paperwork on a private aircraft sale (jury found him not guilty; he was bankrupt anyway). This is the empirical anchor for “bureaucratic friction is calendar-time-tax”.
  • Cessna’s 1986 piston-line shutdown — Russ Meyer (CEO) explicitly cited product liability as cause and said production would resume only if the liability environment improved. Hall reads this as the canonical exhibit of post-1970 tort regime as iteration-loop tax.
  • The Concorde economic envelope (ch. 3) — supersonic travel is 3× the cost just above Mach 1 versus just below it; this explains the Mach-1 plateau in commercial aviation without needing to invoke regulation. Hall uses this as a control case: where the constraint is genuine physics-economics, the plateau is honest. Where the constraint is regulation, the plateau is policy. The contrast disciplines the rest of the book.
  • The 1989 Pons-Fleischmann cold-fusion press conference (ch. 6) — University of Utah administration pressured premature publication; subsequent witch-hunt suppressed all exotic-energy research, including phenomena unrelated to F&P that survived independent replication. The cost asymmetry (career annihilation if you’re wrong; modest career gain if you’re right) is the Machiavelli equilibrium in action.
  • The 1962 Kefauver-Harris drug amendments (ch. 7) — Hall cites the classic Peltzman/Friedman analysis showing the amendments roughly halved new-drug introduction without measurably changing drug quality. This is the most quantitatively grounded specific-regulation example in the book.
  • The Airline Deregulation Act of 1978 (ch. 7) — used as Hall’s positive-control case: the rare deregulatory event whose effects are measurable. Real fares fell 30% by 1990; accident rates fell 20-45%; passenger miles roughly 5×. Hall’s argument is that the converse operation (re-regulating) costs equivalently in reverse.
  • The 1989 Calvin & Hobbes “You call this the future?” strip — Hall opens ch. 3 with this Watterson cartoon as cultural anchor; Thiel’s 2011 “we wanted flying cars, instead we got 140 characters” line is the same observation 22 years later. Both are cited deliberately as evidence that the cultural awareness of the gap is itself old, persistent, and bipartisan.
  • The 2008 Berkeley plutonium-speck rediscovery — Phil Broughton found Glenn Seaborg’s 2.7 µg first-plutonium-ever sample in a “hot landfill” disposal bucket; once recognised, the NRC required bales of paperwork to retain it. Hall uses this as a small-case illustration of how the same regulatory regime that prevented its preservation now imposes huge costs on its retention — a closed loop.

Critiques and limitations

  • The cultural-criticism chapters (esp. ch. 8) are weaker than the empirical ones. Hall’s Eloi framing is rhetorically vivid but does not survive the same falsifiability test as his energy-flatline chart. A reader should weight chapters 3, 5, 6, 7, 12 heavily and chapters 8, 14 lightly.
  • The atoms-vs-bits asymmetry is also a function of the underlying physics of digital information, not solely of regulation. Chris Miller - Chip War documents that semiconductors are an atoms domain that nonetheless kept moving on a 50-year exponential — but only because the unit cost of iteration was driven down by deep-UV lithography and not because semiconductors are unregulated (they are heavily regulated, in fact). Hall under-discusses this counter-example, which weakens the binary frame.
  • Counterfactual estimates assume static demand elasticities. The Dawson-Seater 185k−vs−53k counterfactual implicitly assumes that household preferences and labour supply would have absorbed the additional output the way they did pre-1949. This is a strong assumption. The real counterfactual is probably between the two figures, not at either pole. Hall does not offer sensitivity analysis.
  • The Machiavelli effect framework is hard to falsify. Any time an incumbent objects to a new sub-field, Hall can call it Machiavelli; any time the incumbent welcomes the sub-field, Hall is silent. A more disciplined version of the framework would predict which sub-fields will be suppressed and which welcomed, conditional on funding-pie geometry. Hall gestures at this (ex-ante “zero-sum” funding pies are the suppressing condition) but does not formalise.
  • Hall treats regulator behaviour as primarily endogenous to bureaucratic incentives. Bryan Caplan - The Myth of the Rational Voter would argue that voter cognitive failures are upstream of regulator behaviour: regulators do what voters demand. Hall mostly ignores the demand side. If Caplan is right, Hall’s reform proposals (FDA scope-narrowing, jury reform, Chevron-deference repeal) target the wrong veto point.
  • The book pre-dates COVID-19 and the post-2022 AI moment. Both events partially vindicate Hall (Operation Warp Speed showed FDA can compress timelines under political pressure; AI shows that the bits-side has continued to compound) and partially complicate him (the regulatory state did not break in either direction during COVID; the AI safety conversation looks to most observers more like Hall’s Eloi than his engineers). A 2025 revision would have to engage both.
  • Pagination is unreliable in the EPUB. The note above cites chapters only; the source file is ingested as 117 raw chapter files (TOC fragmentation), and the page_refs_reliable: false frontmatter flag is honest. Readers should anchor follow-up notes to chapter titles.
  • The book is American-centric. Hall’s regulatory mechanism (FAA, FDA, NRC, EPA, OSHA, Chevron-deference) is US-specific; the same flatlining occurred in Western Europe and Japan despite different regulatory architectures. Either the mechanism is more universal than Hall claims, or other mechanisms (oil-shock-induced demographics, secular productivity slowdown) are co-active. Hall does not engage the cross-national variation.

Links to other books

  • Software-iteration-loop complement to Sebastian Mallaby - The Power Law — Mallaby explains why digital VC iteration loops scale (100ksoftwarecompanies,fail−fast,100xoutliers);Hallexplains∗why∗theequivalentatoms−iterationloopsdon′tscale(1B FDA submissions, multi-decade NRC permits, FAA Part 23 certification cycles). Reading them adjacent yields a clean bits-versus-atoms binary that neither book is strong enough to make alone. Mallaby’s VC ecosystem and Hall’s regulated-atoms world are mirror images.
  • Direct atoms-domain counter-exhibit from Chris Miller - Chip War — Miller’s semiconductor industry is the cleanest atoms domain that did keep iterating on a 50-year exponential, despite extensive regulation. The contrast complicates Hall’s binary and is the strongest single argument against him: if semiconductors did it, why didn’t piston aircraft? Hall under-engages; the synthesis is left to the reader.
  • Techno-history cousin to M. Mitchell Waldrop - The Dream Machine — Waldrop documents the postwar bits-side breakthrough (Licklider, ARPA, interactive computing) that Hall partly takes for granted as “the bits-iteration loop that worked.” Reading them together gives the full mid-century technology history: Waldrop on what we got, Hall on what we didn’t.
  • Long-run techno-future contrast with Nick Bostrom - Deep Utopia — Bostrom asks “what happens if we get the productive technologies?”; Hall documents “why we haven’t”. The two together frame a trajectory choice the civilisation faces. Hall is the diagnostic chapter; Bostrom is the prescriptive chapter; both are needed for any serious AI-policy or industrial-policy memo.
  • Political-economy critic of regulatory failure adjacent to Bryan Caplan - The Myth of the Rational Voter — Caplan and Hall agree that US regulatory outputs are systematically worse than the median voter would prefer if rational, and that the bureaucratic-political equilibrium is the binding constraint. They disagree on whether the upstream cause is voter cognition (Caplan) or regulator turf-protection (Hall). Both claims may be true at different timescales.
  • Institutional-failure parallel to Joseph E. Stiglitz - Globalization and Its Discontents Revisited — Stiglitz documents IMF/World Bank technocratic over-reach producing real-world output destruction in low-income economies; Hall documents domestic US technocratic over-reach producing real-world technology suppression. Both insist institutional architecture binds harder than stated intentions. Both would be bemused to be cited together (different priors, different politics) but the structural argument is parallel.
  • Philosophy-of-progress / falsifiability foundation in Karl Popper - The Logic of Scientific Discovery — Hall’s Machiavelli-effect critique reads naturally through Popper: incumbents protect their funding by demoting rival programmes from “falsifiable but unproven” to “unfalsifiable speculation,” denying them the institutional standing to be tested. Reading Hall and Popper together sharpens the philosophical move; Popper provides the demarcation tool that Hall uses but never names.
  • Innovation-economics handle from Daron Acemoglu - Microeconomics — Acemoglu’s standard innovation-economics chapters (R&D externalities, patent races, growth-decomposition exercises) are the formal-economics complement to Hall’s case-study evidence. Reading Hall after Acemoglu disciplines the qualitative claims; reading Acemoglu after Hall makes the textbook treatment feel insufficient — Acemoglu’s standard model has no place for the regulatory iteration-tax Hall documents.
  • Talebian framing throughout Nassim Nicholas Taleb - Incerto — Hall’s Bayesian regulator-incentive analysis, his Peltzman-effect framing, his treatment of FDA-after-Sulfanilamide as a Black Swan response, and his diagnosis of the cold-fusion suppression as a Skin in the Game failure are all implicitly Talebian. Antifragility is the structural concept that names what regulators destroy when they tax iteration loops: the antifragile system that benefits from many cheap failures.
  • Comparative-state mechanism from Angela Huyue Zhang - High Wire — Zhang documents the Chinese state’s cyclical regulatory volatility (lax → crackdown → easing); Hall documents the US state’s monotonic regulatory accumulation. Reading the two together reveals that both failure modes — volatility and ratchet — produce real iteration-loop costs, and that neither the Chinese nor the US regulatory regime has solved the principal-agent problem at the heart of technology governance.
  • Methodological cousin to Gary Goertz and James Mahoney - A Tale of Two Cultures — Hall’s argument is qualitative-set-theoretic in Goertz-Mahoney’s sense: necessary conditions (regulatory scope, liability exposure, funding-pie geometry) for stagnation; case-tracing through specific dockets (Aerocar, NRC permits, NNI) as exhibits. The methodological pattern is identical even though Hall is an engineer, not a political scientist.

Open questions

  • Was the 1970 inflection a single cause or a polycausal cluster? Hall lists Nixon price controls (1971), OPEC (1973), and the regulatory build-out (1970-77) without disentangling them. A cleaner empirical paper would attempt instrumental-variable identification on which sub-cause did how much work.
  • Why did semiconductors evade the iteration-tax pattern? Miller’s Chip War shows the chip industry kept compounding under regulation. Hall’s framework should predict this evasion (small physical footprint, modular product cycles, limited tort exposure on transistors) but he doesn’t formalise the prediction. A second-edition chapter on this would strengthen the book.
  • Is the Machiavelli effect always net-negative? Some incumbent resistance to disruptive sub-fields is correct (cold fusion really is mostly noise; perpetual motion really is impossible). Hall’s framework needs a Bayesian discount rate on incumbent objections — when should we trust them? He does not provide one.
  • What policy levers actually move the bits-vs-atoms wedge? Hall implicitly proposes FDA scope-narrowing, jury-reform on product liability, NRC permit-cycle compression, and Chevron-deference repeal. Each has been proposed independently for decades. The book does not engage why none have been enacted; this is a serious gap.
  • Does AI count as bits or atoms for Hall’s framework? AI training is bits-side (compute scaling, data scaling, software iteration loops); AI deployment in the physical economy is atoms-side (FDA medical-AI clearance, FAA-on-autonomous-flight, NHTSA-on-self-driving). The framework predicts AI will compound on the bits side and stall on the atoms side. The 2024-2026 evidence is consistent with this prediction. A revised edition should formalise it.
  • What is the demand-side counterfactual? If the Aerocar had reached 20% household penetration by 1975, the noise externality, congestion in low-altitude airspace, and accident-frequency-on-untrained-pilots problems would have been real. Hall does not seriously model the negative externalities of his counterfactual; readers should.

Research uses

  • Yang’s blockchain-regulation research: Hall’s atoms-vs-bits regulatory asymmetry is the right master frame for why blockchain regulation has been so inconsistent — blockchain straddles the wedge (the protocol layer is bits; the on-chain financial-instrument layer is atoms-equivalent for regulators) and the result is regulatory whipsaw. The Machiavelli effect predicts that incumbent financial-industry research bureaucracies would behave on blockchain as Smalley behaved on Drexler — relabel existing work as “blockchain” while attacking the disruptive cases. This prediction is well-corroborated by the 2017-2025 record and is publishable as such.
  • AI-policy comparative analysis: Hall + Chris Miller - Chip War + Nick Bostrom - Deep Utopia form a complete reading list for the physical-AI policy question (versus the bits-only foundation-model question). Yang’s policy work needs Hall as the diagnostic chapter on why atoms-domain AI deployment is harder than bits-domain.
  • Methodological influence on stagnation-empirical work: Hall’s case-by-case anchoring (specific FAA dockets, specific NRC permit timelines, specific product-liability lawsuits) is a worked example of engineering-style empiricism on a political-economy question. Yang’s lobbying-network research could borrow the technique: anchor each regulatory-volatility claim to specific dockets, not just aggregate measures.
  • Founder psychology and risk-tolerance work: Hall’s diagnosis (regulators bear no upside, only downside; founders bear all the downside, only some of the upside) maps onto Taleb’s Skin in the Game in a precise quantitative way. Useful for diagnosing why certain founder personality types over- or under-estimate institutional choke points.
  • Reading other people’s stagnation papers: Hall is now a benchmark — papers that argue the slowdown is purely demographic (e.g., Robert Gordon) without engaging the regulatory-iteration-tax mechanism are operating with a partial frame. Useful self-discipline for evaluating Cowen, Gordon, Bloom-et-al, Andreessen, and the broader stagnation literature.
  • Scenario-planning practice: build alternate-history models in which (a) Aerocar reached 20% penetration by 1975, (b) NRC permit cycles capped at 5 years, (c) FDA scope narrowed to safety only (not efficacy), (d) Chevron deference repealed in 1985. Each is a tractable policy counterfactual. Hall provides the parameter ranges; the modelling is open work.
  • Memo for venture-capital due-diligence: when evaluating an atoms-domain startup, use Hall’s checklist: which agencies regulate the iteration loop? what is the calendar-time-per-iteration? what is the product-liability tail? Bits-domain startups skip most of these; atoms-domain startups must price them. This is actionable.

What I would miss if I only read this note

You would miss Hall’s engineering voice. He is a working engineer (nanotechnology research, pilot, computer scientist) and the book reads in places like a technical autopsy — paragraph-level discussions of ducted-fan VTOL aerodynamics, Lofstrom-launch-loop physics, palladium electrode loading kinetics, fission-yield bimodal distributions. The note compresses the technical texture; reading the engineering passages is what differentiates Hall from the median stagnation-essayist (Cowen, Andreessen, Thiel) and is the source of the book’s credibility on the physical-domain claims.

You would miss the counterfactual exercises that occupy chapters 16-23. These are not predictions in the Cowen sense (probability-weighted forecasts) but design-space envelope arguments: for each technology Hall claims is suppressed, he sketches the engineering envelope it would occupy if deregulated and the physical-design constraint that bounds it. The Space Pier and World Weather Control chapters in particular are not speculative fiction — they are physically-grounded calculations that the reader can re-derive. The note flattens them; reading them is the only way to see what physical-engineering optimism looks like done seriously.

You would miss the cumulative weight of the case studies. Hall’s persuasion is partly the Aerocar story, partly the Cessna shutdown, partly the Pons-Fleischmann witch-hunt, partly the Cessna 172 lawsuit history, partly the Knute Rockne aftermath, partly Nader-and-the-Corvair, partly the eAPIS interface humiliation, partly the Berkeley plutonium-speck retrieval — each is small, together they constitute a pattern. The note can list them but cannot accumulate them at the rate the book does. The cumulative rhetorical effect is the source of the book’s emotional force, which is part of why some readers are persuaded even when they reject specific empirical claims. Keep a skeptical highlighter and a generous one.

You would miss Hall’s quotation discipline. He quotes Heinlein, Asimov, Clarke, Wells, Heinlein again, Tolkein, Henry Adams, Coase, Greenspan, Taleb, Thiel, Watterson, Forster, Burroughs, Murray, Kling — and each quotation is load-bearing, not decorative. The book is an unusual hybrid of polemic, engineering essay, and intellectual history; the quotations are the binding agent. Compressing them into a note loses the texture of the intellectual lineage Hall is invoking, which is roughly: the mid-twentieth-century engineering-modernist tradition, hostile to safety-state precaution, optimistic about energy and physical capability, and suspicious of credentialed epistemic authority. That lineage is not the median 2026 reader’s prior; reading Hall is partly an exercise in inhabiting a different prior for a few hundred pages, which is itself the deepest intellectual benefit of the book.

You would miss the honest critic-and-supporter framings Hall maintains. He is not blind to the genuine safety wins of post-1970 regulation (drinking water, automotive seat-belts, ozone-layer protection, OSHA mining-safety rules); he simply argues the price was too high in iteration-loop terms. The note collapses this into a single sentence; the book itself works through it case by case, which is the only way to internalise that the question is trade-off not binary.
