Chip War — The Fight for the World's Most Critical Technology

Chip War — The Fight for the World's Most Critical Technology

Chris Miller - Chip War

One-sentence thesis

The semiconductor industry — built over 75 years from Bell Labs’ 1947 transistor through Fairchild and Intel through TSMC’s foundry model and ASML’s EUV monopoly — is now so geographically and corporately concentrated, and so cumulatively dependent on specific tacit-engineering know-how, that control over advanced chips has become the central pivot of US-China strategic competition; the October 2022 US export controls (and tighter rules since) deliberately weaponize this concentration, and the resulting decoupling will reshape technology, geopolitics, and capital flows for at least a generation.

Why this book matters

The most readable, accurate, and politically-current primer on what semiconductor manufacturing actually is, why its modern form (fabless-foundry split + EUV monopoly + Taiwan concentration) emerged, and how that emergence creates strategic chokepoints. Miller is a Tufts University historian of the Soviet economy and Cold War; his methodological background shows in the book’s central comparative frame (the Soviet failure to develop semiconductors as a model for what the US is not trying to do to China — and what China is trying to avoid).

The book is rare in being equally technically accurate (the lithography process, the doping steps, the EDA toolchain), historically rigorous (interviews with Morris Chang, Pat Gelsinger, ASML’s founders, etc.), and politically current (the October 2022 export controls, the CHIPS Act, the Taiwan-as-chokepoint strategic frame). Most semiconductor primers fail on one or two of these dimensions; Chip War maintains all three.

For Yang’s purposes:

(1) AI-policy work depends on understanding chip supply as the binding constraint on frontier-AI training. Chip War is the necessary primer; without it, AI-capability discussions float free of physical constraints.

(2) China-tech-policy research intersects directly with the chip-export-controls regime. The book documents both the US-side policy logic (which agencies pushed, which exemptions were granted, what the negotiating timeline looked like) and the China-side response (SMIC’s lag, the technology-self-sufficiency push, the 2023+ rebalancing). Reading Angela Huyue Zhang - High Wire and Chip War together gives the inside-China and outside-China views of the same strategic moment.

(3) Career thinking — the chip industry is a substantial employer of quants, ML researchers, and policy specialists. Understanding its structure is part of evaluating positions there.

The book also matters as the canonical reference for the technology-as-geopolitics school of analysis. Books that try this framing (Yasheng Huang on China-as-developmental-state; Walter Russell Mead on energy-as-strategy; Daniel Yergin on oil) succeed when the technology is well-understood and fail when it isn’t. Miller succeeds.

Core argument

  1. The transistor’s invention (1947, Bell Labs) and the integrated circuit (1958, Kilby/Noyce) created a new industrial category — solid-state electronics — whose manufacturing process is unusually demanding (cleanroom-level dust control, atomic-scale precision, sustained R&D investment) and whose product (the chip) becomes universally embedded across every other industry. Early-history chapters establish the technical and business foundations.

  2. The industry consolidated over 50 years from many integrated firms (Intel, Motorola, AMD, IBM all making their own chips) to a fabless-foundry split. By the 2010s, most chip design happens at fabless firms (Apple, NVIDIA, Qualcomm, AMD-modern); most leading-edge manufacturing happens at TSMC (Taiwan) and Samsung (Korea). The fabless-foundry split was not inevitable; it was the deliberate strategic invention of Morris Chang at TSMC (founded 1987) and is now structurally entrenched.

  3. Equipment manufacturing is even more concentrated. ASML (Netherlands) has a near-monopoly on EUV lithography — the single most-essential machine for producing leading-edge chips. Applied Materials, Lam Research, KLA (US) and Tokyo Electron (Japan) dominate other steps (deposition, etching, inspection). EDA software (Cadence, Synopsys, Mentor Graphics — all US) controls chip-design tooling. The supply chain has multiple single-points-of-failure that the US can leverage. The firms that built these chokepoints have Skin in the Game in a way that purely financial owners do not — process-engineering know-how is embodied in factory teams, not transferable through capital alone.

  4. China’s chip industry has lagged for decades despite massive investment. SMIC (China’s leading domestic foundry) trails TSMC by 5+ years on leading-edge processes. The lag is not for lack of trying or money; it reflects the cumulative tacit knowledge embedded in TSMC’s process engineering, ASML’s lithography engineering, and the fab-engineer-network that takes generations to grow. China’s late-2010s “Big Fund” semiconductor investments produced uneven results; Tsinghua Unigroup defaulted on debt; many Chinese fabs failed.

  5. The October 2022 US export controls (and follow-ups) deliberately exploit the supply-chain concentration. Restrictions cover (a) advanced chip-making equipment to specific Chinese fabs, (b) advanced AI chips to specific Chinese end-users, (c) chip-design EDA tools, (d) US-person involvement in advanced Chinese chip development. The October 7, 2022 announcement was the most consequential single tech-policy action of the 2020s. The escalation logic itself is reflexive in the Soros sense — each US tightening confirms the Chinese self-sufficiency thesis, which justifies more US tightening; the policy moves and the policy beliefs about China co-evolve.

  6. Taiwan is now a strategic chokepoint, not just a contested territory. TSMC produces 90%+ of the most-advanced chips globally. A Taiwan crisis would not just be a regional security event; it would freeze global advanced-electronics production immediately. This re-prioritization of Taiwan in US strategic planning is one of the most consequential implications of the chip industry’s concentration.

  7. The CHIPS Act 2022 (US) and parallel EU/Japan/Korea industrial-policy moves attempt to onshore enough manufacturing capacity to make export controls credible. Without onshore capacity, export controls would just shift production to other countries; with onshore capacity, the export-control regime becomes structurally durable.

  8. Implications: (a) a generation-long bilateral technology decoupling between US and China is underway. (b) Frontier-AI training is constrained by chip supply, with downstream effects on which countries can deploy advanced AI at scale. (c) Industrial policy is back in style, justifying interventions (subsidies, export controls, screened FDI) that were ideologically out of fashion since the 1980s. (d) The Soviet-failure analogy (heavy investment, weak ecosystem, ultimately falling behind) is being explicitly invoked by both US and Chinese policymakers, with opposite lessons drawn.

Argument map

The book reasons from technical foundations to industrial structure to current geopolitical stakes. Each transition is a chapter cluster.

The diagram emphasizes that the current geopolitical moment (October 2022 onwards) is structurally produced by 75 years of industry-formation choices. Every step matters: without Chang’s TSMC, no fabless-foundry split. Without ASML’s EUV monopoly, no equipment-side chokepoint. Without TSMC’s Taiwan concentration, no Taiwan-as-chokepoint logic. The strategic moves of 2022+ are only possible because of the structural moves of 1947-2020.

Key concepts

  • Fabless-foundry split — design separated from manufacturing; the structural invention of the modern industry
  • EUV lithography — extreme-ultraviolet light technology; the leading-edge process step ASML monopolizes
  • Process node — measure of transistor density (5nm, 3nm, 2nm); the central competitive metric
  • Export-control chokepoints — points in the supply chain where US export-licensing has decisive leverage
  • Cumulative tacit engineering knowledge — the unwritten know-how that makes process engineering possible; the reason China cannot replicate TSMC by spending money
  • Black Swan — chip-supply chokepoints make the industry uniquely vulnerable to a Taiwan crisis; explicitly Talebian framing applies
  • Antifragility — the book’s industrial-policy implications point toward antifragile industrial design (multiple production sites, redundant equipment-tooling supply, deliberate slack)

Key arguments created from this book

  • Chip-industry concentration is the central strategic asset of US-China competition — Miller’s central political claim
  • The Soviet semiconductor failure is the controlled experiment for what fails in chip-industry development — Miller’s distinctive historiographical contribution; uses the Soviet Cold War failure as a counterfactual for what China is trying to avoid

Best chapters/pages to actually read

  • Prologue + Cast of Characters — orientation. The Cast of Characters page (~12 names with one-line descriptions) is unusually useful for keeping track of who’s who across decades.
  • Part I (Cold War Chips, 1940s-1980s) — chs. 1-15, ~150 pp. The technical and corporate foundations. If short on time, focus on chs. 1-3 (transistor + IC invention), ch. 5 (Morris Chang’s TI years), and the Cast of Characters.
  • Part II (Japan’s Rise and Fall) — chs. 16-25. Important historical contrast — Japan in the 1980s was where China is in the 2020s, and the lessons (and disanalogies) matter for predictions.
  • Part III (TSMC and the Foundry Model) — chs. 26-35. The structural-invention chapters. Chang’s specific decisions in founding TSMC are the central case.
  • Part IV-V (China rises, equipment chokepoints emerge) — chs. 36-55. The current strategic-context chapters. Most directly relevant to current AI-policy / China-policy work.
  • Part VI (Recent Decoupling, 2018-onward) — chs. 56-end. The October 2022 export controls in their full institutional context. Read at minimum the chapters on the export-control announcement and the CHIPS Act.
  • Skim — middle chapters on specific business histories (TI’s consumer-electronics period, Intel’s various pivots) unless your research depends on the corporate detail.

Important examples / cases

  • Morris Chang’s emigration from Mainland China — Chang was born in Mainland China (Ningbo, 1931), educated at Harvard and MIT, worked at Texas Instruments for 25 years before being passed over for CEO in 1983 and departing to found TSMC in 1987 at age 56. The fabless-foundry split — separating chip design from chip manufacturing — was Chang’s specific strategic invention. The fact that the most consequential industry restructuring of the past 40 years was made by a Chinese-American chemical engineer who was passed over for a US CEO role is one of the genuinely strange specifics of the chip industry’s history.
  • The 1947 transistor at Bell Labs — Bardeen, Brattain, Shockley. AT&T (Bell Labs’ parent) initially saw the transistor as a phone-network signal-amplifier, not as the foundation of a new computing industry. The corporate misjudgment is instructive — disruptive technologies are often misclassified by their inventors’ employers.
  • Andy Grove’s Intel turnaround (1985-87) — Grove (Hungarian refugee; Andras Grof) led Intel’s exit from memory chips into microprocessors. The famous “only the paranoid survive” management style. Most directly applicable for any current strategic-pivot question.
  • The Japanese memory-chip dominance and US response (1980s) — Japanese firms (Toshiba, NEC, Hitachi, Fujitsu) dominated DRAM in the 1980s, threatening US semiconductor industrial base. The US response — Sematech consortium, anti-dumping cases, R&D coordination — is the policy precedent the CHIPS Act explicitly invokes. The Japanese firms then collapsed in the 1990s, with US industrial policy claiming credit (debatable; cycle dynamics matter too).
  • ASML’s near-impossible EUV development (1995-2017) — over $9 billion of R&D over two decades. The bet was so large that ASML required customers (Intel, TSMC, Samsung) to invest in the company directly, locking in their commitment. The success of EUV gave ASML its current monopoly position; the failure of competitors (Nikon, Canon failed to commercialize EUV) cemented it.
  • The October 7, 2022 export controls — Miller documents the specific Biden administration officials (Alan Estevez, Thea Kendler) who pushed the controls, the negotiating dynamics with allies (Japan and Netherlands eventually agreed to align their own controls), and the immediate impact on Chinese fabs. The policy was unusually broad and surprised both Chinese government and US tech-industry.
  • The Soviet semiconductor failure — the USSR invested heavily in semiconductors but failed to develop a competitive industry. Miller traces the structural reasons: weak ecosystem (no fabless-foundry split possible in command economy), poor international integration, brain drain, copycat strategy that never closed the gap. The Soviet case is Miller’s controlled experiment for what fails.

Critiques and limitations

  • The Taiwan chokepoint framing is overdetermined. Miller treats Taiwan-as-chokepoint as the essential current strategic frame; some critics argue this overestimates how much US policy is actually driven by chip-industry geography vs broader great-power competition logic. The book under-engages with the IR-realist counterargument that chip dominance is one of several strategic considerations, not the central one.
  • Optimistic on the CHIPS Act. Miller takes the 2022 CHIPS Act largely at face value as a strategic response. As of 2024+, the actual onshoring-of-fabs has been slower and more expensive than planned (TSMC Arizona delays, Intel fab cost overruns). The book’s predictive confidence about onshoring outpaces the actual evidence. A 2025-revision would have to acknowledge the slower pace.
  • Light on EDA tools. Miller treats the EDA software (Cadence, Synopsys, Mentor Graphics) as one chokepoint among several; in practice, EDA tools are arguably the most sticky chokepoint because they are deeply embedded in design teams’ workflows and have multi-year switching costs. The book’s coverage of EDA is correct but proportionally underweighted.
  • Weak engagement with the open-source / RISC-V counter-trajectory. Open-source instruction sets (especially RISC-V) and open-source EDA tools are a meaningful (if currently small) counter-development to the proprietary-lock-in story Miller tells. The book mentions RISC-V in the cast of characters but doesn’t develop the implications. China is investing heavily in RISC-V partly as an export-control workaround; this story deserves a chapter the book doesn’t have.
  • US-centric narrative voice. Miller is a US-trained historian writing for largely-US readers. The book is fair to non-US actors (especially Chang and ASML’s founders) but the strategic frame is US-strategic-frame. Reading it alongside a Chinese-perspective treatment (which doesn’t yet exist in English at this scope) would be productive.
  • Historical-economic determinism risk. The book reads the past as if the current chip-industry concentration was inevitable. In practice, many specific decisions could have gone differently (Japan could have maintained its 1980s dominance; Intel could have entered foundry; ASML’s EUV bet could have failed). The narrative tends to flatten contingency. Some of the most interesting parts of the book (Chang’s 1987 decision; ASML’s EUV bet) are recoverable as contingent rather than determined; the book mostly chooses determinism.
  • Underweights TSMC alternatives. Samsung is treated as TSMC’s #2 but is given proportionally less analysis. SMIC is covered but mostly as China’s failure case. Intel’s foundry strategy (announced 2021) is treated as too uncertain to evaluate. A more even treatment of the industry’s structure would weight all four major fab-operators more equally.

Links to other books

  • Direct geopolitical complement to Angela Huyue Zhang - High Wire — Zhang on the internal Chinese regulatory dynamics around tech (consumer-tech crackdown 2020-2022); Miller on the external US-imposed constraints (chip-export controls 2022 onward). The two together give the full picture of Chinese tech policy in 2020-2025: domestic crackdown driving capital toward “hard tech” + external sanctions limiting access to that hard tech. Read together for the full vertical.
  • Industrial-policy thinking aligned with Sebastian Mallaby - The Power Law and M. Mitchell Waldrop - The Dream Machine — Mallaby on VC’s role in 1990s+ tech development; Waldrop on ARPA’s role in 1960s-80s computing. Miller covers semiconductors specifically and traces both VC-style funding (Fairchild’s “traitorous eight” leaving Shockley to found their own firms) and ARPA-style government funding (Cold War missile-program demand for chips). The three books triangulate: what kind of capital structure produces transformative tech industries?
  • Strategic-competition framework adjacent to Acharya and Lee - The Cartel System of States — Acharya-Lee on the international state-cartel; Miller on how that cartel is now structuring tech-supply-chain rules. The chip-export-controls regime is partly a cartel-maintenance mechanism: states with chip-industry presence enforcing collective rules to manage non-cartel-member access.
  • Path-dependence implications for Meg E. Rithmire - Land Bargains and Chinese Capitalism — Rithmire on Chinese subnational variation; Miller documents how specific Chinese cities (Hefei, Wuhan, Shanghai, Shenzhen) became chip-fab hubs as a result of municipal industrial-policy bargains. Rithmire’s framework predicts that chip-fab hubs would not be uniform across cities, and Miller’s data confirms it.
  • AI-capability constraint discussion in Nick Bostrom - Deep Utopia and Max Bennett - A Brief History of Intelligence — Bostrom and Bennett discuss AI capability as if compute is exogenously available; Miller documents the manufacturing constraints on that compute and their political-economic distribution. Reading Bostrom and Miller together is reading the same trajectory from two ends.
  • Risk framework via Nassim Nicholas Taleb - Incerto — chip-industry concentration is a Black Swan-prone structure: a Taiwan crisis is low-probability per year but high-impact. Miller’s argument is the strategic-policy-implication of Taleb’s framework applied to one specific industry. Reading both together: Taleb names the structural feature (fat tails); Miller applies the diagnostic to the chip industry; the policy implication is to deliberately engineer Antifragility (multiple production sites, redundant equipment-tooling, deliberate slack).

Open questions

  • Will the export-controls regime hold under stress? US allies (Netherlands, Japan, Korea) have agreed in principle to align their controls but the actual implementation has been slower and more contested than the headline announcements suggest. Whether a future crisis (Taiwan tensions, Iran-Israel, US political turnover) erodes the alignment is open.
  • Can China close the leading-edge gap before the controls bite? SMIC’s progress on 7nm-class processes (announced 2023+) was a partial surprise; whether China can sustain leading-edge progress without ASML EUV access is the central technical question. Open empirical work; Miller’s framework predicts it will be very hard but not impossible.
  • What replaces TSMC if Taiwan goes hot? The “what-if” question Miller’s framework most clearly poses but does not answer. Onshoring takes 5-10 years per fab; a Taiwan crisis would freeze global supply for at least that long. The economic consequences would be unprecedented.
  • Does open-source / RISC-V change the strategic logic? If open-source instruction sets and EDA tools mature, the proprietary chokepoints Miller identifies become less binding. The trajectory is mostly outside the book’s frame but increasingly important.
  • What does the model predict for AI-specific chips (TPUs, NPUs, custom AI accelerators)? The fab-equipment chokepoints apply but the design concentration is different (NVIDIA dominates but is fabless; AMD, Apple, Google, Amazon, Microsoft, Meta all have custom-chip design teams). Whether AI-chip strategy follows the general semiconductor pattern or diverges is open.

Research uses

  • Yang’s tech-policy research: Miller is the primary source on how chip-export controls actually work, what their second-order effects are, and how they interact with broader US-China competition. Essential for any China-AI-policy or technology-and-society analysis. Specifically useful for distinguishing which AI capabilities are constrained by chip supply (training-frontier models, mass deployment) from which are not (inference, smaller models).
  • AI capability forecasting: chip supply directly bounds frontier-AI training. Understanding the supply chain is part of understanding AI trajectories. The book’s implicit prediction — that frontier AI will increasingly be a US-allied phenomenon — has policy implications for compute-access negotiations, AI-export-controls regime extensions, and AI-safety governance design.
  • Career thinking (Yang): the book documents a generation of strategic-technology specialists. Useful background for evaluating positions at chip companies (Intel, NVIDIA, ASML, TSMC), AI labs, or policy organizations focused on this nexus. The chip industry is unusually international and technical; entry paths combine engineering, policy, and capital-allocation expertise.
  • Comparative state-failure analysis: the Soviet-failure case Miller traces is portable to other state-led-tech-industry failures. Russian software, Indian aerospace, certain European-Commission tech initiatives all admit similar analysis. Useful diagnostic when evaluating any state-led tech-industry-development proposal.
  • AI-safety / -governance scenario planning: the export-controls regime is a partial template for what AI-safety-controls might look like if needed. Miller’s documentation of how the chip-controls were designed (multilateral coordination, specific entity-list focus, end-use restrictions) is the closest existing model for any future AI-controls regime.

What I would miss if I only read this note

Miller’s specific anecdotes are the textural content the note loses. Chang’s bridge-playing approach to manufacturing engineering (chs. 5-6); the “traitorous eight” who left Shockley to found Fairchild; the Hungarian-refugee back-story of Andy Grove that shaped his “only the paranoid survive” management; the specific Trump-era officials who pushed the 2019-2020 export controls; the ASML founders’ Dutch-fab conviction during the 1990s when EUV was widely thought impossible. These are not decoration; they are the human drivers of an apparently-deterministic industrial-policy story, and Miller’s argument is that the determinism is partly retroactive — many specific contingent decisions produced the current concentration.

The note also under-represents the book’s technical-explanatory depth. Miller explains the lithography process, the doping steps, the EDA toolchain, the wafer-fab sequence, in ways that propositional summary cannot reproduce. For a reader trying to actually understand what makes leading-edge chip manufacturing hard, the technical-explanatory chapters are essential and the note compresses them away.

Finally, the political-historical comparative texture — Miller’s repeated juxtaposition of Cold War US semiconductor industrial policy, Japanese 1980s industrial policy, and current US chip-act industrial policy — produces an analytical clarity that single-period treatments cannot. Reading the book chronologically, one is trained to see the parallels and disanalogies. The note flattens this; the book teaches the comparative-historical eye.
