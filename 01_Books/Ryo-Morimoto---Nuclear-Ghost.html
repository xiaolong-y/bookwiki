Nuclear Ghost — Atomic Livelihoods in Fukushima’s Gray Zone

One-sentence thesis

Across nine years of fieldwork in Minamisōma — the only city in coastal Fukushima that fell under all three TEPCO-accident evacuation zones yet refused total evacuation — Ryo Morimoto argues that the technosensory regime that visualizes radiation as the disaster’s defining metric (what he calls half-life politics) is itself a second species of harm against the residents who stayed, and that an ethnography of fallout attentive to the residents’ chosen lives, deaths, soil, ancestors, and multispecies entanglements (their atomic livelihoods) is the political-anthropological alternative the gray zone demands (Morimoto 2023, Intro and chs. 1–9).

Why this book matters

The book is the first English-language full-length engaged ethnography of post-3.11 coastal Fukushima built on continuous Japanese-language fieldwork (2013–2019, with follow-ups through 2022) by a Japanese anthropologist trained in the US (Princeton). That positionality is load-bearing: most English-language Fukushima literature is either biopolitical-critical (Petryna-style biological-citizenship reading imported from Chornobyl) or risk-communication technical (epidemiology, ICRP-policy critique). Morimoto’s book is neither — he refuses both the damage-centered framing (Tuck 2009) and the ghostbusting-the-radiophobia framing (Ono 2015 / mainstream RSO training), and instead develops a third position that is the book’s actual intervention.

For the BookWiki’s purposes the book matters in three concrete ways. First, it supplies a fully worked example of how to conduct sustained engaged ethnography in a post-disaster context where the dominant epistemic regime is technoscientific — the methodological lessons translate to any field where “the data” appears to settle a question that residents experience as unsettled (e.g., environmental-toxin debates, AI-risk debates, vaccine debates). Second, the named concept half-life politics is a portable diagnostic for the broader pattern in which a measurable proxy is allowed to stand in for a contested whole; this is one of the most useful conceptual exports from this book. Third, the atomic livelihoods frame supplies a positive-construction alternative to “victimhood” / “exposure” / “denial” — the three default categories outsiders impose on populations living in contaminated landscapes.

For readers tempted to file this as a regional Japan book: Morimoto explicitly links Fukushima to Hiroshima, Nagasaki, Chornobyl, Hanford, the Four Corners, the Marshall Islands, French Polynesia, the Belgian Congo, the Navajo Nation — i.e., the entire 20th-c. radioactive-colonialism geography. Tengo’s closing argument in chapter 9 (TEPCO operated 1F since the 1970s to supply Tokyo exclusively, while the harm fell on Hamadōri) makes the radioactive-colonialism reading explicit, citing Akasaka & Oguma (2012) and Iwamoto (1994, 2013a). The book’s portability to any periphery-bears-the-cost-of-the-center political geography is direct.

For policy / advocacy audiences, the book is sharply against two narratives: (i) the “residents are in denial” narrative held by anti-nuclear activists and outside journalists, and (ii) the “residents are stoic and have moved on” narrative held by state and TEPCO communications. Both treat residents as objects rather than agents. Morimoto’s evidence for the third position — that residents are agents engaged in active livelihood-construction with full awareness of contamination and equally full awareness that radiation is not the most important thing in their lives — is detailed, careful, and (where biographical specifics are involved) traceable to long-form field interactions with named informants (Hatsumi, Naoko, Tengo, Toru, Saeki, Sachiko, Yamada, Oshima, Miura, Nishiyama, Tsubokura, Hinton).

Core argument

  1. The radiation-centered narrative is itself a harm. When the state, experts, media, and outsiders treat the TEPCO accident only as a question of radiation dose and biomedical effect, they render visible only what is technoscientifically measurable and render invisible everything else residents value (cultural and intergenerational continuity, personal belongings, sustainability of livelihood, biodiversity, the dead, en with the supernatural). Morimoto names this pattern half-life politics — the term gestures at radioactive half-lives but the analytic is broader: a politics organized around a measurable half of the phenomenon, dropping the unmeasured half (Intro, ch. 5).

  2. The gray zone is real, perspectival, and not resolvable by more data. Borrowing from architect Kishō Kurokawa’s The Culture of Gray (1977), Morimoto treats the gray zone as a Buddhist-aesthetic category: gray is the color of all colors mixed and canceling each other, a state of continuous transition and shapeshifting whose meaning depends on perspective and time. Coastal Fukushima sits in a gray zone because: (i) the medical science of chronic low-dose radiation is genuinely uncertain — there is no scientific consensus on safety thresholds; (ii) the social experience oscillates between the universal (radiation is everywhere always) and the hyperlocal (Fukushima specifically); (iii) the political administration shifts standards (1 mSv/y → 20 mSv/y in November 2013) and zone boundaries with each policy revision (Intro, ch. 4).

  3. Atomic livelihoods are the empirical residue of the gray zone. Morimoto coins atomic livelihoods against atomic victimhood. The livelihoods are “atomic” in two senses: (i) materially, residents live in an objectively more contaminated environment near the damaged plant; (ii) sociologically, environmental disaster is at heart about discrimination that triggers divisions and corrodes a community (Ui Jun [1971] 2006). Naoko’s kariage garden in Kashima, Tengo’s two-hour commute to refuse compensation, Hatsumi’s caretaking of muen botoke (unidentified tsunami remains), Nishiyama’s TEPCO-employee-recruitment to clean Hiwashi shrine, Miura’s Shinchi rice farm with 20 Bq/kg internal limit (5× stricter than the 100 Bq/kg national standard) — these are atomic livelihoods (Intro, chs. 2, 5, 8).

  4. Distance is the operative variable, not contamination. The state’s evacuation orders (March 11–April 22, 2011) were initially set by physical-distance radii from 1F (2 km → 3 km → 10 km → 20 km → 30 km stay-indoors) and only secondarily, after April 22, 2011, redrawn to reflect actual contamination levels. Residents experienced and remembered the distance-based zones; the compensation policy cemented them. The result: districts in Minamisōma at virtually identical contamination levels (Kashima ~30 km, Haramachi ~20 km) received radically different compensation and evacuation status, with cascading social-economic consequences (Hatsumi-Kashima friend rupture; ch. 3 case of the resident whose house was administratively reclassified outside the 30 km zone, losing ~11 months of compensation — ch. 3, ~p. 4 of ch. 3 in extracted text).

  5. Compensation is structurally pathogenic. Compensation produced kimin (abandoned people, Wakamatsu) — those not compensated felt disposable; those compensated felt watched, stigmatized as compensation-dependent, and trapped (compensation flow ends ~1 year after district reopening, regardless of whether the household has rebuilt). The compensation policy structurally sets residents against each other and frames the harm as a static event with a quantifiable settlement (Galtung 1969 / Farmer 1996 structural violence). Saeki’s silence rule names the political condition: voicing concern about radiation marks one as stuck-in-the-past or envious; voicing acceptance marks one as indifferent. Either utterance loses (chs. 3, 4).

  6. Decontamination is biographical erasure dressed as remediation. The 2011 Act on Special Measures defines decontamination “waste” as “filthy and unnecessary matter” without specifying who decides. Frecon baggu (1-ton flexible polyethylene bags) accumulated to 965,000 m³ in 22 Minamisōma temporary-storage sites by March 2017. Naoko’s house was demolished as decontamination 2016; her topsoil bagged; her persimmon tree (with its layered migration-history meaning from the 1782–88 Tenmei Famine and Hokuriku migrants) removed; her wedding kimono almost discarded. Saeki’s read: decontamination is “kind of like a ritual” — its main psychosocial work is to lower anxiety by visibly cleaning, not to lower dose, since most properties already met the 1 mSv/y target before decontamination (chs. 6, 7).

  7. The radiation-centered frame is anthropocentric and ecologically naive. Decontamination chases boars from inside the 20-km zone outward; boars, having consumed contaminated food, become highly radioactive (Sōma incinerator ash >135,000 Bq/kg, 1,350× the food-safety standard); residents-turned-hunters cull, the city cremates, ash is concentrated. The system creates concentrated nuclear waste from organisms that were merely living in the contaminated environment. Hinton (radioecologist, Fukushima University) tracks boars and snakes as radiotracers. The TEPCO drone-chase strategy fails the ecological-systems test (chased boars become Sōma’s problem, then Date’s). The half-life politics frame breaks at the ecosystem boundary (ch. 9).

  8. The disaster is colonial, not punctate. Tengo’s closing argument: 1F operated since the 1970s to supply Tokyo exclusively; coastal Fukushima bore the harm. Hamadōri was already peripheralized — Isabella Bird’s 1880 Unbeaten Tracks, Akasaka 2014 on Tōhoku as “perpetual colony of Tokyo.” The 18th-c. Tenmei Famine demographics (~89,000 → ~32,000) and Hokuriku-migrant-Shin-Buddhist reconstruction prefigure the 2011 demographic collapse and gradual return. Hiroshima 1945 → Hamadōri 1971 (1F) → 2011 fallout → 2065+ decommissioning is a continuous radioactive-colonial chronotope, not a sequence of unrelated events (chs. 6, 9, epilogue).

  9. The methodological argument: ethnography as following en. En (縁) is the Buddhist concept of indirect cause, a hidden condition for interconnection (Hashimoto 2013; Jensen, Ishii, and Swift 2016; Nozawa 2015). Morimoto frames his ethnography as following invisible threads of chance encounter rather than searching for representative victims. This is methodologically distinctive: the book is not organized around a sample frame (random or purposive), and it does not aggregate quantitative outcomes. Instead, it threads through six or seven named informants whose biographies recur across chapters — the en-method is what allows the book to render the gray zone in textured, place-specific, non-spectacular form (Intro, ch. 1, ch. 9).

  10. The ethical argument: refusing damage-centered research. Morimoto cites Eve Tuck’s “Suspending Damage” (2009): research that establishes harm to achieve reparation fixates subjects as broken and further harms already-dispossessed populations. The book’s structural counter-move is to feature residents’ desires, humor, labor, spiritual practice, farming, aesthetic judgments — Naoko’s joking that she’d soon join the family altar’s cremated remains; Hatsumi’s anger at the foreign film director who wanted residents visibly furious; Tengo’s drunken theory of boars-as-ethical-surrogate-bodies. The book is not afraid of the residents’ agency; the agency is the empirical content (Intro, all chapters).

The diagram reads top-down. The 1955–1970s upstream produces the structural setup; the 2011 disaster cascades through distance-based emergency, compensation cementation, decontamination materialization, and the ecological boar-chase. The right-hand column tracks residents’ counter-construction (atomic livelihoods) parallel to the state’s narrative; the two columns interpenetrate at every step but never resolve. The bottom is the present, which Morimoto refuses to close — the age of decommissioning frames the reader’s continuing complicity.

Key concepts

  • Half-Life Politics — Morimoto’s central named concept (Intro, ch. 5); a technosensory politics that foregrounds the presence/absence of radiation in the environment and its potential biological harm above all other things; the portable diagnostic the book exports
  • Atomic Livelihood (Gray Zone) — Morimoto’s positive-construction alternative to “victimhood” (Intro, chs. 2, 7); the lives, deaths, farming, prayer, and ancestral care that remain after half-life politics has done its sorting
  • Sacrifice Zone Chronotope — the periphery-bears-the-cost-of-the-center temporal-spatial nesting (Hamadōri 1955→2065+) Morimoto traces (chs. 1, 6, 9)
  • Public Anthropology Method — UC Press’s California Series identity (Borofsky, Scheper-Hughes, Bourgois, De León, Ralph) and Morimoto’s en-based, refusal-of-damage-centered, named-informant ethnography is a worked example of the method
  • Structural Violence (Galtung 1969 / Farmer 1996) — explicitly invoked by Morimoto for the compensation policy (ch. 4); not yet a vault concept note
  • Infrastructural Power — implicit; the Japanese state’s despotic power vs. its infrastructural capacity to actually decontaminate, reach forests, make TEPCO accountable; useful frame for the gap between policy declaration and ground-truth implementation

Best chapters/pages to actually read

  • Introduction — the book’s conceptual core: nuclear ghost (Hatsumi vs. Ono), gray zone (Kurokawa borrow), atomic livelihoods coinage, half-life politics coinage, en-as-method, refusal of damage-centered research. Read in full; it is the most concentrated theoretical chapter and frames every subsequent move.
  • Chapter 3 (Kaleidoscopic Harm) — the political-cartography critique: the timeline of evacuation-order changes (March 11 distance-based → April 22 contamination-based → 2012 three-zone reorganization), the “stupid lines” anecdote, fūhyō higai legal-historical genealogy (1954 Lucky Dragon → 1956 kansetsu songai doctrine), the anzen vs. anshin distinction (Sternsdorff-Cisterna 2015). The cleanest chapter on how administrative geography produces social rupture.
  • Chapter 5 (Radioactive Mosquitos and the Science of Half-Lives) — the methodological-political payoff. Tsubokura’s locally-grounded WBC + Hibari FM radio program contrasted against Columbia Mailman School’s RSO training. The mosquito-bite question as residents’ translation of science back into experience. Tsubokura’s reflection that “every message hurts someone” is the cleanest single articulation of the book’s risk-communication ethic.
  • Chapter 7 (Frecon Baggu and the Archive of [Half-]Lives) — the biographical-erasure argument. Naoko’s wedding kimono, soil-as-intergenerational-patrimony, Saeki’s ritual reading of decontamination, the temporary-temporary storage humor (kari kari okiba). The most affectively concentrated chapter; it does the work the data-driven chapters cannot.
  • Chapter 9 (A Wild Boar Chase) — the multispecies-ecological argument. TEPCO drones, $1.6M wildlife incinerator, ash >135,000 Bq/kg, Hinton’s snake-radiotracer research, Tengo’s radioactive-colonialism closing. The chapter that exports the half-life politics critique beyond anthropocentrism. Worth reading even if time-constrained as the book’s structural climax.
  • Skim: ch. 6 (between fūhyō and fūka) is well-done but does heavy ethnographic-historical lifting on TEPCO’s PR strategy and 1F siting; useful if the radioactive-colonialism reading is the user’s priority. Epilogue (Age of Decommissioning) restates the political-temporal frame and is required if the book’s policy-relevance is the user’s question.

Important examples / cases

  • Hatsumi (Buddhist temple-keeper, Haramachi, ~60s in 2013) — the book’s epistemic anchor. Refused to elaborate on the nuclear ghost beyond the July 2013 conversation; the temple housed ~570 muen botoke (unidentified tsunami cremains); the Kashima-friend rupture; by 2017 she was no longer receiving visitors. Embodies the “live with it; don’t let outsiders narrate it” position.
  • Naoko (Odaka evacuee, late 70s in 2013, lived to ≥86 in 2019+) — the book’s affective center. Kariage in Kashima with Tengo, Toru, daughter-in-law; Buddhist family altar; persimmon tree; garden-plot 2015; house demolished 2016; warehouse archive. Her sustained desire to die at home in Odaka is the direct counter-evidence to evacuation-as-protection.
  • Tengo (Naoko’s son, Odaka) — the political-economic critic. Two-hour commute to refused company in Miyagi; refusal of compensation as identity preservation; air-rifle wildlife patrol upon 2019 return; the closing radioactive-colonialism speech.
  • Saeki (Kashima resident, mother of teenagers) — the silence-rule informant. Frecon-baggu first observation 2015; the disposability fear; the decontamination-as-ritual reading. Cleanest articulator of the silence-rule political condition.
  • Miura (Shinchi rice farmer, original Odaka) — the consumer-producer-solidarity figure. 5× stricter internal standard (20 Bq/kg vs 100 Bq/kg national); Fukushima Rice Is Safe but I Am Fine If You Do Not Want to Eat It (2015) book title. Refuses to wedge consumers and producers against each other.
  • Dr. Tsubokura (Tokyo U. → Fukushima U. radiation health) — the dialogic risk-communication exemplar. WBC monitoring July 2011, Hibari FM program 2012–2013, booklet for mothers 2014. “Every message hurts someone” is his signature.
  • Nishiyama (Hiwashi Shrine, Odaka, ~60s) — the spiritual-ecology figure. 650-year-old shrine; recruited TEPCO employees for cleaning (2014); priest-as-blesser-of-beginnings vs Hatsumi’s temple-as-ender-of-things.
  • Thomas Hinton (radioecologist, Fukushima University) — the multispecies-science figure. Boars-as-radiotracer GPS+dosimeter collars; snakes-as-radiotracer (responding to Hatsumi’s no-snakes remark from ch. 8); 2021 Cunningham et al. paper on wildlife resilience.

Critiques and limitations

  • The en method is unfalsifiable in the way ethnographic methods often are. Morimoto threads through six or seven named informants whose biographies he cultivates over nine years. This is methodologically powerful for rendering the gray zone but it is hard to know what the typical coastal Fukushima experience is — Hatsumi, Naoko, Nishiyama are not a sample. The atomic livelihoods frame is generative but cannot be checked against a population frequency. Morimoto would likely say the absence of frequency is the point; readers wanting external validity should triangulate with Sekiya and Takakura (2019), Kainuma (2011, 2015), Kumaki (2022).
  • The radiation-centered narrative critique cuts both ways. Morimoto’s argument that the dominant discourse over-attends to radiation is well-defended for Minamisōma (where average exposures are medically negligible) but does not generalize to Iitate village, the western parts of Namie (>200 μSv/h zones in Tsushima, March 13, 2011 readings >1,200 μSv/h), or other higher-dose areas where radiation is the central material harm. The book is honest about this geographical scope (chs. 7, 8) but the framework’s exportability needs the gray-zone qualifier kept loud.
  • TEPCO and state actors are largely absent voices. The 2019 TEPCO Tomioka-headquarters meeting (ch. 9) is the only sustained TEPCO-employee scene. The 200+ TEPCO drone engineers / Ministry of Environment decontamination policymakers / Reconstruction Ministry officials behind the policies are heard mostly through their public documents. This is a methodological choice consistent with public anthropology (give voice to the residents, not the powerful) but the demand-side of half-life politics (why the state and TEPCO chose this strategy and not another) is undertheorized.
  • The Hiroshima/Nagasaki/Chornobyl comparison is invoked rather than constructed. Morimoto cites Petryna (2013) and Masco (2006, 2015) on biological citizenship and the nuclear uncanny but does not develop a sustained counter-reading of those frames against the Fukushima case. The implicit argument — that biological-citizenship strategies don’t work in coastal Fukushima because residents refuse to weaponize their bodies — is gestured at but not fully argued against the cases where biological citizenship was mobilized (Chornobyl liquidators).
  • The atomic livelihoods coinage may be too residual. It is defined largely by what it is not (not victimhood, not exposure, not denial). The positive content (farming, prayer, soil-care, ancestor-care, multispecies coexistence) is rich but theoretically heterogeneous. A skeptical reader could argue that “atomic livelihoods” reduces to “ordinary life with chronic environmental contamination,” which has parallels in non-radioactive contaminated communities (Erin Brockovich-type cases, Flint Michigan, Bhopal). The portability is strong but the specificity to atomic contamination is weaker than the term suggests.
  • The ethical critique of damage-centered research could over-correct. Morimoto cites Tuck (2009) and refuses the “suffering slot” (Robbins 2013). But thyroid cancers are real; ~287 cases by June 2022 are not screening artifact in their entirety; some Fukushima children did die during evacuation. The book’s structural emphasis on residents’ agency and aliveness is a needed correction, but it risks underweighting the genuine acute-medical harm to a small number of individuals (especially those with high-dose immediate exposure or those still inside Tsushima in March 2011 who weren’t told). The book mostly handles this in passing references; a sustained argument would help.
  • Half-life politics is a rich coinage but the analytical machinery is informal. The term is used metaphorically (it gestures at half-lives) and analytically (it names the technosensory regime). Morimoto does not separate these uses cleanly, and a more formal version would distinguish (i) the radiation-focal cognitive regime, (ii) the technical-instrumental measurement regime, (iii) the administrative-cartographic regime, (iv) the compensation-economic regime. The book treats them as variations on a theme; readers wanting to deploy the concept elsewhere will need to do this disaggregation themselves.
  • The book is engaged anthropology but rarely-engaged-with-engaged-anthropology. Morimoto is in the California Series in Public Anthropology with Scheper-Hughes / Bourgois / De León lineage, but he does not engage extensively with the public-anthropology methodological tradition’s debates (engagement vs. detachment; the role of advocacy; positionality of the diaspora-Japanese-academic-trained-in-the-US). The book’s own methodological self-reflection is strong; its placement within the field is light.

Links to other books

  • Anthropological-political-economy companion — Authoritarian Commons - Neighborhood Democratization in Urban China. Both books are sustained engaged ethnographies of state-society relations in East Asia under conditions of contested authority. Qiao’s cooperating to resist (CtR) maps onto Morimoto’s atomic livelihoods: in both, residents inside a high-capacity authority cooperate with the state structurally while resisting at specific moments. Reading them together clarifies that the residents-as-agents framing is exportable across Asian-developmental-state ethnographies. The methodological-rigor contrast is also useful: Qiao supplements ethnography with survey data + legal-doctrinal analysis; Morimoto stays inside long-form ethnography. Different epistemic strategies for similar political-anthropological work.
  • Japanese modernity / atomic-future reckoning — Lin Zhongjie - Kenzo Tange and the Metabolist Movement. Lin’s history of postwar Japanese architecture and the Metabolist movement (1960s) traces the atomic-modern aesthetic emergence in Tange’s Hiroshima Peace Memorial Park (1955) and his nuclear-age city plans. Morimoto’s ch. 6 traces the same postwar moment from the other end: 1955 is when TEPCO founded its nuclear-research department and began planning the 1F siting. Tange’s atomic-modern hope and the Hamadōri sacrifice are two sides of the same 1955 moment. The two books should be read as a pair for any work on Japanese postwar atomic-modernity.
  • Subnational China parallel — Meg E. Rithmire - Land Bargains and Chinese Capitalism. Rithmire’s land-bargain framework (city-developer accord) maps onto the 1F siting deal (TEPCO-municipality kyōzon kyōei “symbiosis & mutual prosperity”). In both, the upstream institutional accord that produced the contemporary geography is the analytical key. Reading the two together produces a comparative frame for how peripheral regions accept centralizing infrastructure and bear the consequences — applicable to nuclear plants, megaprojects, data centers, etc.
  • Geopolitical context — Chris Miller - Chip War. Miller’s account of US-China-Japan-Taiwan-Korea semiconductor competition includes the Japanese postwar industrial-policy period that produced TEPCO’s plant-siting framework. The 2022 Russian invasion of Ukraine + climate-change pressure (Morimoto’s epilogue) is reshaping the same energy-vs-security tradeoff that Miller’s book traces for chips. The two books are different slices of the same Japanese late-20th-c. industrial-state geometry.
  • Voter-rationality / risk-communication contrast — Bryan Caplan - The Myth of the Rational Voter. Caplan argues democratic outcomes are bad because voters are rationally irrational. Morimoto’s ch. 5 implicit critique of the “radiophobia” framing (Ono / Ropeik / Columbia Mailman School) is structurally a refusal of Caplan-style epistemic-paternalism: residents are not irrational, they are operating in a different epistemic register (life vs. science). The books disagree on whether the epistemic-deference-to-experts solution is right. Reading them together sharpens the question of who decides what counts as expertise in contested-evidence policy domains.
  • Institutional-failure cousin — Joseph E. Stiglitz - Globalization and Its Discontents Revisited. Stiglitz’s IMF-technocratic-failure analysis parallels Morimoto’s ICRP/IAEA radiation-safety-paradigm analysis. Both books document how a technocratic apparatus oriented around a single measurable proxy (GDP growth, mSv exposure) systematically underweights what the measure does not capture. Both call for a reframing toward affected populations’ situated knowledge. The portability is strong; the concept of half-life politics generalizes to technical-paradigm-as-political-displacement.
  • Medicine-as-empirical-stalemate parallel — James Le Fanu - The Rise and Fall of Modern Medicine. Le Fanu’s argument that 1980s+ epidemiology produced inconclusive findings dressed as policy guidance maps onto Morimoto’s ch. 5 critique: the science of chronic low-dose radiation has no consensus; “no scientific consensus on the effects of chronic low-dose exposure” is the gray-zone’s epistemic foundation. Both books surface how medical-scientific authority overreaches its empirical base. Useful pairing for any work on policy-relevant scientific authority.

Open questions

  • Will the atomic livelihoods frame export to non-radioactive contaminated communities? The frame’s content (livelihood-construction in a contaminated landscape with state-administered remediation that is also biographical erasure) is structurally similar to Erin Brockovich’s PG&E case, Flint Michigan water, Bhopal post-1984, post-Three Mile Island Pennsylvania. Whether the en-method finds the same texture in those cases without the Buddhist-aesthetic vocabulary is open.
  • Can half-life politics be operationalized for non-radiation domains? The concept is rich but informally defined. Operationalization as a four-part regime (cognitive / instrumental / administrative / economic) might allow it to be exported to AI-risk debates, vaccine-safety debates, climate-attribution debates. Morimoto does not do this; future research could.
  • What does the en-method’s null hypothesis look like? Morimoto’s evidence is six or seven named informants whose biographies recur. What if the gray zone is actually inhabited by mostly-disengaged residents who don’t have the en-method’s articulate interlocutors? The book’s empirical residue would not detect them. A complementary survey-based study (Kumaki?) could test.
  • Will the 2045 out-of-Fukushima decontamination-waste promise be kept? The state has no host-prefecture agreement. If the 2045 deadline slips (highly likely), what is the residents’ political-rhetorical recourse? The legality-review pipeline that worked in Qiao’s Authoritarian Commons (Wei v. Huizhou, 2023) does not have a Japanese parallel; what comes next?
  • Can Tsubokura-style locally-grounded risk communication be institutionalized? The chapter portrays him as a charismatic individual exception to the ghostbusting-RSO mainstream. Whether the next nuclear accident’s risk-communication architecture can build on the Tsubokura model — or whether it depends on rare individual commitment — is open.
  • What is the methodological status of refusing damage-centered research when the data show damage? The thyroid-cancer cluster and the >440 evacuation-related deaths in Namie are real harms. Morimoto handles them in passing but does not theoretically integrate them with the atomic-livelihoods-against-victimhood frame. A future ethics-of-engaged-anthropology paper would need to specify when refusing damage-centered research becomes refusing-to-acknowledge-damage.
  • How does the radioactive-colonialism reading translate to the Marshall Islands, French Polynesia, Navajo Nation? Morimoto cites these comparisons (Brugge et al. 2006; Bannerman 2018) but does not develop them. A genuinely comparative radioactive-colonialism literature would test the atomic livelihoods frame against Indigenous post-nuclear cases. The 2022 Russian invasion + Zaporizhzhia raises European cases.

Research uses

  • Yang’s blockchain regulation research / China political economy. The half-life politics frame is useful for any case where a measurable proxy is allowed to stand for a contested whole. In Chinese crypto regulation, the proxy is “financial stability” or “anti-money-laundering”; the unmeasured-half is innovation, capital flight, technological sovereignty. Morimoto’s analytical machinery (technosensory regime → administrative cartography → compensation-economy → biographical erasure) can be retooled for regulatory regimes outside radiation. Concrete next step: write a short note applying half-life politics to one Chinese crypto-regulation case (the 2021 mining ban, the DCEP-merchant rollout).
  • Subnational comparative political-economy. Coastal Fukushima (post-2011), Northeast China (Rithmire), Beijing/Shanghai/Shenzhen condominium commons (Qiao) are three sub-national East Asian samples worth integrating. Common variable: state’s chosen measurable proxy in a high-stakes governance domain (radiation, land valuation, neighborhood compliance) and the proxy’s social externalities. The atomic-livelihoods / cooperating-to-resist / land-bargain triad is a productive comparative typology.
  • Engaged anthropology methodology. Morimoto is a teaching example of how to do nine-year sustained fieldwork without the damage-centered framing. For Yang’s qualitative-research-design work, the book demonstrates: (i) cultivate 5–10 named informants whose biographies recur across chapters; (ii) refuse the urgent-ethnography frame for slow ethnography; (iii) write against the dominant outside-narrative rather than for an inside-the-discipline audience; (iv) use vernacular concepts (en, koi, anzen/anshin) as analytical infrastructure rather than translating them away.
  • Radioactive-colonialism comparative frame. The book’s chs. 6, 9 chronotope (Hiroshima 1945 → Hamadōri 1971 → 2011 → 2065+) is portable to any periphery-bears-the-cost-of-the-center case. For Yang’s policy-research consulting, this is useful when evaluating energy / megaproject / data-center siting decisions: who is the periphery, what is the temporal nesting, what is the deferred-disposition promise (analog of 2045 out-of-Fukushima).
  • Risk-communication design. Tsubokura’s locally-grounded WBC + Hibari FM model + “every message hurts someone” ethic is a teachable design pattern for risk communication in any contested-evidence domain (vaccines, AI safety, environmental toxins). For Yang’s work on regulatory communication, the design lessons translate directly.
  • Teaching uses. The book is the right single text for a session on: (a) post-disaster ethnography methodology; (b) science-and-technology-studies on contested-evidence regimes; (c) East Asian state-society relations under technocratic governance; (d) public-anthropology methodology; (e) environmental humanities. Pair with Petryna Life Exposed (Chornobyl) for the biological-citizenship contrast Morimoto is explicitly arguing against.
  • Policy-memo target. For policymakers in foreign ministries / multilateral agencies engaging with Japan-energy or East-Asia-nuclear-policy questions, the book is the right reference to cite when arguing against the technical-data-settles-the-question framing and for the residents-have-situated-knowledge-that-must-be-incorporated framing. Specific use: ch. 5’s Tsubokura/Columbia contrast is the cleanest exhibit for design recommendations.

What I would miss if I only read this note

  • The vernacular Japanese. The book is built on careful Japanese translation work — en, innen, ketsu-en, kusare-en, muen botoke, higaisha, hibakusha, hikensha, katarinikui, ikinikui, anzen, anshin, koi, takai, hikui, fūhyō, fūka, higai, jitsugai, ikune, benkei, kaga nami, yowai tsunagari, kyōzon kyōei, kyouji, bokeru, nuclear obake, radio-active brain (housha nō), jinsai, jyosen, isen, hanten kou, hairo no jidai, hakuchi, kari kari okiba, kanrenshi, kasetsu, kariage, yosomono, waganohito, en-inflected ethnography. The note uses a handful; the book uses the full lexicon as analytical infrastructure. Reading the book in full trains the analytical ear for Japanese-vernacular conceptual moves.
  • The interview voice. Hatsumi’s tone in July 2013, Naoko’s bange/omakane dinner-time inflections, Tengo’s drunken theory of boars-as-surrogates, Nishiyama’s loud laughter at the dinner he hosted for the TEPCO employees, Saeki’s smartphone-bargaining over her parents’ blue bucket — the texture of how each interlocutor narrates the gray zone is the empirical residue that the note flattens. Read the book for the texture; the note gives only the analytical residue.
  • The Murakami epigraphs. Each chapter opens with a Haruki Murakami quote (1Q84, Norwegian Wood, Kafka on the Shore, A Wild Sheep Chase, Colorless Tsukuru Tazaki, After Dark, the 2018 New Yorker interview). Morimoto borrows Murakami’s vocabulary of the surreal-as-ethnographic-mode systematically; the epigraphs are not decorative but methodological. The note loses this entirely.
  • The maps and figures. Maps 1–5 (UNSCEAR effective dose to infants; Minamisōma 2014; August 2011 evacuation; coastal Fukushima 2020; March 2018 decontamination zoning) and figures 1–19 (radiation monitoring posts, frecon baggu, the cow memorial tablet, the wedding altar, Naoko at her farm, Tengo with air rifle, the boar tail at Odaka district hall, the boar carcasses in Sōma incinerator freezer, the workers piling boar carcasses for incineration) carry the book’s visual argument. Morimoto’s photographs are integral to the en-method’s place-specificity. The note has none.
  • The footnote infrastructure. ~300+ footnotes across the book, many citing Japanese-language sources (Kainuma, Akasaka, Iwamoto, Sekiya, Yokemoto, Sawano, Kuroda, Tsubokura’s papers) not catalogued in English-language databases. The footnote tree is the bibliographic infrastructure for further research; the note loses it.
  • The author’s positionality wrestling. Morimoto explicitly works through being a western-Japan native (Aichi) doing fieldwork in northeastern Japan (Hamadōri); a US-trained anthropologist writing for an English-language audience; a Japanese person navigating both Japanese and Anglophone conversations about Fukushima; a researcher who chose to call it the TEPCO accident in English. The wrestling is muted in chapters but legible across the book; the note loses it.
