Robert J. Gordon - The Rise and Fall of American Growth

Read chapter-by-chapter → Deep dive of The Rise and Fall of American Growth (5 chapters)

One-sentence thesis

American growth was dominated by a one-time special century from roughly 1870 to 1970, when electricity, sanitation, internal combustion, communication, chemicals, medicine, and mass production transformed ordinary life more deeply than post-1970 digital innovation has, while inequality, education, demography, debt, globalization, and environmental constraints now slow the translation of invention into median living standards.

Why this book matters

Gordon is making the most systematic stagnation argument in modern economic history. The book’s strength is not a single productivity chart; it is the domain-by-domain reconstruction of ordinary life. He asks the reader to compare 1870 with 1940, and then 1940 with 2015, across light, water, sewage, transport, food, clothing, retail, communication, entertainment, medicine, work, household labor, risk, and government. The result is a historically grounded version of the claim that some inventions are more basic than others. Indoor plumbing is not just one consumer convenience among many; it destroys a regime of disease, hauling, smell, darkness, and household labor.

This is the natural empirical counterweight to David Deutsch - The Beginning of Infinity. Deutsch argues that explanatory knowledge opens an infinite frontier; Gordon argues that the frontier’s effect on measured living standards depends on which constraints remain to be solved. It also belongs beside J. Storrs Hall - Where Is My Flying Car, but the emphasis differs. Hall asks why the expected physical future failed to arrive; Gordon argues that the deepest physical transformation already happened, and that the digital future, while real, has not matched the earlier package.

For the tech shelf, the book is an antidote to semiconductor triumphalism. Chris Miller - Chip War, Leslie Berlin - The Man Behind the Microchip, and Andrew S. Grove - Only the Paranoid Survive show why chips matter. Gordon asks a colder question: how much of Moore’s Law became broad productivity growth and median welfare? That makes the book essential for AI and automation debates. A technology can be astonishing at the frontier and still produce modest aggregate effects if diffusion, complementary institutions, labor markets, and headwinds block the channel to living standards.

Core argument

  1. Growth is episodic, not smooth. Gordon rejects the idea that modern economic growth proceeds at a stable trend driven by a generic innovation machine. The post-Civil-War United States experienced a cluster of general-purpose inventions that took decades to diffuse, recombine, and reorganize life. The gains were concentrated in a historically unusual interval. The title’s “rise and fall” is a periodization claim: growth rose because the great inventions arrived and diffused; growth fell because later inventions had less material scope and because structural headwinds intensified.

  2. The baseline of 1870 matters. Gordon begins by reconstructing life and work in 1870 not as medieval misery but as a still-constrained world. America was already relatively rich by international standards, yet ordinary households faced darkness, isolation, dangerous water, outdoor privies, horse-based transport, limited medical care, home production of food and clothing, hard physical labor, and high risk. This baseline makes the later transformation legible. The reader is not asked to admire inventions in isolation, but to track which daily burdens disappeared.

  3. The home was a central site of revolution. Electric light, central heating, running water, sewer connections, gas networks, household appliances, and telephones changed domestic life more than most macro histories admit. Gordon’s strongest chapters are about the hidden labor of ordinary households, especially women’s work: hauling water, tending fires, washing clothes, preserving food, cleaning soot, and managing illness. Electrification and plumbing altered time use, health, comfort, and the boundary between home and market.

  4. Transportation and communication integrated the nation. Internal combustion, rail improvements, automobiles, paved roads, trucking, aviation, telegraphy, telephony, newspapers, magazines, radio, and movies compressed space and time. Gordon emphasizes the move from isolation to connection: a rural household without mail delivery, phone, radio, or reliable roads lived in a different social universe. The automobile did not merely move people faster; it reorganized suburbs, retail, labor markets, courtship, tourism, and the built environment.

  5. Health improvements were civilizational. Clean water, sewer systems, pasteurization, public-health campaigns, antibiotics, vaccines, imaging, and hospital systems drove down infant mortality and infectious disease. Gordon distinguishes sharply between early public-health gains and later expensive medical innovation. The earlier package often produced large population-level benefits by removing environmental causes of disease; later medicine produces real gains but at higher cost and with less effect on measured productivity.

  6. The postwar golden age was a diffusion-and-completion phase. The midcentury period did not invent every great technology from scratch. It completed the spread of electricity, cars, appliances, suburbs, air conditioning, mass retail, commercial aviation, television, corporate benefits, pensions, and government safety nets. This is why the 1940-1970 period could feel like explosive progress: it combined accumulated prewar invention with wartime mobilization, postwar demand, mass production, union wages, education, and expanding public infrastructure.

  7. Computers are powerful but narrower in everyday transformation. Gordon does not deny the computer revolution. His chapter on computers recognizes Moore’s Law, mainframes, SABRE, banking automation, ATMs, photocopying, word processing, personal computers, ARPANET, Mosaic, Windows 95, the Web, and smartphones. But he argues that much of the productivity effect was concentrated in a limited window and in specific sectors. Once offices had word processors, databases, email, spreadsheets, barcodes, and networked systems, later improvements often became faster, smaller, and more convenient rather than comparably transformative.

  8. The future faces headwinds. Gordon’s long-run forecast depends not only on slower innovation but on distribution and structure. Inequality means aggregate gains do not become median gains. Education no longer expands as dramatically as it did in the high-school and college revolutions. Demographic aging reduces labor-force growth. Debt constrains public investment. Globalization pressures wages. Energy and environmental constraints complicate growth. These headwinds mean even decent innovation may produce disappointing median living standards.

  9. The book’s deeper move is to compare technologies by the constraints they remove. A technology that saves minutes, entertains cheaply, or improves information access can be valuable while still ranking below a technology that eliminates contaminated water, night darkness, horse manure, household fuel hauling, or lethal childhood infection. Gordon is not asking whether an invention is impressive at the frontier; he is asking whether it changes the normal life of most households in ways that are broad, cumulative, and hard to imagine giving up.

  10. Gordon’s pessimism is therefore a diffusion argument as much as an invention argument. The earlier great inventions required complementary networks: electrical grids, pipes, roads, sewers, schools, hospitals, insurance systems, retail logistics, and public-health administration. A future AI or biotech wave would have to build comparable complements before it could overturn his forecast. A laboratory breakthrough or a high stock-market valuation is not enough; the question is whether the breakthrough becomes a cheap, reliable, universal input into daily life.

Argument map

  1. The 1870 baseline is a rich-country baseline, but daily life is still constrained by darkness, disease, isolation, horse transport, household drudgery, and high risk.
  2. The great inventions - electricity, sanitation, internal combustion, communication, chemicals, medicine, and mass production - remove basic physical constraints.
  3. Their effects depend on diffusion and recombination through homes, cities, factories, farms, transport systems, retail, entertainment, public health, and government.
  4. The 1940-1970 golden age occurs when earlier inventions reach mass adoption, wartime systems spill over, wages rise, and institutions spread gains broadly.
  5. Post-1970 digital innovation is real and important, but Gordon argues it is narrower in material scope and weaker in measured productivity effects.
  6. The headwinds convert even successful innovation into slower median-income growth when inequality, education plateaus, demographics, debt, globalization, and environment absorb gains.

Key concepts

  • Special Century - Gordon’s main periodization: 1870-1970 as a unique transformation.
  • Headwinds to Growth - structural forces blocking future median living-standard growth.
  • Integrated Circuit - the strongest post-1970 counterexample; Gordon treats computing seriously but denies it matches the earlier great inventions.
  • Infrastructural Power - electricity, water, roads, sewers, and communication networks matter because they change the substrate of daily life.
  • Market Failure - public health, sanitation, education, roads, and environmental constraints show why growth is not only private invention.
  • Industrial Policy of Foreign Direct Investment - useful contrast: Gordon studies the U.S. internal transformation, while LKY shows late-developer leapfrogging through multinationals.

Best chapters/pages to actually read

Read chapter 1 first, then chapters 2, 4, 5, 7, and 8. Those chapters give the material baseline and the deepest transformations: home, transport, disease, and work. Chapter 6 on communication is worth reading for the claim that 1870-1940 already produced a mass-media revolution before television and the Internet. Chapters 13 and 17 are essential for tech readers because Gordon gives the computer revolution its due and then limits its macro significance. Chapter 18 and the postscript contain the forecast and the headwinds.

If time is short, do not start with the computer chapter. Gordon’s argument only works after the reader has felt how different 1870 was. The book is persuasive because the first half changes the comparison set. “The Internet changed everything” sounds less decisive after chapters on water, sewage, electric light, automobiles, antibiotics, and the end of routine household drudgery.

Important examples / cases

  • Rural Free Delivery - a reminder that communication progress was institutional as well as technological; mail delivery changed rural isolation.
  • Indoor plumbing and sewerage - the strongest example of welfare gains understated by GDP: health, dignity, time, smell, and household labor all changed.
  • Radio and movies - mass culture arrived before the Internet; Gordon uses the 1939 arrival of color/sound film masterpieces as a symbolic endpoint.
  • Automobiles - the car is a general-purpose transformation: mobility, logistics, suburbs, retail, dating, tourism, policing, and road finance.
  • Antibiotics and public health - health gains came from both medical science and infrastructure; later high-tech medicine has more mixed cost-benefit effects.
  • SABRE and banking automation - computers first mattered by automating information-intensive back offices before they became consumer devices.
  • Mosaic / Windows 95 / broadband - the consumer Internet is real but temporally late and, in Gordon’s view, less material than water, electricity, and cars.
  • Moore’s Law slowdown - Gordon emphasizes demand-side and practical limits: once chips are fast enough for common tasks, extra speed has weaker welfare effect.
  • The 1920s as a convergence decade - electric utilities, autos, roads, household appliances, radio, chemical products, and urban public-health infrastructure begin reinforcing one another, which is why Gordon treats the later leap as cumulative rather than as one invention’s triumph.
  • Women and household labor - washing, water carrying, cooking fuel, cleaning soot, preserving food, and child illness are central because they show progress as time use and bodily burden, not only market output.
  • Zero-price digital services - search, maps, email, online media, and social networks are the hardest cases for Gordon because they create consumer surplus that GDP undercounts, yet he still asks whether they rival sanitation or electrification in depth.

Critiques and limitations

The largest critique is measurement and imagination. Gordon is strongest where welfare is material and visible: water, sewers, electricity, cars, antibiotics. He is weaker where digital goods produce consumer surplus, optionality, search, coordination, creative tools, and zero-price services that GDP handles poorly. The smartphone may not remove chamber pots, but it changes navigation, communication, photography, banking, learning, and work coordination in ways aggregate productivity can miss.

The book also predates the post-2022 AI wave. That does not refute Gordon, but it changes the live question. If AI mostly automates text, code, search, and administrative tasks, it may fit his “narrow digital” pattern. If it accelerates scientific discovery, robotics, drug development, education, and energy technology, it could be a stronger challenge. Gordon’s framework remains useful precisely because it tells us what would have to be true for AI to matter at the scale of electricity or internal combustion.

Gordon’s American focus is both a strength and a limit. The U.S. was the frontier economy for much of the period, but late-developing countries can experience compressed versions of the special century. The book is less useful for China, Singapore, South Korea, or India without a diffusion-and-catch-up layer. Finally, the pessimistic forecast can be psychologically misleading if read as destiny. David Deutsch - The Beginning of Infinity is the necessary counterweight: slow growth is not a law of nature.

Links to other books

  • Core philosophical counterpoint - David Deutsch - The Beginning of Infinity says knowledge has infinite reach; Gordon says historical living-standard transformation depends on which constraints remain.
  • Engineer-stagnation companion - J. Storrs Hall - Where Is My Flying Car shares growth disappointment but places more blame on regulation and physical-technology suppression.
  • Semiconductor counterweight - Chris Miller - Chip War and Leslie Berlin - The Man Behind the Microchip show why Gordon cannot dismiss chips casually; Gordon’s question is whether chips transformed broad welfare enough.
  • Strategy connection - Andrew S. Grove - Only the Paranoid Survive explains firm-level inflection points inside the computer revolution Gordon treats macroeconomically.
  • Policy complement - Abhijit V. Banerjee - Good Economics for Hard Times is a humility check on growth policy and distribution after the special century.

Research uses

Use Gordon as the baseline for evaluating claims that AI, crypto, biotech, robotics, or clean energy will transform growth. Ask: does the technology remove a basic constraint like darkness, disease, isolation, water contamination, physical immobility, or household drudgery? Does it diffuse broadly? Does it complement labor or displace median workers? Does it show up in productivity, health, time use, or consumer surplus? Are headwinds strong enough to absorb the gains?

For economic history, the book is a model of “standard-of-living history” rather than pure GDP history. It shows why serious growth analysis needs bathrooms, kitchens, roads, hospitals, schools, offices, insurance, and family structure. For policy, it warns that innovation policy alone is insufficient if education, inequality, debt, demography, and public infrastructure block diffusion.

Open questions

  • Does generative AI attack enough high-value bottlenecks to challenge Gordon’s post-1970 pessimism?
  • How should zero-price digital consumer surplus be compared with sanitation, cars, and antibiotics?
  • Are the headwinds reversible through policy, or are they structural features of mature economies?
  • Can climate technology produce a new “great invention” wave, or is it primarily defensive replacement?
  • How does Gordon’s U.S.-frontier story translate to late-developer catch-up cases?

What I would miss if I only read this note

I would miss the accumulation of concrete ordinary-life detail. Gordon’s argument is persuasive because it makes 1870 physically vivid: darkness, manure, contaminated water, exhausting laundry, dangerous food, infectious disease, isolation, and insecure work. A summary can state that indoor plumbing mattered; the book makes the reader understand why it mattered more than another app.

I would also miss the empirical discipline of moving domain by domain. The book can feel long because it is long, but the length is part of the method. Gordon is trying to prevent the reader from cherry-picking a favorite technology. He forces every candidate revolution to compete against the full bundle of electricity, sanitation, internal combustion, public health, communication, and mass production. That comparative discipline is the book’s durable value.
