Jonathan Berk and Peter DeMarzo - Corporate Finance

One-sentence thesis

A graduate-grade U.S. corporate-finance textbook organised around a single load-bearing axiom — the Law of One Price (no-arbitrage) — from which the authors derive NPV as a theorem (Ch. 3), the CAPM as the equilibrium consequence of mean-variance optimisation under homogeneous beliefs (Ch. 11), the Modigliani-Miller propositions as the perfect-capital-market benchmark for capital structure (Ch. 14), the WACC / APV / FTE equivalence as a value-additivity statement under leverage (Ch. 18), and the Black-Scholes-Merton option-pricing formula as the continuous-time limit of replicating-portfolio pricing (Ch. 21) — sequencing the reader from valuation primitives through risk-adjusted discount-rate estimation, capital-structure-friction theory, leveraged DCF, options theory, real options, raising capital, working-capital management, and the special topics of M&A, governance, risk management, and international corporate finance, on the wager that one axiom plus value-additivity plus equilibrium-investor optimisation generates a self-consistent treatment of every CFO and treasurer decision a public corporation faces.

Why this book matters

This is the Sixth Edition (Global Edition, Pearson 2023) of Berk and DeMarzo’s Corporate Finance — the dominant graduate / advanced-undergraduate corporate-finance textbook in U.S. business schools and a serious competitor to Brealey-Myers-Allen and Ross-Westerfield-Jaffe at the MBA level. Stanford GSB, Berkeley Haas, Wharton, Booth, and Columbia all use Berk-DeMarzo or its companion Fundamentals of Corporate Finance as the primary text. Jonathan Berk (A.P. Giannini Professor of Finance, Stanford GSB; ex-Goldman; Stephen A. Ross Prize winner) and Peter DeMarzo (Mizuho Financial Group Professor, Stanford GSB; past president of the American Finance Association) are theorists whose research-frontier work is in dynamic security design, capital-structure theory, and active-management equilibrium (the Berk-Green 2004 mutual-fund-flow model). Their pedagogical insistence that arbitrage organises every result — rather than appearing as a derivative-pricing tool in an options chapter — is the textbook’s single most distinctive choice.

For a research-oriented reader (quant-finance interview prep, private-equity case interviews, equity-research training, CFO-track corporate-development work, blockchain-economics modelling, AI-policy-financial-regulation analysis), this textbook serves three functions distinct from a graduate text like Cochrane’s Asset Pricing or Tirole’s Theory of Corporate Finance: it provides (i) a shared dialect for discussing capital-budgeting, capital-structure, and valuation problems with practitioner counterparties (bankers, lawyers, accountants, controllers); (ii) a quick refresher between the formal results (CAPM, MM, Black-Scholes) and their U.S.-2020s-vintage worked examples (Tesla beta, Netflix dividend policy, Snap IPO, Amazon’s CCC<0); and (iii) a baseline curriculum-shape for what topics are expected in MBA and CFA-curriculum corporate-finance instruction, against which to detect gaps in newer pedagogy (open-access OER, modular CORE-Finance pods).

The 6th-edition Global timing matters: Chapters 6, 16, 23, 30 fold in 2008–2020 stress-period data (Lehman, Bear Stearns, money-market-fund “breaking the buck”, March 2020 oil-price collapse, COVID-era IPO surge), and Ch. 21 carries Myron Scholes’ own retrospective on the 2007–2009 financial-crisis criticism of Black-Scholes — making the text a snapshot of how the corporate-finance canon absorbed two decades of crisis-driven model-skepticism without yet abandoning the theory’s core. It is also the textbook paired with Daron Acemoglu - Microeconomics as the price-theory / corporate-finance diptych in U.S. business-school curricula.

Core argument

The argument is pedagogical (what corporate-finance machinery is most teachable as a single coherent body) rather than research-frontier (what new results to prove). It proceeds:

  1. One axiom organises the entire textbook (Ch. 1, Ch. 3). The Law of One Price (no-arbitrage) — that two assets generating identical cash flows in every state must trade at the same price in a competitive market — is asserted in Ch. 1 and proved as the operational axiom in Ch. 3.4. No other axiom is needed for the rest of the book: NPV (Ch. 3), bond pricing (Ch. 6), DDM and FCF valuation (Ch. 9), MM I and II (Ch. 14), interest tax shield (Ch. 15), WACC / APV / FTE equivalence (Ch. 18), put-call parity (Ch. 20), binomial and Black-Scholes option pricing (Ch. 21), the structural credit-risk model (Ch. 21 + 24), and covered-interest parity (Ch. 31) all reduce to applications of value-additivity under no-arbitrage.

  2. NPV is a theorem, not a procedure (Ch. 3, Ch. 7). Once the no-arbitrage axiom and the value-additivity property are established, the NPV rule NPV=PV(benefits)−PV(costs) follows: any project with NPV>0 creates wealth; any project with NPV<0 destroys it. Ch. 7 then proves the IRR rule is equivalent to NPV only when (a) cash flows have a single sign change and (b) there is a single cost of capital — and exhibits explicit counterexamples (multiple IRRs, borrowing-vs-investing reversal, scale and timing differences across mutually exclusive projects). The textbook’s polemical move is to assert that NPV is the only universally correct decision rule.

  3. Risk-adjusted discount rates from CAPM (Chs. 10–13). The discount rate r used in NPV is the opportunity cost of capital — the expected return on a market alternative of equivalent systematic risk. Ch. 10 proves diversifiable risk earns no premium (large-portfolio variance argument); Ch. 11 derives the CAPM equation E[Ri​]=rf​+βi​(E[RMkt​]−rf​),βi​=Var(RMkt​)Cov(Ri​,RMkt​)​, under homogeneous-expectations and frictionless-markets assumptions. Ch. 12 operationalises CAPM into a usable equity cost of capital via OLS regression and unlevering / relevering of beta. Ch. 13 then tests the model — and reports the size, value, momentum anomalies, the Fama-French-Carhart four-factor model as a multifactor extension, and the limits-to-arbitrage caveat that makes EMH a claim about expected returns net of risk premia, not an unconditional efficiency claim.

  4. Capital structure under perfect markets is irrelevant (Ch. 14). MM Proposition I: VL​=VU​ — total firm value is independent of capital structure. Proof: arbitrage via homemade leverage. MM Proposition II: rE​=rU​+(D/E)(rU​−rD​) — equity cost of capital rises linearly with leverage to compensate for the additional risk concentration on the residual claim. The unlevered-cost-of-capital / pretax-WACC remains constant. This is a benchmark result, not an empirical claim — the textbook’s didactic strategy is to derive the perfect-markets benchmark in Ch. 14 and then introduce frictions sequentially in Chs. 15–17.

  5. Frictions make capital structure relevant (Chs. 15–17). Corporate taxes (Ch. 15) yield an interest tax shield τC​×I; PV of tax shield with permanent debt is τC​×D, so VL​=VU​+τC​D. Personal taxes (Miller 1977 offset) reduce the effective debt advantage to τ∗=1−(1−τC​)(1−τe​)/(1−τi​). Distress costs (Ch. 16) bound debt away from 100%: VL​=VU​+τC​D−PV(distress costs), the tradeoff theory. Agency costs of debt (asset substitution, debt overhang, milking) and equity (free-cash-flow over-investment) further constrain optimal leverage. Information asymmetry (Myers-Majluf 1984) yields the pecking order (internal funds → debt → equity) and explains the −1.5 to −3% average price drop on equity-issuance announcements. Payout policy (Ch. 17) is irrelevant in MM-perfect markets, but tax preference, signalling, and clientele effects make it relevant in practice.

  6. Three internally consistent leveraged-valuation methods (Ch. 18). WACC discounts unlevered FCF at the after-tax WACC, assumes constant target leverage. APV discounts FCF at the unlevered rU​ then adds PV of tax shield separately, allowing time-varying leverage. FTE discounts free-cash-flow-to-equity at the cost of equity rE​. All three give the same NPV under consistent leverage assumptions — by the Law of One Price. The capstone case study (Ch. 19) builds an end-to-end DCF model on the Ideko target, mimicking what private-equity associates do.

  7. Options pricing from replication (Chs. 20–22). Put-call parity C+PV(K)=P+S is a Law-of-One-Price application. The binomial model (Cox-Ross-Rubinstein 1979) prices any path-independent option via a replicating portfolio of Δ shares of stock and B in risk-free bonds. As the period length shrinks to zero and the underlying becomes geometric Brownian motion with constant volatility σ, the binomial price converges to the Black-Scholes formula: C=SN(d1​)−PV(K)N(d2​),d1​=σT​ln[S/PV(K)]​+2σT​​,d2​=d1​−σT​. Five inputs (S,K,T,rf​,σ); the stock’s expected return does not appear. Real options (Ch. 22) — to delay, expand, or abandon — are valued via binomial trees adapted to project-specific payoffs.

  8. Long-term and short-term financing institutions (Chs. 23–27). Equity issuance (Ch. 23) — IPO mechanics, ≈15% first-day-underpricing pattern, SEO announcement-effect drops; debt issuance (Ch. 24) — coupon, maturity, covenant taxonomy, ratings; leasing (Ch. 25) lease-vs-buy NPV at after-tax rD​; working-capital management (Ch. 26) — CCC = DIO + DSO − DPO; short-term financial planning (Ch. 27) — matching-principle, refinancing risk, Bear-Stearns-2008 cautionary case.

  9. Special topics as application (Chs. 28–31). M&A valuation (Ch. 28); corporate governance (Ch. 29); risk management (Ch. 30) with the MM-extended view that hedging adds value only via friction-reduction (distress costs, tax convexity, internal-funds preservation); international corporate finance (Ch. 31) — covered interest parity F=S(1+rd​)/(1+rf​) and the equivalence of foreign-currency and domestic-currency NPV under integrated capital markets.

  10. Cumulative claim. A reader who completes the book has the formal apparatus to value any project, choose between any financing alternatives, price any plain-vanilla derivative, build any standard DCF or LBO model, and reason from first principles about M&A, payout, hedging, and international expansion decisions a CFO might face. The textbook’s real claim is that this apparatus reduces — under one axiom plus equilibrium-optimisation — to a coherent body of theory rather than a heap of finance-MBA tricks.

Key concepts

  • Net Present Value (Formal) — Ch. 3.4: NPV=PV(benefits)−PV(costs)=∑t​Ct​/(1+r)t. Derived as a theorem from no-arbitrage / value-additivity, not asserted as a procedure. The single most-load-bearing concept in the textbook and the entire field.
  • Capital Asset Pricing Model (CAPM) — Ch. 11.8: E[Ri​]=rf​+βi​(E[RMkt​]−rf​). Equilibrium consequence of mean-variance-optimal investors holding the market portfolio under homogeneous beliefs and frictionless markets. Tested empirically in Ch. 13; partial empirical success motivates Fama-French-Carhart multifactor extensions.
  • Modigliani-Miller Propositions — Ch. 14: under perfect capital markets, VL​=VU​ (Proposition I) and rE​=rU​+(D/E)(rU​−rD​) (Proposition II). The benchmark theorem of corporate finance; everything else in Part 5 is “what happens when an MM assumption fails.”
  • Weighted Average Cost of Capital (WACC) — Ch. 12 + Ch. 15 + Ch. 18: rwacc​=(E/V)rE​+(D/V)rD​(1−τC​), valid under constant target leverage. The discount rate used in firm-level FCF valuation and in capital-budgeting NPV when project leverage matches firm target.
  • Efficient Market Hypothesis (Formal) — Ch. 13: in equilibrium, expected returns net of risk premia are zero on all publicly available information; informational competition forces this. Three forms (weak, semi-strong, strong); empirical anomalies (size, value, momentum, post-earnings-announcement drift) qualify but do not refute the underlying competitive-equilibrium logic.
  • Black-Scholes Formula — Ch. 21: C=SN(d1​)−PV(K)N(d2​). Continuous-time limit of replicating-portfolio binomial pricing. Five inputs; expected return not an input. The textbook’s clearest demonstration that no-arbitrage replication delivers practitioner-usable formulas without preference assumptions.
  • Pareto Efficiency — referenced as the welfare benchmark; the textbook does not develop it but assumes it from the microeconomics prerequisite (compare Daron Acemoglu - Microeconomics Ch. 7).
  • Risk Arbitrage Liquidity Spiral — implicit in Ch. 13’s limits-to-arbitrage discussion; explicitly developed in Roger Lowenstein - When Genius Failed.
  • Career as Portfolio of Bets — distantly relevant via Ch. 11’s portfolio-optimisation logic applied to human capital.

Key arguments created from this book

  • No-arbitrage as the single organising axiom of corporate finance — the textbook’s distinctive doctrinal claim, candidate for an argument note.
  • NPV as theorem rather than procedure — operationalised in Ch. 3’s derivation; doctrinally distinct from textbooks that present NPV as a formula to memorise.
  • MM perfect-market irrelevance theorem as scientific benchmark in Galileo’s-air-friction sense — the textbook’s explicit analogy (Ch. 14.2 sidebar) for how to use idealised theorems in applied work.

Best chapters/pages to actually read

For a researcher / practitioner returning to corporate finance, the highest-leverage chapters are:

  • Chapter 3 (pp. 99–134) — the Law of One Price, value-additivity, NPV-as-theorem. ~35 pp; read in one sitting; sets the conceptual structure for everything that follows. Skipping this chapter to “get to the formulas” is the single most common mistake; the formulas don’t make sense without it.
  • Chapter 7 (pp. 248–274) — Investment Decision Rules. NPV vs IRR vs payback vs profitability index, with explicit IRR-failure counterexamples. Highest-leverage for any capital-budgeting interview question.
  • Chapter 9 (pp. 313–355) — Valuing Stocks. DDM, Gordon Growth, total-payout model, FCF model, multiples. Highest-leverage chapter for equity-research and stock-pitch contexts.
  • Chapter 10 + 11 (pp. 356–444) — Capital Markets, Pricing of Risk, and CAPM. The diversification argument (large-portfolio-variance proof) and the CAPM derivation. Read together; this is the asset-pricing core.
  • Chapter 12 (pp. 445–482) — Estimating the Cost of Capital. The chapter that turns CAPM theory into a usable WACC. Highest-leverage for valuation work in any role.
  • Chapter 14 (pp. 526–554) — MM I and II. Read with Ch. 15’s tax-shield extension; this is the capital-structure core.
  • Chapter 16 (pp. 589–634) — Financial Distress, Managerial Incentives, Information. The chapter that develops the frictions — distress costs, agency costs, asymmetric information, pecking order — that drive observed capital structures. Highest-leverage for credit-research and PE/LBO contexts.
  • Chapter 18 (pp. 678–728) — Capital Budgeting and Valuation with Leverage. WACC, APV, FTE equivalence. Read with Ch. 19’s case study; this is the leveraged-valuation core.
  • Chapter 21 (pp. 795–830) — Option Valuation. Binomial replicating portfolio, Black-Scholes formula, risk-neutral probabilities, beta of risky debt. Highest-leverage chapter for any derivatives, structured-products, or convertible-bond context.
  • Skip on first pass: Ch. 1’s Dartmouth-College historical introduction, Ch. 2’s accounting refresher (only needed if you don’t already read 10-Ks fluently), Ch. 19’s case-study spreadsheet mechanics (read the method, skip the line-by-line spreadsheet unless building the model yourself), Ch. 25 leasing (skim only for cross-border lease-vs-buy questions), Ch. 27 short-term planning (operating-treasury rather than strategic-finance content).

Important examples / cases

  • HomeNet running example (Chs. 8, 18, 19) — fictitious Cisco home-networking project used to teach FCF construction, NPV with leverage, and end-to-end DCF modelling. The textbook’s most extended applied example.
  • Apple, Microsoft, Amazon, Tesla beta data (Ch. 10, Table 10.6) — 2017–2022 betas computed from weekly returns; Walmart 0.41, Coca-Cola 0.83, Microsoft 0.93, Apple 1.06, AMD / Tesla / Avis substantially above 1. The empirical anchor for the systematic-risk discussion.
  • Anheuser-Busch / InBev acquisition (Ch. 28 opening) — 2008 $70-per-share-cash deal used to motivate M&A valuation mechanics.
  • Ideko target case study (Ch. 19) — full PE-buyout-style DCF, including beta unlever / relever, terminal-value handling, exit-multiple sensitivity, equity-IRR computation. The chapter mimics the work product of a private-equity associate.
  • Argentina 2001 sovereign default (Ch. 6) — used to teach the gap between yield-to-maturity and expected return when default probability is non-zero.
  • Walmart vs Hormel beta contrast (Ch. 10) — non-cyclical packaged-food beta 0.16 vs Walmart superstore 0.41; teaches that beta reflects business-cycle sensitivity.
  • Myron Scholes interview (Ch. 21) — Scholes’ own retrospective on the Black-Scholes derivation, the journal-rejection history, and the 2007–2009-crisis criticism. Pairs naturally with Roger Lowenstein - When Genius Failed as the LTCM-failure case study.
  • Google 2004 Dutch-auction IPO (Ch. 23) — used to contrast book-building vs auction IPO mechanisms and to discuss whether ≈15% first-day-underpricing can be reduced by mechanism design.
  • Sarbanes-Oxley 2002 / Dodd-Frank 2010 regulatory case (Ch. 29) — used to teach how governance rules become costly responses to scandal-revealed agency-cost realisations.

Critiques and limitations

The textbook has the characteristic limitations of any market-leading MBA-grade corporate-finance textbook in its 6th edition (2023 vintage), plus a few specific to its theory-first authorial perspective:

  1. CAPM is taught as if it were correct, then the problems are flagged in Ch. 13. A student reading sequentially internalises CAPM-as-the-discount-rate before being told it has known empirical failures (size, value, momentum, post-earnings-announcement drift). The Ch. 13 caveats do not affect Chs. 12, 18, 19, 22 calculations, which all silently assume CAPM. A more honest pedagogy might foreground multifactor models earlier; the textbook’s choice tracks practitioner reality (CAPM is still what most CFOs use) but understates the empirical case for alternatives.
  2. Behavioral finance is appendix-like. Ch. 13’s behavioral content — disposition effect, overconfidence, herding, limits to arbitrage — is presented as exception material, not as theory. A student who reads only Chs. 1–12 and 14–22 finishes the book without much exposure to the post-1990s behavioral revolution. Compare Daron Acemoglu - Microeconomics’s deeper integration of behavioral material.
  3. Black-Scholes is taught with constant-volatility / no-jump / lognormal-returns assumptions barely flagged. Ch. 21 mentions these are simplifications but does not develop volatility smiles, stochastic volatility (Heston), jump diffusion (Merton 1976), or local-volatility models — all of which are in active practitioner use post-1987-crash. The 2007-2009-crisis Scholes-interview gestures at this but the chapter’s worked examples are all constant-vol Black-Scholes. A reader encountering Roger Lowenstein - When Genius Failed’s LTCM case learns why this matters in a way the textbook does not foreground.
  4. Agency theory is compressed and U.S.-specific. Ch. 16’s treatment of agency costs is consistent but does not develop the full Tirole / Holmström-Tirole / Aghion-Bolton machinery, and Ch. 29’s governance examples are predominantly U.S. (with brief gestures at Continental-European stakeholder models, German two-tier boards, Japanese keiretsu). A reader studying Angela Huyue Zhang - High Wire or Meg E. Rithmire - Land Bargains and Chinese Capitalism needs to import most China-specific governance / capital-structure content from elsewhere.
  5. The role of intermediaries is under-developed. Banks, broker-dealers, insurance companies, money-market funds, exchanges, clearing houses — the institutional plumbing that the 2008 GFC stressed — appear only in scattered examples. The book’s frictionless-markets default downplays the welfare-economic significance of intermediation, and the post-GFC literature (Gorton, Adrian, Brunnermeier, Pedersen) is barely cited.
  6. No serious treatment of dynamic security design or contracting. Despite DeMarzo’s research being on dynamic security design, the textbook stays at the static / single-period / two-period level. Readers wanting Tirole’s Theory of Corporate Finance depth must go to a separate graduate text.
  7. Information-economics treatment is shallow relative to its importance. Pecking order (Myers-Majluf) and free-cash-flow problem (Jensen) appear in Ch. 16 but are not rigorously derived; the asymmetric-information apparatus from Daron Acemoglu - Microeconomics Ch. 16 is assumed but not redeveloped. A student finishing the textbook can apply pecking-order intuition but cannot prove it.
  8. Network effects, two-sided markets, and platform-economy capital structures are absent. The 2023 economy is dominated by intangible-heavy, platform-driven firms whose capital-structure logic differs from the textbook’s manufacturing-/retail-vintage examples. A reader of Andrew Chen - The Cold Start Problem would find this striking.
  9. Crypto and digital-asset finance are essentially absent. The 6th edition was published before mainstream adoption of stablecoin, DeFi, and tokenised-asset instruments. A 2030 edition will need a major addition; a reader with interest in blockchain-financial-economics must supplement extensively.
  10. AI / labor / automation interactions with capital-budgeting and capital-structure are absent. The Acemoglu-Restrepo-Johnson 2018-2024 literature on AI and labor displacement is not cited; no Ch. 8 example treats AI capex differently from manufacturing capex; no Ch. 16 example treats high-skill-labor-replacement risk as a distress factor. A student studying Sebastian Mallaby - The Infinity Machine alongside this textbook must impute these connections.
  11. The textbook is U.S.-centric in its tax-code, governance, and currency examples. The Global Edition retrofits non-U.S. case studies but the structural treatment (e.g. Ch. 15’s interest-tax-shield calculations, Ch. 23’s IPO mechanics, Ch. 29’s SOX compliance) remains U.S.-anchored. A non-U.S. CFO must mentally re-translate.
  12. Two-author voice but largely seamless. Unlike a multi-author committee textbook (compare Daron Acemoglu - Microeconomics), Berk-DeMarzo’s joint voice is unified; there is no observable seam between Berk’s asset-pricing-equilibrium framing and DeMarzo’s contracting-and-capital-structure framing. This is editorially admirable but means the reader does not see the disagreements between asset-pricing and corporate-finance theory traditions that exist in the research literature.

Links to other books

  • Foundational microeconomic prerequisite: Daron Acemoglu - Microeconomics — Acemoglu et al supply the price-theory, mean-variance utility, and information-economics machinery (adverse selection, moral hazard, signalling, principal-agent) that Berk-DeMarzo’s Chs. 11, 13, 16 take as given. Reading them sequentially: Acemoglu Ch. 16 develops adverse selection / signalling / moral hazard rigorously; Berk-DeMarzo Ch. 16 applies the same apparatus to capital-structure choices without re-deriving it. The pairing is the canonical price-theory + corporate-finance diptych in U.S. business-school instruction.

  • Empirical-philosophy contrast with George Soros - The Alchemy of Finance — Soros’s reflexivity argument explicitly attacks the rational-actor / efficient-markets / no-arbitrage baseline that Berk-DeMarzo treat as foundational. Reading them together is productive: Berk-DeMarzo is the canonical mainstream against which Soros’s Reflexivity is in dialogue. Soros would say that boom-bust feedback loops in capital markets — the kind he profited from — make the textbook’s clean Ch. 14 MM analysis miss the dynamics that actually produce the financial-crisis episodes Ch. 16 / Ch. 23 / Ch. 30 cite as cautionary tales.

  • LTCM as Black-Scholes-Merton failure case in Roger Lowenstein - When Genius Failed — Lowenstein’s narrative of Long-Term Capital Management’s 1998 collapse is the single most important real-world case study of the Ch. 21 framework breaking under stress (LTCM’s principals included Robert Merton and Myron Scholes themselves). The Berk-DeMarzo textbook teaches Black-Scholes as a clean replication argument; Lowenstein documents the constant-volatility / no-jump / liquidity-frictionless assumptions failing simultaneously in August-September 1998. Read together, the pair gives both the formal apparatus and the cautionary case.

  • Venture-capital / private-equity application in Sebastian Mallaby - The Power Law — Mallaby’s history of venture capital is direct applied corporate finance using Ch. 16’s principal-agent and signalling tools (LP-as-principal, GP-as-agent; track-record-as-signal; carry-as-skin-in-the-game), Ch. 21’s option machinery (VC investments are call options on follow-on rounds), and Ch. 23’s IPO mechanics (the exit channel for VC-backed firms). Berk-DeMarzo gives Mallaby’s vocabulary; Mallaby gives Berk-DeMarzo’s institutional-finance chapters their richest current case studies.

  • Chip-industry capital-budgeting / capex application in Chris Miller - Chip War — Miller’s history of the semiconductor industry is a sustained applied case for the textbook’s capital-budgeting (Ch. 8), real-options (Ch. 22), and risk-management (Ch. 30) machinery: TSMC’s fab-construction decisions are real-options problems with timing flexibility; foundry-vs-IDM strategic choices are corporate-structure questions; export-control geopolitical risk is a Ch. 30 / Ch. 31 cross-currency / country-risk problem. The textbook gives the toolkit; Miller gives the running case.

  • Information-economics application in Joseph E. Stiglitz - Globalization and Its Discontents Revisited — Stiglitz’s IMF-conditionality critique builds on his Nobel-winning information-economics work, the same apparatus that underlies Berk-DeMarzo Ch. 16. Reading the two together: Berk-DeMarzo supplies the formal tools (asymmetric information, adverse selection, moral hazard); Stiglitz applies them at country-level institutional scale (capital-account-liberalisation as adverse-selection trap; conditionality as principal-agent contracting failure).

  • Methodological complement in Abhijit V. Banerjee - Poor Economics — Banerjee and Duflo’s RCT-driven development-economics methodology is the empirical counterpart to Berk-DeMarzo’s theory-driven corporate-finance methodology. Both belong to the post-1990s social-science empirical-revolution but operate in different domains. Reading both helps a researcher see what RCT-style identification would look like applied to corporate-finance questions (microfinance loan structure, capital-structure-experiment in small firms) — work that Banerjee and his Poverty Action Lab collaborators have actually done.

  • Capital-structure / capex applied to platforms in Andrew Chen - The Cold Start Problem — Chen’s network-effects framework is implicit in Berk-DeMarzo’s discussions of platform IPO timing (Ch. 23) and risk-management for two-sided-market firms (Ch. 30) but never named as such. Reading both fills the textbook’s most striking topical gap; Chen’s “atomic network” / “hard side” framework slots onto Ch. 8 capex-decision frameworks in a way that distinguishes platform-firm capital-budgeting from manufacturing-firm capital-budgeting.

  • Note on companion / related textbooks not currently in this vault: Brealey-Myers-Allen Principles of Corporate Finance (12e), Ross-Westerfield-Jaffe Corporate Finance (13e), Tirole The Theory of Corporate Finance (graduate-level theoretical companion), Cochrane Asset Pricing (graduate asset-pricing companion), Hull Options, Futures, and Other Derivatives (graduate derivatives companion), Damodaran Investment Valuation (practitioner-focused companion), and Berk-DeMarzo’s own Fundamentals of Corporate Finance (the lighter undergraduate twin). Cross-link to them only after they are ingested.

Open questions

  • Is no-arbitrage really the right organising principle for corporate finance, or is it a Berk-DeMarzo authorial idiosyncrasy? Brealey-Myers-Allen organise around NPV-then-CAPM-then-MM as separable modules; Berk-DeMarzo’s chain is more elegant but more rigid. Whether this difference matters pedagogically (vs cosmetically) is an open question.
  • How should a 2030 edition incorporate stochastic volatility, jumps, and crypto-asset pricing? The 6th edition’s Black-Scholes treatment is constant-vol; future editions need to either acknowledge the empirical failures in body text or add a chapter on volatility-smile pricing.
  • Does the WACC / APV / FTE equivalence hold under more exotic leverage policies? The textbook’s equivalence proof assumes either (i) constant target leverage (WACC) or (ii) predetermined debt schedule (APV) or (iii) flow-to-equity discounting at rE​ (FTE). Real-world dynamic capital-structure policies (Hennessy-Whited, DeMarzo-Sannikov dynamic-trade-off) require modifications.
  • Should the textbook foreground the limits-to-arbitrage literature (Shleifer-Vishny, Gromb-Vayanos, Brunnermeier-Pedersen)? Currently this material is squeezed into Ch. 13 caveats. Given that the entire textbook builds on no-arbitrage, the failure of arbitrage in stress periods deserves more than a footnote.
  • What is the right relationship between corporate finance and labor economics, AI economics, and climate economics? Acemoglu’s recent research argues these are deeply linked; Berk-DeMarzo treat them as exogenous. A future edition could integrate; current edition does not.

Research uses

This textbook serves five distinct research-prep functions for a reader with my (Yang’s) profile:

  • Quant-finance / investment-banking interview prep. Chs. 3, 7, 9, 10, 11, 12, 14, 15, 16, 18, 21 cover the corporate-finance fundamentals that appear in interview question banks: NPV / IRR mechanics, DCF construction, CAPM beta and cost of capital, MM propositions, WACC / APV / FTE distinctions, Black-Scholes inputs and Greeks. Useful as a structured interview-prep map; pair with Sebastian Mallaby - The Power Law for VC-style case studies and Roger Lowenstein - When Genius Failed for derivatives-failure cases.

  • Blockchain-economics and regulatory research. Ch. 14’s MM machinery applied to stablecoin reserve structure (debt-vs-equity-like claims on a reserve pool), Ch. 16’s information-asymmetry / agency-cost framework applied to DeFi protocol governance (token-as-equity, validator-as-agent), Ch. 21’s option-pricing applied to perpetual futures and structured DeFi products, and Ch. 28’s M&A / Ch. 30’s risk-management applied to crypto-asset-treasury management at corporates. The textbook supplies the analytical vocabulary; the crypto-specific institutional features are an extension.

  • AI-policy and platform-economics research. Ch. 8’s capex frameworks adapted for AI compute investment, Ch. 22’s real-options machinery applied to model-training-then-deploy decisions, Ch. 30’s risk-management framework adapted for catastrophic-AI-risk hedging (where standard derivatives don’t exist). Pair with Sebastian Mallaby - The Infinity Machine for the AI-firm-history case, Andrew Chen - The Cold Start Problem for platform-economics extension.

  • China political-economy modelling. Ch. 16’s agency-cost machinery applied to state-owned-enterprise capital-structure (multiple principals: party-state, controlling shareholder, public investors), Ch. 23’s IPO mechanics adapted to the Hong Kong / Shanghai / Shenzhen exchanges, Ch. 31’s international corporate finance applied to RMB-USD covered-interest-parity arbitrage and capital-control-driven segmentation. Pair with Angela Huyue Zhang - High Wire (regulatory-state interaction with corporate finance) and Meg E. Rithmire - Land Bargains and Chinese Capitalism (land-finance / local-government-debt as a corporate-finance problem).

  • Lobbying-network / corporate-political-strategy research. Ch. 28’s M&A material on antitrust review (Microsoft, AT&T, Pfizer-Wyeth cases) and Ch. 29’s regulatory-regime material (SOX, Dodd-Frank, post-FTX legislative responses) are direct corporate-political-strategy content. Cross-link to In Song Kim’s lobbying-network research on industry-coalition formation around regulatory change, with the textbook supplying the firm-level cost-benefit-of-political-action analytical apparatus.

What I would miss if I only read this note

A graduate-grade corporate-finance textbook is largely worked examples, end-of-chapter problems, and Excel-based modelling, not propositions — and this note can replicate none of these. Specifically, I would miss: (a) the cumulative reps from end-of-chapter problems that build automatic reflexes for NPV calculation under non-trivial cash-flow patterns, IRR pitfalls in mutually exclusive projects, beta unlevering / relevering algebra, MM-Proposition-II equity-cost adjustments, WACC / APV / FTE consistency-checks, binomial-tree backward-induction, and Black-Scholes input-sensitivity — fluency that note-reading alone does not produce; (b) the Ideko case-study spreadsheet build (Ch. 19) that turns the abstract Parts 1–5 machinery into an actual deliverable PE-style DCF model with sensitivity tables, exit-multiple analysis, and equity-IRR computation — this is what private-equity associates, investment-banking analysts, and corporate-development professionals actually build, and the textbook’s pedagogy is engineered around the student building it themselves; (c) the MyLab Finance adaptive-difficulty problem-set environment, which is how the print/PDF reader actually internalises the formulas — the textbook expects the student to use this; (d) the CFO and practitioner interview boxes scattered throughout (David Viniar at Goldman on bank-treasury risk; Sidney Hindle of Hindle Power on emerging-market investing; Myron Scholes’ own retrospective in Ch. 21 on the Black-Scholes derivation and the 2007–2009-crisis criticism) — these are voice-of-practitioner moments that humanise the propositions and that bare summary erases entirely; (e) the diagrams and visual pedagogy — efficient-frontier curves with the tangency portfolio at T, capital-market-line and security-market-line graphs, NPV-profile and IRR-where-NPV-crosses-zero charts, Black-Scholes N(d1​) shaded normal-distribution illustrations, binomial-tree branching diagrams, MM-Proposition-II linear plot of rE​ vs D/E, tradeoff-theory VL​ curve with tax-shield-rising / distress-cost-falling crossover — which is how the field actually communicates and which note prose flattens; (f) the glossary ([source extract], ~120K chars) with its hundreds of canonical term definitions, which is itself a curated map of the field’s vocabulary; (g) the footnote-and-source-trail to the original research-frontier papers (Modigliani-Miller 1958, Black-Scholes 1973, Merton 1973-1974, Myers-Majluf 1984, Jensen 1986, Fama-French 1992, Cox-Ross-Rubinstein 1979) that lets a curious reader go from textbook example to source paper to current research literature; (h) the Sixth Edition Global content — additional non-U.S. case studies (LVMH, Samsung, Vale, ICBC), OECD-equivalent cross-references for U.S. tax numbers, multi-currency end-of-chapter problems — that the U.S.-edition-only summary would miss; and (i) the detailed contents ([source extract], ~43K chars) of every section heading and sub-heading across 31 chapters, which is a finer-grained map than the chapter-by-chapter table above and which lets a reader looking for a specific topic (e.g. “rights offerings vs cash offerings”, “Black-Scholes with continuous dividends”, “covered interest rate parity”) jump directly to the relevant pages.
