Adnan Aziz - Elements of Programming Interviews

One-sentence thesis

Aziz, Lee and Prakash argue that algorithmic-interview competence is a recognition reflex trained by drilling problems organized around a small, named set of patterns (data-structure, algorithm-design, abstract-analysis, system-design) — not a body of memorized solutions — and they back the claim with a 17-chapter problem catalog whose internal structure forces the reader to encounter each pattern five to twenty times in different costumes until the underlying invariant becomes visible at first glance.

Why this book matters

EPI is the harder sibling of McDowell’s Cracking and the difference matters: McDowell teaches how to interview (behavioral, soft skills, the 150-problem starter set with prose preambles), while Aziz teaches how to recognize the pattern under the costume (300+ problems with terse statements, hint-only Part III, and a Part IV that walks the brute-force-to-optimum derivation explicitly). For candidates targeting the algorithmic bar at Google, Meta, Jane Street, Citadel, or research-engineer roles, the McDowell-only path tops out around mid-level competence; the EPI path is what produces senior-level recognition speed. The two books are complements, not substitutes — McDowell first to internalize the interview as an event, EPI second to internalize the pattern catalog as a working vocabulary.

The book also matters as a taxonomy artifact. Chapter 4’s four pattern tables — data-structure, algorithm-design, abstract-analysis, system-design — are the cleanest taxonomy of algorithmic problem types in the interview-prep literature. A reader who masters those four tables has a search index against which any new problem statement can be queried: “which entry in Table 4.1 names the right structure? which entry in Table 4.2 names the right technique? which entry in Table 4.3 names the right analytical move?” That habit transfers directly to production work — code review, design review, and on-call debugging all benefit from “name the pattern first, derive the solution second.”

The third reason EPI matters is the variants discipline. Each solution in Part IV ends with 2–5 variants (“what if duplicates are allowed? what if the array is rotated? what if k is unbounded?”) that force the reader to re-derive the invariant under perturbed preconditions. This is exactly the Dijkstra-style derivation applied at interview scale, and it is the reason EPI graduates outperform LeetCode-grinding peers under stress: their preparation builds the derivation reflex, not just the recall reflex.

Core argument

Aziz’s argument is structurally simple and pedagogically aggressive. The book stakes five claims and demonstrates each with hundreds of pages of problem-solution evidence:

  1. Patterns over problems. Interview problems look different but reduce to a small set of named patterns. Chapter 4’s four tables enumerate them: data structures (primitive, array, string, list, stack/queue, tree, heap, hash, BST), algorithm techniques (sort, recursion, divide-and-conquer, dynamic programming, greedy, invariants), abstract analyses (concrete examples, case analysis, iterative refinement, reduction, graph modeling), and system design moves (caching, parallelism, indexing, randomization). Mastery of the patterns is mastery of the problems.
  2. Recognition is a muscle. Pattern recognition is not innate; it is built by encountering each pattern five to twenty times in different problem costumes. Part II’s 17 chapters are designed so that every chapter re-uses every previously-introduced pattern under a new data-structure constraint. By chapter 18 (greedy + invariants), the reader has seen invariants in chapters 4, 6, 8, 12, 13, 14, 15, 16, 17 — and the recognition reflex is built by sheer repetition under varied costumes.
  3. Brute force first, then refine. Every Part IV solution starts with the brute-force algorithm, names its complexity, then walks through the optimization derivation step by step. This is the iterative refinement abstract-analysis pattern made into a book-wide pedagogical convention. The reader who reads only the optimum solution learns the algorithm but loses the derivation path that produced it; the reader who attempts the brute force first, then reads the refinement, builds the path-finding reflex.
  4. Hints are pattern-naming, not solving. Part III gives one-line hints, deliberately calibrated to name the pattern without giving the answer (“find an invariant,” “use a sweep,” “sort first”). The hint should reduce the search space by naming which pattern-table entry applies, leaving the reader to do the derivation. This three-stage scaffolding — problem in Part II, pattern-name in Part III, derivation in Part IV — is the book’s pedagogical engine.
  5. Variants force re-derivation. Each Part IV solution closes with 2–5 perturbed-precondition variants. The variants ensure that the reader cannot pass by memorizing the headline solution; the derivation must survive perturbation, which means the reader must have understood the invariant, not just the code. This is the closest the interview-prep literature comes to [[Edsger W. Dijkstra - A Discipline of Programming|Dijkstra’s wp(S, R) derivations]] applied to whiteboard-scale problems.

The cumulative claim: an EPI graduate has recognition reflexes, not memorized solutions. When a Citadel quant-engineer interviewer presents a never-before-seen problem (“given a stream of stock-quote ticks, design a data structure that supports update(symbol, price) in O(log n) and top_k_by_volatility(k) in O(k log n)…”), the EPI graduate’s first move is not to search memory for “have I seen this exact problem?” but to query the pattern tables: “this is a streaming + top-k pattern, so heap + augmented-BST or Bloom-filter + sorted-set; the volatility metric implies a rolling-window invariant, which means we need Amortized Analysis for the update path.” That query loop is the deliverable.

Argument map

EPI’s pedagogical scaffolding flows top-down from the meta-claim (recognition is the deliverable) through the four pattern tables (the recognition vocabulary) into the three-stage drill (problem → hint → solution) that builds the reflex.

Algorithmic interview = recognition under time pressure
  └── recognition is a muscle, not a memory
        └── built by drilling 300+ problems organized by pattern (Part II)
              └── patterns enumerated in 4 tables (ch. 4)
                    ├── data structures (Table 4.1)
                    ├── algorithm techniques (Table 4.2)
                    ├── abstract analyses (Table 4.3)
                    └── system design (Table 4.5)
              └── each pattern re-encountered in many costumes (chs. 5–21)
        └── three-stage scaffolding (Parts II / III / IV)
              ├── Part II: terse problem statements
              ├── Part III: pattern-naming hints (one line each)
              └── Part IV: brute-force → refine → variants
                    └── variants force re-derivation under perturbation
        └── result: candidate sees pattern at first glance
              └── transfers to production code review, design, on-call

Key concepts

  • Algorithmic Pattern Recognition — the central pedagogical claim of the book; the four pattern tables are its operational form.
  • Time-Space Complexity Trade-off — every Part IV solution presents at least two algorithms with different (time, space) profiles and asks the reader to choose; the parity worked example moves O(n) → O(s) → O(1)-with-table.
  • Asymptotic Analysis — the lingua franca for comparing brute-force and optimum solutions; chapter 4’s complexity primer is the prerequisite for every Part IV refinement step.
  • Amortized Analysis — used in chapter 6 (dynamic-array doubling) and chapter 11 (heap operations), and in many Part IV variants where a single expensive operation is followed by many cheap ones.
  • Abstract Data Type — chapter 4’s data-structure pattern table is implicitly an ADT taxonomy; the heap-as-priority-queue and BST-as-sorted-map mappings are the load-bearing examples.
  • Invariant-preserving local repair — the cross-chapter discipline that ties together chapter 18 (greedy + invariants) with the rotations of chapter 15 (BSTs) and the sift operations of chapter 11 (heaps).
  • Brute-force-first iterative refinement — chapter 4’s abstract-analysis pattern, used as the structural template for every Part IV solution.

Best chapters/pages to actually read

The book is too long for a linear read; it is reference + drill material. Read in this order:

  • Chapter 4 (Problem Solving Patterns) — the load-bearing chapter. Read it twice. After the second read, all four pattern tables (4.1, 4.2, 4.3, 4.5) should be loaded into working memory. Without this, Part II is opaque; with it, Part II becomes a guided drill.
  • Chapters 1–3 (lifecycle, candidate playbook, interviewer playbook) — read once, before the first interview. The interviewer-playbook chapter (ch. 3) is the highest-leverage chapter for candidates because it explains what signal interviewers are calibrating against. Specifically, ch. 3’s “no single point of failure / multiple solutions / multi-area coverage” criteria for problem selection tell the candidate which kind of dialogue to expect.
  • Chapter 6 (Arrays) — start here for Part II. Five problems including Dutch national flag (problem 6.1) and max-difference (problem 6.2) cover invariants, two-pointer technique, and amortized resizing. The single highest-yield chapter per page.
  • Chapter 11 (Heaps) — the highest-yield chapter for systems-engineering candidates. The k-merge sorted streams problem and online-median problem are production algorithms that appear in stream processors, log aggregators, and quant-research signal pipelines.
  • Chapter 12 (Searching) — binary search and its variants. The first/last-occurrence variant and the search-rotated-array variant are the two most asked binary-search interview problems in the FAANG / quant ecosystem; mastering them is high-yield.
  • Chapter 17 (Dynamic Programming) — the chapter most candidates fail. Score-combinations, Levenshtein, knapsack, longest-non-decreasing-subsequence are the four canonical DP problems; if these four are reflexive, ~80% of DP interview problems become tractable.
  • Chapter 18 (Greedy + Invariants) — the chapter whose pattern recurs most across other chapters. The 2-sum / 3-sum and longest-contiguous-sequence problems exemplify the invariant-propagation discipline that ties the book’s pedagogy together.
  • Chapter 21 (Design Problems) — system-design questions for senior candidates. TinyURL, online advertising, distributed throttling. Pair with John Ousterhout - A Philosophy of Software Design for the API-design dimension that EPI’s design problems touch but do not deeply develop.

Skip-and-return strategy: chapters 7 (strings), 8 (linked lists), 9 (stacks/queues) are necessary but boring — the problems are mostly index-juggling drills. Do them when stamina allows. Chapter 20 (parallel computing) is shallow relative to a real systems text and can be deferred unless the target role explicitly tests concurrency.

Important examples / cases

The parity problem (5.1) is the book’s signature worked example because it traverses three points on the Time-Space Complexity Trade-off curve in a single page: O(n) brute-force bit-iteration → O(s) using x & (x-1) to clear lowest set bit → O(1) per query with a 65,536-entry precomputed lookup table. The progression embodies the iterative-refinement abstract-analysis pattern, and the reader who works through it once has internalized the move “if the same operation runs many times, precompute a table” — a heuristic that recurs in chapters 13 (hash tables), 17 (DP memoization), and 18 (rolling-window aggregates).

The Dutch national flag problem (6.1) is the book’s signature invariant example: maintain three regions in the array (less-than, equal, greater-than the pivot) and grow them by single-element moves, each preserving the three-region invariant. The same shape recurs in quicksort partitioning, in three-way partitioning for k-th-element selection, and in the radix-sort variants of chapter 14. A reader who has internalized DNF can derive a half-dozen partitioning algorithms from first principles without consulting reference material.

The 2-sum invariant (chapter 4 + chapter 18) exemplifies the converging-pointer invariant that subsumes a large family of array problems: i starts at 0, j starts at n-1, the invariant says “no i’ < i and no j’ > j can be part of an optimal solution,” and each step either succeeds or shrinks the search space. The reader who masters 2-sum derives 3-sum, k-sum, longest-substring-without-repeats, and container-with-most-water as variants.

What I would miss if I only read this note

I would miss the physical density of the problem catalog. EPI Part II is 100KB of problem statements; the experience of facing problem after problem and feeling pattern-recognition fatigue is itself a load-bearing piece of the pedagogy. A note flattens the 300+ problems into a chapter table; the actual book makes the reader feel the volume, which is what produces stamina under time pressure.

I would miss the variants discipline. Each Part IV solution closes with 2–5 perturbed-precondition mutations that the note cannot reproduce except by example. The variants are how EPI separates “memorized the algorithm” from “internalized the invariant” — and the difference is precisely what FAANG-level interviewers calibrate for.

I would miss the language-fluency layer. EPI’s Part IV solutions are presented in C++ with optional Java/Python sketches. The C++11 preamble in Part IV teaches the language idioms (smart pointers, lambdas, range-based for, emplace) that show up across solutions. A candidate who only reads paraphrased pseudocode loses the practice in production-grade language fluency.

I would miss the interleaving of patterns across chapters. Every chapter in Part II reuses every previously-introduced pattern. By chapter 18, the reader has seen invariants in chapters 4, 6, 8, 12, 13, 14, 15, 16, 17 — and the recognition reflex is built by sheer repetition under varied costumes. A summary picks one pass; the book picks twenty.

I would also miss the failed attempts. EPI’s solutions deliberately walk through wrong attempts before the right one — the white/black-cities greedy that fails before the sorted-pairing greedy that works (chapter 4); the naive recursive DP that times out before the memoized version (chapter 17); the brute-force quadratic that motivates the invariant-driven linear (chapter 18). The note can only summarize the right answer; the book makes the reader feel why the wrong answer was wrong, which is what produces the recognition reflex.

Critiques and limitations

EPI is a hammer; not every problem is a nail. The book’s pattern-table framing is exactly right for FAANG-style algorithmic interviews and exactly wrong for several adjacent contexts. Distributed-systems interviews (the kind run by infrastructure teams at Google Cloud, AWS, Stripe) ask for failure-mode reasoning and consistency-model tradeoffs that EPI does not develop; pair with John Ousterhout - A Philosophy of Software Design and a real distributed-systems text. ML / research-engineer interviews emphasize numerical-stability reasoning, gradient mechanics, and tensor-shape discipline that EPI explicitly disavows; pair with a deep-learning text. Quant interviews mix algorithmic problems with stochastic-process and PnL-attribution questions; EPI covers the algorithmic half well and is useless for the rest.

The C++ assumption ages the book. EPI’s Part IV C++11 code is excellent in 2014; in 2026 it reads as idiomatic but dated (no concepts, no ranges, no coroutines, no std::span, no optional use). Candidates targeting modern C++ shops should cross-check against current cppreference.com idioms; candidates targeting Rust, Go, or modern Python need to translate idioms back into their language’s memory model and the cost story will sometimes change (a “linked list” in Rust, with ownership and borrowing, has different operational costs than EPI’s C++ implementation).

Concurrency chapter is shallow. Chapter 20 (parallel computing) is the weakest chapter in the book. It treats concurrency as a problem-pattern catalog (singleton, dining philosophers, readers-writers) rather than as a domain with its own cost model — there is no amortized analysis under contention, no discussion of lock-free data structures, no engagement with the C++/Java memory models. A 2026 candidate for any infrastructure role needs to supplement aggressively.

No engagement with cache locality or hardware reality. EPI’s complexity claims are pure-asymptotic; the constant factors that determine production performance (cache hits, branch prediction, NUMA effects, memory hierarchy) are not engaged. This is the gap that Bryant & O’Hallaron’s CSAPP fills, and the two books should be read in tandem.

Pedagogy assumes self-discipline that most readers lack. The three-stage scaffolding (Part II problem → Part III hint → Part IV solution) only works if the reader resists the temptation to skip from Part II directly to Part IV. Most readers don’t, which means most readers extract a fraction of the book’s pedagogical value. EPI’s authors do not mitigate this; an LLM-tutor wrapper that enforces the discipline would be a higher-leverage product than any printed edition.

Comparison gap with Cracking is rarely surfaced. EPI’s authors do not directly engage with McDowell’s Cracking the Coding Interview, even though the two books share 60% of their problem space. A reader buying both will have to discover the complementarity (McDowell = behavioral + first 150 problems; EPI = pattern recognition + 300 problems) on their own, which is a missed pedagogical opportunity given how often the two are recommended in the same breath.

Links to other books

  • Sibling — direct comparison — Gayle Laakmann McDowell - Cracking the Coding Interview — McDowell teaches the interview as event (behavioral, lifecycle, first 150 problems with prose preambles); Aziz teaches the pattern catalog as recognition vocabulary (300+ problems with terse statements, three-stage scaffolding, variants discipline). Read McDowell first to internalize interview mechanics, then EPI to deepen algorithmic recognition. Treating them as substitutes is the most common mistake interview candidates make.
  • Data-structure prerequisite — Marcello La Rocca - Grokking Data Structures — La Rocca teaches the Abstract Data Type / data-structure / cost-model triad that EPI’s chapter 4 Table 4.1 assumes. A reader who finds chapter 4 dense should retreat to La Rocca’s chapters 7–13 (ADT through graphs), then return to EPI with the visual intuition built.
  • Systems grounding — Randal E. Bryant and David O’Hallaron - Computer Systems A Programmer’s Perspective — fills the constant-factor gap that EPI’s pure-asymptotic treatment leaves open. Read alongside EPI Part IV: the asymptotic story is necessary but not sufficient for production performance, and CSAPP supplies the cache, branch-prediction, and memory-hierarchy machinery that determine real wall-clock time.
  • API and module design — John Ousterhout - A Philosophy of Software Design — EPI chapter 21 (design problems) is shallow; Ousterhout’s deep modules and complexity-management discipline supplies the design vocabulary that EPI gestures toward but does not develop. Pair for system-design interview prep at staff level.
  • Implementation depth — Robert Nystrom - Crafting Interpreters — every data structure EPI catalogs (hash tables, BSTs, dynamic arrays, linked lists) appears in jlox or clox in working production-grade code. Reading EPI’s chapter 13 (hash tables) alongside Nystrom’s hash-table chapter gives the reader both the interview-shaped and the production-shaped mental model of the same structure.
  • Formal-derivation counterpoint — Edsger W. Dijkstra - A Discipline of Programming — EPI’s variants discipline (re-derive under perturbed precondition) is the visual-derivation analogue of Dijkstra’s wp(S, R) predicate-transformer derivations. A reader who has done both books has the picture and the proof of the same invariants.

Open questions

How should an LLM-tutor wrapper around EPI work? The three-stage scaffolding (problem → hint → solution) is the load-bearing pedagogy, and naive LLM use destroys it (the reader asks GPT for the solution, gets it, and skips the recognition-building struggle). The right interaction model is probably Socratic until the reader has produced the brute-force solution, then pattern-naming-only until the reader names the pattern, then derivation review — but no current interview-prep LLM defaults to this posture.

How does the book scale to contemporary interview formats? EPI is written for whiteboard interviews; the 2024–2026 reality is increasingly take-home assignments, pair-programming sessions, and live-coding on shared editors. The pattern catalog still applies, but the meta-pedagogy (how to think out loud, when to consult docs, how to handle infrastructure) does not transfer cleanly. A 4th edition addressing live-coding and take-home formats is overdue.

What is the right post-EPI reading list? A reader who finishes EPI and wants to continue deepening algorithmic mastery has no obvious next book. CLRS is too theoretical; Skiena’s Algorithm Design Manual overlaps; Kleinberg-Tardos is graduate-level. The actual next move is competitive-programming material (Codeforces, ICPC) — but the bridge from interview-prep to competitive-programming is unwritten in the textbook ecosystem.

How should the variants discipline scale to system-design interviews? Chapter 21 hints at this but does not develop it. A staff-level interviewer asks “design TinyURL,” then perturbs it (“now make it geo-distributed; now make it support analytics; now make it multi-tenant”) in a sequence structurally identical to EPI’s algorithmic variants. A book that explicitly builds the design-variants discipline does not yet exist.

Research uses

  • Interview prep for quant-research roles: chapters 11 (heaps) and 18 (greedy + invariants) supply the algorithmic vocabulary for Citadel / Jane Street / Two Sigma quant-engineer rounds; chapter 17 (DP) covers the pricing-tree and optimal-execution problems that surface in those rounds.
  • Code review checklist: the four pattern tables of chapter 4 give a vocabulary for reviewing PRs — “this is an O(n²) join hidden in a list-membership check; the right structure is a hash set” — that scales from junior to staff. Asking “which pattern from EPI does this PR match?” is a high-leverage code-review move.
  • On-call debugging: EPI’s failure-mode storytelling (brute-force first, refine when it bottlenecks) maps onto the on-call workflow (instrument the slow path, identify the dominant cost, refine). The recognition reflexes the book builds are the same ones a senior on-call responder uses to triage a latency spike at 2 a.m.
  • Hiring panel calibration: chapter 3 (interviewer’s playbook) is required reading for hiring managers building an algorithmic interview loop. The “no single point of failure / multiple solutions / multi-area coverage / calibrate on colleagues” criteria are the operational definition of a fair algorithmic interview.
  • LLM-agent coding evaluation: EPI’s pattern catalog is exactly the right reference for evaluating LLM-generated code. An agent that produces an O(n²) join when the pattern table demands a hash set is failing on the same axis as a junior interview candidate, and the same vocabulary lets reviewers articulate the failure mode precisely.
  • Curriculum design for boot camps: a 12-week boot camp that uses EPI chapter 4 as week 1 and then drills two chapters of Part II per week — with strict adherence to the Part II → Part III → Part IV scaffolding — produces graduates who clear FAANG algorithmic rounds at materially higher rates than alternatives that grind LeetCode without a pattern-catalog spine.
