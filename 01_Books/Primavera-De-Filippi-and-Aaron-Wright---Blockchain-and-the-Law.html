Primavera De Filippi and Aaron Wright - Blockchain and the Law

One-sentence thesis

Blockchains do not — contra both cypherpunk hopes and regulator fears — produce ungovernable spaces beyond the rule of law; they produce a parallel rule system called lex cryptographica that operates autonomously and transnationally, but that remains regulable through seven identifiable chokepoints (end users, ISPs, information intermediaries, exchanges, miners, software developers, hardware manufacturers) and through Lessig’s four classical levers (law, market, norms, architecture), so the genuine governance question is not whether states can constrain code but which layer to constrain, how to balance ex-ante automatic enforcement against ex-post judicial discretion, and whether to accept a regulatory-substitution from the tyranny of centralized intermediaries to the tyranny of code that may turn out to be worse — particularly when authoritarian states adopt code-as-law to implement social-credit-style rule-by-algorithm at the level of the protocol itself (De Filippi and Wright 2018, ch. 11 pp. 173-192; ch. 12 pp. 193-204; ch. 13 pp. 205-210).

Why this book matters

Published by Harvard University Press in 2018, Blockchain and the Law is the first comprehensive scholarly treatment by legal academics of how blockchain interacts with existing legal regimes — written before the 2018-2024 wave of policy responses (Wyoming’s DAO LLC Act 2021, MiCA 2023, the SEC v. Ripple ruling 2023, the OFAC Tornado Cash sanctions 2022, the post-merge Ethereum censorship-compliance debate 2022-2023) but anticipating the structural questions each of these later events forced. De Filippi (CNRS researcher, Harvard Berkman Klein faculty associate, Coalition for Automated Legal Applications co-founder) and Wright (Cardozo Law clinical professor, former director of the Cardozo Tech Startup Clinic, OpenLaw / Lex DAO co-founder) wrote it as practitioner-academic hybrids — both directly involved in the legal-engineering communities trying to build the institutions the book analyzes. That positionality is unusual; most legal-academic blockchain treatments of the 2017-2018 period were written from outside the developer ecosystem, and most developer-side treatments lacked legal training.

For Yang’s research stack, the book matters in four concrete ways. First, it is the canonical academic treatment of the regulatory-chokepoint question in crypto, providing the analytical vocabulary (lex cryptographica, code-as-law vs. code-is-law, the seven chokepoints, autonomy-as-alegality) that subsequent scholarship works within. Second, the cross-mapping of Lessig’s four-mode regulation theory onto blockchain (ch. 11, fig. 11.2) is the cleanest portable tool the book offers — it lets a regulator or scholar quickly classify any blockchain-regulation proposal by which lever it pulls and predict its likely failure modes. Third, the TheDAO case as a worked example (chs. 8-9) is the foundational empirical reference for DAO governance research — anyone working on DAO legal status without engaging this treatment is starting from a weaker base. Fourth, the code-as-law vs. rule-of-law tension (ch. 12) is the deepest theoretical move; it bears directly on Yang’s regulatory-architecture interests and on the algocracy-in-China question that intersects with the Authoritarian Commons - Neighborhood Democratization in Urban China frame.

For the broader crypto-policy audience, the book reframes several misleading narratives. It is sharply against the cypherpunk-utopian position (May, Barlow, the early Bitcoin community) that blockchains are structurally unregulatable — the seven-chokepoint analysis demonstrates that every layer has identifiable participants in identifiable jurisdictions susceptible to coercive force. It is equally sharp against the regulator-paranoid position that blockchains threaten the entire legal order — the four-lever analysis shows that the rule of law has substantial residual reach even in a blockchain-saturated world, and that the policy choice is which regulatory mode to deploy, not whether regulation is possible. The right inference is neither that crypto wins nor that the state wins; it is that the regulatory architecture must adapt to a substrate where some rules are now self-executing, transnational, and tamper-resistant.

For the practitioner audience — exchange compliance teams, DAO legal counsel, smart-contract developers — the book is the shared analytical vocabulary. When a Kraken compliance officer and a Vermont BBLLC drafter and an OFAC sanctions analyst use the same term “lex cryptographica,” they are using De Filippi and Wright’s term in De Filippi and Wright’s sense; the book has effectively succeeded in establishing a doctrinal lexicon. Even practitioners who disagree with its prescriptions argue within its framework.

The book’s eight-year shelf life (2018-2026) has been remarkable for technology-policy scholarship. The seven-chokepoint analysis remains the right map for the current enforcement landscape (exchanges, developers, miners — exactly where enforcement has concentrated). The code-as-law concerns about social-credit-style customized rule application have grown more salient as China’s social-credit system matured and as Western algorithmic-governance debates (predictive policing, automated benefits adjudication) accelerated. The book’s specific predictions about DAO regulatory status have been substantially borne out — though the legislation it called for (giving DAOs legal-entity status with limited liability) has appeared piecemeal and inconsistently. Blockchain and the Law is the right starting point for anyone entering this regulatory space; eight years later, no other text serves the same function.

Core argument

  1. Blockchains are not a single technology but a stack of five fused prerequisites: packet-switched networks (Baran 1964); public-private key cryptography (Diffie-Hellman 1976; Rivest-Shamir-Adleman 1978); peer-to-peer architectures (Napster, Gnutella, BitTorrent); cypherpunk digital-cash experiments (Chaum’s DigiCash 1994; Wai Dai, Hal Finney, Nick Szabo c. 1998-2008) that all foundered on the double-spending problem; and Nakamoto’s 2008 nine-page paper, which solved double-spending without a clearinghouse via proof-of-work consensus and a 21M-bitcoin block-reward incentive scheme (ch. 1, pp. 13-32). Each prior technology is necessary; none alone is sufficient. Treating blockchain as a 2008 invention rather than a 50-year accumulation misses the structural argument that legal regulation can attack any layer of the stack.

  2. Eight characteristics of public permissionless blockchains are each dual — capability and limitation are the same property: disintermediation/transnationality (no centralized regulable entity / no centralized accountable maintainer); resiliency (hard to destroy / hard to take down lawfully); tamper resistance (immutable record / impossible to correct errors); transparency (auditable / surveillance-attack-surface); pseudonymity (privacy / criminal-friendly); incentivization (decentralized security / mining-pool consolidation); consensus (coordination without intermediaries / political-process for upgrades); autonomy (alegality of execution / alegality of execution) (ch. 2, pp. 33-58). The dual-property structure is the book’s most portable analytical tool — every blockchain-regulation question can be reframed as “which dual is at stake, and which side does the regulation prefer?”

  3. Lex cryptographica is the cryptographically-secured rule system that emerges from the eight characteristics — private regulatory frameworks that operate autonomously, transnationally, and independently of any government or centralized intermediary (ch. 2, pp. 49-52, the term’s first defined appearance; ch. 1 p. 6, its introduction). Joel Reidenberg’s 1997 lex informatica is the conscious precursor (centralized-platform code-rules), but lex cryptographica differs decisively: lex informatica is administered by a regulable centralized operator, while lex cryptographica is administered by autonomous code with no central operator to regulate (ch. 2 fn. 14, p. 214). The conceptual move is fundamental — it is what makes the book a treatise on a new regulatory phenomenon rather than another iteration of Code v2.

  4. Code-as-law is the inversion of code-is-law. Lessig’s code is law (1999, 2006) treats code as one of four regulatory modes states use to constrain behavior; the technology is the regulator. Code as law (ch. 12, p. 199) treats code as the substrate of legal rules themselves — laws automatically executed by smart contracts rather than implemented by bureaucrats and judges. AML reporting, derivatives margin calls, tax collection, welfare eligibility can all in principle be transposed; the chapter walks through specific worked examples for each. The benefit is reduced compliance cost, ex-ante enforcement, tamper-evident audit trails. The cost is brittleness — the open-textured law that judges interpret with reasonableness/good-faith/due-care standards cannot be coded faithfully, and coding the ambiguity away is itself a major legal change that the technology smuggles in under the guise of efficiency.

  5. The seven chokepoints are the regulatory map (ch. 11, pp. 173-192): (i) end users — direct prohibition or vicarious liability, effective on identifiable users but not at scale; (ii) ISPs / transport layer — packet blocking, deep-packet inspection, the China-firewall template; (iii) information intermediaries — search-engine de-listing, social-media suppression, the discoverability lever; (iv) blockchain-specific intermediaries — exchanges, custodial wallets, fiat onramps, the practical enforcement layer where 2018-2026 effort has concentrated; (v) miners and transaction processors — mining-pool concentration as a regulatable feature, with limits when miners can cross-jurisdictionally migrate; (vi) software developers — direct code regulation, registration, strict liability, with First Amendment limits in the US; (vii) hardware manufacturers — ASIC vendors, kill-switches, export controls, with the Clipper Chip cautionary precedent. The chapter is the cleanest single guide to where regulation is feasible and what its costs are.

  6. The four Lessig levers retain force on blockchain (ch. 11, fig. 11.2): law (direct prohibition); architecture (mandated protocol features, backdoors, registration); market (taxing transactions, manipulating native-token reserves); norms (community pressure, miner-coordination on protocol upgrades). The chapter argues that the autonomy characteristic does not eliminate any of the four — it only redirects each to a non-traditional layer. Direct user prosecution gives way to exchange compliance; direct code regulation gives way to mining-pool norms and hardware export controls; legal sanction gives way to market-cost shifting. The book does not deliver a how-to manual, but it provides the analytical scaffolding for any specific policy choice.

  7. Smart contracts as legal contracts (ch. 4, pp. 72-88) are the bridge between blockchain-as-payment-system and blockchain-as-organizational-substrate. Nick Szabo’s late-1990s vision (ch. 4 p. 73) — programs that resemble “contractual clauses” with autonomous execution — became practical with Ethereum’s Turing-complete EVM and Solidity. The doctrinal claim is that smart contracts share contract formation with traditional contracts (offer, acceptance, meeting-of-the-minds) but diverge sharply at performance — moving from ex-post court-enforced to ex-ante self-executing. Oracles bridge on-chain logic to off-chain data; standardized contract libraries threaten consumer-protection by concentrating authoring power; the rigidity-flexibility tradeoff is the central legal problem. The TheDAO hack (June 2016, ~$55M drained via reentrancy bug) is the canonical case where code execution diverged from party intent, with the Ethereum hard-fork rolling back the chain to refund holders — an early demonstration that code-is-law purity collapses under social pressure when stakes are high.

  8. DAOs (decentralized autonomous organizations) are the most aggressive test case for legal personhood (chs. 8-9, pp. 131-155). The book carefully distinguishes decentralized organizations (DOs — token-mediated human governance, the TheDAO model) from decentralized autonomous organizations (DAOs — AI/algorithmic governance without ongoing human direction, Meir Dan-Cohen’s 1986 self-owning corporation made real). Three legal challenges are walked through systematically: lack of limited liability (default general-partnership exposure for members); securities laws (Howey-test application to tokens); non-regulatability (smart contracts execute regardless of court orders). The 2017 European Parliament electronic personhood proposal and Houman Shabad’s call for legislative recognition are the live policy responses; the book’s call for legislative recognition has been answered piecemeal (Vermont BBLLC 2018, Wyoming DAO LLC 2021) but inconsistently across jurisdictions.

  9. The dual-nature thesis runs throughout each application chapter — payments (improvement of remittance + AML evasion, ch. 3); contracts (efficient performance + criminal-friendly autonomy, ch. 4); securities (24/7 markets + regulatory arbitrage, ch. 5); information (censorship-resistance for dissidents + censorship-resistance for criminals, ch. 7); IoT (machine-economic-agency + perfect-enforcement PRM dystopia, ch. 10). The architecture’s core property is autonomy, and autonomy is symmetric — it benefits whoever uses it. There is no protocol-level distinction between liberation and lawlessness. Regulatory choice is therefore not between freedom and constraint but between which constraints — Lessig and Benkler are invoked as the canonical statements of this point (ch. 13 p. 207, 210).

  10. The most concerning trajectory is not crypto-anarchy but code-as-authoritarianism (ch. 12, pp. 202-203, with the Chinese social-credit system as named exhibit). The same architecture that lets dissidents transact without state knowledge lets authoritarian states implement individualized rule application — different rules for different citizens based on accumulated behavioral profile, mediated by smart contracts that execute without judicial review. This violates the universality principle (“all are equal before the law”) and the non-discrimination principle by design. The book treats this as the worst-case alignment — code-as-law combined with authoritarian state capacity — and argues that protecting flexibility, ambiguity, and judicial discretion in core law is the right defense even when this means accepting some efficiency loss.

Argument map

The diagram traces the book’s logical structure from foundations through the dual-nature assessment to the regulatory recommendation, with each top-level node corresponding to a chapter cluster:

  • Five technological prerequisites fuse in 2008-2015 (ch. 1)
    • Packet switching → public-private key cryptography → P2P networks → cypherpunk digital cash → Nakamoto consensus → Ethereum’s Turing-complete EVM
    • Output: public permissionless blockchains as a new general-purpose substrate
  • Eight characteristics emerge, each dual (ch. 2)
    • Disintermediation, resiliency, tamper resistance, transparency, pseudonymity, incentivization, consensus, autonomy
    • Each property is simultaneously the source of value and the source of regulatory friction
    • Output: lex cryptographica — autonomous transnational rule systems
  • Five application domains exhibit the dual-nature (chs. 3-10)
    • Payments: remittance improvement / AML evasion
    • Contracts: efficient performance / criminal-friendly autonomy
    • Securities: 24/7 markets / regulatory arbitrage
    • Information: dissident publishing / illegal-content distribution
    • IoT: machine economic agency / perfect-enforcement consumer-rights erosion
    • Output: empirical demonstration that no protocol-level distinction separates liberation from lawlessness
  • Seven regulatory chokepoints map the regulator’s leverage (ch. 11)
    • End users → ISPs → info intermediaries → exchanges → miners → developers → hardware manufacturers
    • Each layer’s effectiveness ranked by directness, friction, and side-effects
    • Output: blockchain is not unregulatable; effective regulation is layered, indirect, architectural
  • Code-as-law inverts code-is-law (ch. 12)
    • Lessig 1999: code regulates → De Filippi-Wright 2018: code IS the rule
    • Three concrete regtech applications (AML, derivatives, taxation) plus three structural limitations (open-textured law, gaming, bug exposure)
    • Output: efficient automatic enforcement at the cost of flexibility, contestability, and judicial discretion
  • The worst-case is authoritarian code-as-law (ch. 12 closing, ch. 13)
    • Customized rules-by-individual-profile (Chinese social-credit named explicitly)
    • Violates universality and non-discrimination by design
    • Output: protect open-textured judicial discretion in core law as the guardrail

The diagram reads top-to-bottom. The technology layer determines the substrate; the substrate produces the dual-nature applications; the applications create the regulatory crisis; the regulatory chokepoints are the response; code-as-law is the dual-edged use of the technology to implement regulation; and the authoritarian inflection is the trajectory the book most warns against. Each move depends on the prior — taking the book’s regulatory recommendations seriously requires accepting the eight-characteristic analysis, which requires accepting the technological-stack analysis. The argument is cumulative rather than independent.

Researchers should use the map both forwards (predict whether a given application is regulable from its position in the seven-chokepoint stack) and backwards (read off implied regulatory pressure from observed enforcement patterns — the 2018-2024 pattern of exchange-and-developer-targeting matches the book’s prediction that those layers are the practical enforcement chokepoints). Inconsistencies between predicted and observed regulation diagnose either a missed chokepoint (e.g. the rise of MEV bots and validator censorship post-merge as a new layer the 2018 analysis didn’t anticipate) or a missed political-economy variable (state preferences shifting on what to enforce).

Key concepts

  • Lex Cryptographica — De Filippi and Wright’s central concept; cryptographically-secured private regulatory frameworks that operate autonomously, transnationally, and without centralized administration
  • Code-as-Law — the inversion of Lessig’s “code is law”: using smart contracts as the executable substrate of legal rules themselves, not just as one of four regulatory modes
  • Smart Contract Doctrine — the legal status, formation, performance, and dispute-resolution treatment of self-executing blockchain-based agreements; the rigidity-flexibility tradeoff vs. natural-language contracts
  • Decentralized Autonomous Organization (DAO) — token-and-smart-contract-governed entities that operate without traditional corporate hierarchy; the legal-personhood, securities, and liability frontier
  • Permissionless Innovation Spectrum — the regulatory-stance continuum from prohibit (authoritarian default) to permission-required (US securities regime) to permissionless-with-chokepoints (the book’s recommended middle path) to permissionless-with-no-friction (cypherpunk ideal); calibrating regulatory friction without prohibition
  • Pathetic Dot Theory — Lessig’s four-mode regulation framework (law, market, norms, architecture), which the book extends from internet-of-1999 to blockchain-of-2018 by identifying that all four levers retain force even when the regulated subject is autonomous code

Best chapters/pages to actually read

  • Chapter 1 (Blockchains, Bitcoin, and Decentralized Computing Platforms), pp. 13-32 — the technological-stack analysis. Read for the five-prerequisite genealogy (Baran → Diffie-Hellman → Napster → Chaum → Nakamoto), the proof-of-work mechanics, the Ethereum/EVM extension, and the closing claim that public permissionless chains (not permissioned consortium chains) are the analytical focus. Worth annotating against any other history of blockchain you have read; this is the cleanest legal-academic version.
  • Chapter 2 (Characteristics of Blockchains), pp. 33-58 — the analytical heart. The eight-characteristic dual-property analysis (pp. 34-46) is the book’s most portable contribution. The TCP/IP-stack diagrams (figs. 2.1, 2.2, pp. 48-49) set up the chokepoint analysis of ch. 11. The closing protocols-and-power discussion (pp. 53-56) tracks Foucault → Deleuze → Aneesh’s algocracy and announces lex cryptographica as the core concept. Read this twice.
  • Chapter 4 (Smart Contracts as Legal Contracts), pp. 72-88 — the doctrinal contracts treatment. The Szabo genealogy (pp. 73-74) places smart contracts in their proper intellectual context (a 1990s vision finally executable on Ethereum). The rigidity-flexibility tradeoff and the oracle bridge are the central theoretical claims. The TheDAO foreshadowing (referenced here, fully developed in chs. 8-9) is the empirical anchor. Skip on a first reading at risk of misunderstanding chs. 8-9.
  • Chapter 8 (The Future of Organizations), pp. 131-145 — the DO/DAO foundation chapter. Read the three legal challenges (limited liability, securities, non-regulatability, pp. 141-145) systematically; they are the structure of the entire DAO-legal-status policy debate of 2018-2024. The Uber-as-DAO thought experiment (pp. 138-139) is the most accessible illustration. The TheDAO case is integrated throughout; read alongside the Slock.it founding documents if you want primary-source depth.
  • Chapter 9 (Decentralized Autonomous Organizations), pp. 146-155 — the AI-DAO speculation. The Dan-Cohen 1986 reference (p. 146) is the canonical philosophical anchor for the self-owning organization concept. The plantoid case (pp. 166-167, in ch. 10 but referenced here) is De Filippi’s own art-and-research project, the empirical proof-of-concept. The legal-personhood discussion (pp. 152-155) is the cleanest treatment of the question in the 2018 literature.
  • Chapter 11 (Modes of Regulation), pp. 173-192 — the regulatory-chokepoint chapter. This is the single most important chapter for anyone working on crypto policy. The seven chokepoints (end users, ISPs, info intermediaries, exchanges, miners, developers, hardware) are walked through with case-law and historical-precedent grounding. The Lessig-four-lever extension (fig. 11.2, p. 175) is the analytical scaffolding. Required reading for any policy-targeted use of the book.
  • Chapter 12 (Code as Law), pp. 193-204 — the most theoretically ambitious chapter. The shift from code-is-law to code-as-law (p. 199) is the book’s distinctive contribution to legal theory. The three regtech applications (AML, derivatives, taxation) and the three limitations (open-texture, gaming, bug exposure) are the core. The customized-law / Chinese social-credit closing (pp. 202-203) is the most concerning trajectory the book identifies. Required reading for anyone working on regulatory-architecture or algocracy questions.
  • Conclusion, pp. 205-210 — the synthesis. Reads as a unified statement of the book’s thesis after the chapter-by-chapter exposition. The Lessig and Benkler quotes (pp. 207, 210) are the rhetorical anchors for the book’s central claim — replacing intermediary tyranny with code tyranny is not obviously a victory.
  • Skim — chapters 5 (smart securities), 6 (tamper-resistant data), 7 (resilient information systems), 10 (blockchain of things) are extension chapters that elaborate the characteristics-of-ch.2 frame onto specific application domains. Useful as references; not load-bearing for the analytical argument. Chapter 3 (digital currencies) is well-written but extensively superseded by post-2018 events; read for the AML / surveillance-inversion frame and the Georgetown-1971-thought-experiment vignette (p. 69), skim the rest.

Important examples / cases

  • The Bitcoin genesis block (January 3, 2009) with the embedded message “The Times 03/Jan/2009 Chancellor on brink of second bailout for banks” (ch. 3, p. 62) — the political claim that Bitcoin emerged as a deliberate response to the 2008 banking crisis. This is the canonical fact for the crypto-as-political-project reading; any treatment of Bitcoin that ignores it misreads the original cypherpunk intent.
  • TheDAO hack (June 2016, ~$55M drained) with the recursive-call exploit and the Ethereum hard-fork response (chs. 8 pp. 140-141, with detail in ch. 9). The case demonstrates: (i) smart-contract code can diverge from party intent in ways that produce legal-style disputes; (ii) the code-is-law purity collapses under social pressure when stakes are high (the hard-fork was a community decision overriding the code); (iii) the resulting Ethereum/Ethereum-Classic split shows that the exit option in blockchain governance is fork-the-chain. Required reading for any DAO governance or smart-contract liability work.
  • The 2017 SEC Report on TheDAO (referenced in ch. 5, ch. 8) applying the Howey test to find DAO tokens are securities under US law. This is the single most consequential regulatory document of the 2017-2018 period; it established the doctrinal framework that subsequent SEC enforcement (Telegram, Kik, Ripple, Ethereum-via-Hinman-test, post-publication) operates within.
  • The Silk Road darknet marketplace (~$200M in Bitcoin-denominated drug sales, ch. 3 p. 66) — the canonical illegitimate-use case for blockchain payments. Read alongside the AML / BSA / Bank Secrecy Act regulatory framework to understand how the cypherpunk ideal of free transaction met the state’s surveillance-and-control posture.
  • The 2012 Linode hack and Mt. Gox tainted-coin freeze (ch. 3 p. 69) — 43,000 BTC stolen (~$755M as of December 2017 prices); Mt. Gox froze all accounts whose transactions could be traced back to the theft until users verified their non-involvement. The empirical anchor for the fungibility erosion concern: tainted coins downstream face secondary-liability risks even when their current holders are not involved.
  • The Julian Assange December 2016 Bitcoin signing (ch. 6 p. 38, ch. 6 p. 113) — Assange used a known-WikiLeaks Bitcoin address to broadcast “We’re Fine. 8 Chan Post [is] Fake” via transaction metadata, refuting death rumors without making a public appearance. The cleanest exhibit of blockchain-as-authenticated-broadcast-infrastructure — high-probability proof of origin without traditional CAs.
  • The Clipper Chip (1990s NSA mandated encryption backdoor) (ch. 11 p. 184) — the canonical cautionary precedent for hardware-backdoor regulation. The chip was found to have security holes that anyone (not just the government) could exploit, demonstrating that backdoors weaken systems against intended targets, not just unintended ones. The book invokes this against any contemporary proposal to mandate kill-switches in mining hardware.
  • The Bitcoin and Ethereum mining-pool concentration figures (December 2017: 4 pools >50% of Bitcoin, 2 pools >50% of Ethereum; ch. 1 p. 40, ch. 11 p. 180) — the empirical anchor for the miners-as-regulatable-chokepoint argument. Geographic concentration in China (2018) made this lever particularly potent for one regulator; the post-2021 Chinese mining ban and US/Kazakhstan/Russia migration shifted but did not eliminate the lever.
  • The Daemon darknet-marketplace announcement (ch. 8 p. 145) — a planned anonymous-shareholder DO designed explicitly to be outside any government’s reach. The cleanest exhibit of the non-regulatability of decentralized organizations limit case; even with court orders, asset seizure requires majority-token coordination across distributed jurisdictions.
  • The Plantoid art-research project (De Filippi’s own work, ch. 10 pp. 166-167) — a metallic-flower mechanical structure with an Ethereum smart-contract “spirit” that solicits donations, votes on reproduction proposals, hires artists to build offspring, and “reproduces” autonomously. The first proof-of-concept of an emancipated device with its own economic agency — read as both art piece and legal-philosophy thought experiment.

Critiques and limitations

  • The 2018 publication date is a real limitation. The book pre-dates MiCA (EU 2023), the SEC v. Ripple ruling (2023), the OFAC Tornado Cash sanctions (2022), the Ethereum merge and validator-censorship debate (2022-2023), the FTX collapse (November 2022), MakerDAO’s Black Thursday (March 2020), the post-2021 mining-jurisdiction shift, and Wyoming’s DAO LLC Act (2021). Many of the book’s specific predictions have been substantially borne out, but the regulatory landscape has evolved significantly. A reader should consult post-2022 secondary sources for current state-of-play; the book’s analytical frame remains accurate but the empirical examples are dated.
  • The treatment of permissioned blockchains is dismissive. Ch. 1 closes with the claim that permissioned blockchains are not the analytically interesting case (p. 32). This was defensible in 2018 but undersells the eight-year experiment with consortium blockchains in trade finance (Maersk TradeLens, Marco Polo Network), in central-bank settlement (Project Ubin, Project Jasper, Project Helvetia), in supply chains (IBM Food Trust). Some of the book’s seven chokepoints behave very differently in permissioned settings — for instance, the consortium itself becomes the regulatable entity, eliminating most of the chokepoint analysis. A 2026 update would need a substantial permissioned-chain treatment.
  • The legal-doctrinal treatment is thinner than the policy-architecture treatment. The book is strongest on regulatory chokepoints, weakest on contract law, securities law, tort law, and corporate-form doctrine. Ch. 4’s treatment of smart contracts as legal contracts barely engages UCC Article 2, restatement-of-contracts, or the differing common-law / civil-law approaches to performance. Ch. 8’s treatment of DOs as general partnerships does not engage the deeper RUPA / RULPA / RULLCA analysis that practitioners need. Pair the book with practitioner treatments (Wright/Vermont/Wyoming DAO LLC commentaries, Werbach treatments of smart contracts, Allen/Berg crypto-legal monographs) for doctrinal depth.
  • The cypherpunk genealogy is well done but the China-specific governance trajectory is underdeveloped. Ch. 12’s brief treatment of Chinese social-credit (pp. 202-203) is the most prescient single concern in the book, but it is one paragraph plus a short closing. The full architecture of state-controlled digital currency (DCEP), state-aligned blockchain consortiums (BSN), and state-mandated KYC for all crypto activity that emerged in China 2019-2024 deserved a dedicated chapter. The book’s most concerning trajectory is the most under-analyzed.
  • The ethics of design choices is hand-waved. The book accepts the autonomy characteristic as given without engaging deeply with the developer choices that produce it. Bitcoin’s irreversibility, Ethereum’s hard-fork willingness, Tornado Cash’s mixing, Zcash’s optional privacy — these are choices, not technical inevitabilities. A more sustained critique of crypto-development culture (whose values, whose interests, whose visions of liberation) would have strengthened the regulatory-architecture argument. As written, the book treats the developer community as a black box generating systems for others to regulate.
  • Mining-pool concentration analysis is thin. Chs. 1 and 11 cite the 2017 4-pool / 2-pool figures but do not analyze the why — economies of scale in ASIC manufacturing, electricity-cost arbitrage, geographic concentration of cheap power, and pool-vs-solo-mining variance. Without this, the regulatory-leverage argument is incomplete; readers cannot evaluate how durable the concentration is or how it might shift under different incentive regimes. The post-2021 China-ban migration was unanticipated by the 2018 analysis.
  • The First Amendment treatment is US-centric in a transnational analytical framework. Ch. 11’s developer-regulation discussion leans heavily on US First Amendment doctrine (Bernstein, Junger, Mendelsohn) without symmetrically treating European (EUC Charter, Council of Europe Convention 108), Chinese, Russian, or developing-world equivalents. For a book that argues blockchain is fundamentally transnational, the regulatory analysis is asymmetrically domestic-US in important ways.
  • The autonomy thesis may overstate. The book treats autonomy (smart contracts execute regardless of human intervention) as a fundamental characteristic. In practice, every blockchain has had its protocol amended through social process — Bitcoin’s BIPs, Ethereum’s EIPs, the various hard-forks, the proof-of-stake transition. Autonomy in operation is real; autonomy in evolution is not. The book’s regulatory-chokepoint analysis already acknowledges this implicitly (miners, developers, and users are all constrainable), but the high-level rhetoric of alegality doesn’t always integrate this point. A sharper formulation would distinguish runtime autonomy from upgrade autonomy.
  • The CtR / mutual-dependence dynamic is missing. Comparing with Authoritarian Commons - Neighborhood Democratization in Urban China (Qiao 2025), where homeowners can withdraw cooperation to force state policy reversals, the equivalent in blockchain is fork resistance — users / miners / validators can fork to escape regulator-imposed protocol changes. The book recognizes this in passing but does not develop it as a regulatory-equilibrium concept. A cooperating-to-resist analog at the blockchain layer would clarify when state coercion succeeds (compliance becomes worthwhile) and when it fails (forks become viable).
  • The author positionality. De Filippi’s CNRS / Berkman Klein / COALA affiliation and Wright’s Cardozo / OpenLaw affiliation place them in the academic-legal-engineering hybrid space the book describes — they are participants, not detached analysts. This is a strength (they understand the technical and legal details with rare combined fluency) and a limitation (their normative posture is shaped by the milieu they inhabit). The book’s neither-utopia-nor-dystopia register reads in part as a careful negotiation between the cypherpunk-developer community and the legal-academic establishment, both of which the authors must remain credible to. A reader should weight critical claims accordingly.

Links to other books

  • Direct sibling on this topic — Kevin Werbach - The Blockchain and the New Architecture of Trust (B46 ingestion alongside this book — the most direct overlap in the vault). Werbach’s trust frame and De Filippi-Wright’s law frame are paired analyses of the same 2017-2018 moment by adjacent legal academics. Werbach emphasizes the trust-architecture continuity (blockchains as one in a sequence of trust mechanisms from royal seals to credit rating agencies); De Filippi-Wright emphasize the regulatory-architecture rupture (blockchains as a new substrate that breaks the intermediary-targeting regulatory model). Read together; if forced to choose, De Filippi-Wright is the more analytically rigorous and more useful for anyone working on policy questions.
  • Practitioner-perspective companion — CZ - Freedom of Money (B45 — just-written sibling). CZ’s narrator persona faces exactly the regulatory questions De Filippi and Wright analyze academically. The Binance compliance arc — KYC implementation, BSA settlement, AML reporting failures, $4.3B DOJ settlement — is the live empirical test of the seven-chokepoint analysis: enforcement concentrated on the exchange chokepoint (not the protocol, not the users, not the miners). The book’s ch. 11 prediction that exchanges would absorb the bulk of compliance burden is borne out in CZ’s account. Read in tandem; De Filippi-Wright provides the analytical scaffolding for understanding why CZ’s company faced what it did.
  • State-monopoly-on-law context — Acharya and Lee - The Cartel System of States. Acharya-Lee model the international order as a state cartel maintaining monopoly over the legitimate use of coercion and the issuance of currency. Lex cryptographica is precisely the kind of cartel-undermining technology that the cartel framework predicts states will jointly resist. The 2018-2024 enforcement coordination (FATF Travel Rule 2019, MiCA 2023, US-EU sanctions alignment 2022) reads as exactly the cartel-protection response Acharya-Lee predict. Read for the macro-political-economy frame within which De Filippi-Wright’s micro-regulatory analysis operates.
  • Regulatory-architecture critique parallel — Joseph E. Stiglitz - Globalization and Its Discontents Revisited. Stiglitz’s analysis of IMF/WTO/Washington-Consensus failure parallels De Filippi-Wright’s analysis of blockchain regulation: in both, formal institutional design produces predictable failure modes; in both, information asymmetry between principals (regulators) and agents (firms / blockchain participants) is the structural primitive. Stiglitz argues for regulatory humility and case-by-case calibration; De Filippi-Wright argue for the same applied to crypto. The parallel is generative for thinking about regulatory-architecture more broadly than blockchain alone.
  • VC-funding-of-crypto context — Sebastian Mallaby - The Power Law. Mallaby’s history of venture capital includes the crypto-VC arc (a16z’s Marc Andreessen and Chris Dixon driving 2017-2022 crypto investment, Sequoia’s FTX bet and writedown, the broader VC capital flows that funded the projects De Filippi and Wright analyze). Reading both clarifies that lex cryptographica is not a self-organizing emergent phenomenon but a venture-capital-funded engineering project with specific economic interests behind it. The seven chokepoints become more legible when one tracks which chokepoints VCs lobbied to keep open vs. close.
  • Tech-libertarian sibling — Marc Andreessen - The pmarca Blog Archives (B44 — just-written sibling). Andreessen’s pmarca-era essays articulate the tech-libertarian / techno-optimist position that De Filippi-Wright treat with measured skepticism. Andreessen’s a16z-era crypto advocacy (Why Software Is Eating the World, Why Web3 Matters) is the natural-language version of the cypherpunk position the book evaluates. Read for the steelmanned version of the position the book is partially refuting; the book’s measured posture is more defensible after engaging the strongest version of the position it disagrees with.
  • Quantum-mechanics-of-money frame — Carlo Rovelli - Helgoland is the wrong link here. The right complement on epistemology of regulation is Karl Popper - The Logic of Scientific Discovery — Popper’s falsification frame applies to regulatory policy: a regulation is evaluable only if it makes risky predictions about behavior, and most blockchain regulations of 2018-2024 have been evaluated by exactly that standard (FinCEN guidance, BitLicense, FATF Travel Rule, MiCA — each made specific predictions and was tested against subsequent behavior). De Filippi-Wright’s chokepoint analysis is itself a Popperian framework: it makes specific predictions about which layers will absorb enforcement, and those predictions can be tested.
  • Code-as-law authoritarianism context — Authoritarian Commons - Neighborhood Democratization in Urban China (Qiao 2025). Qiao documents how Chinese homeowners use the formal-legality-review system to push back on party-quota directives (Wei v. Huizhou, March 2023) — a real-world test of legality-as-citizen-leverage even in authoritarian regimes. De Filippi-Wright’s worst-case (code-as-authoritarianism, social-credit) is the inversion: when rules are coded into smart contracts, the legality-review lever Qiao documents may not work because there is no legality-document to review. Read together for the question of whether code-as-law structurally favors or disfavors citizen pushback under authoritarian conditions.

Research uses

  • Yang’s blockchain-regulation research direct application. The seven-chokepoint analysis (ch. 11) is the right map for any specific blockchain-regulation question: which layer is the proposal targeting, what historical precedent governs that layer, what side-effects does targeting that layer produce. Yang’s existing work on Chinese-citizen administrative-law pushback (Authoritarian Commons - Neighborhood Democratization in Urban China direction) can be cross-mapped to crypto regulation by asking: which chokepoints are accessible to Chinese citizen legality-review pushback (the exchange chokepoint via Coinbase-vs-Huobi-style operators? the developer chokepoint via local-mandatory-document-against-central-guideline patterns?) Concrete next step: identify any local Chinese crypto-related document with mandatory language that contradicts a higher-tier central guideline, evaluate legality-review viability, write up as comparative-regulatory-architecture paper.
  • Lex-cryptographica-as-comparative-legal-system project. The concept generalizes beyond blockchain: any system of automated rule-execution by code (algorithmic trading, automated benefits adjudication, content-moderation algorithms, predictive-policing algorithms) shares some properties with lex cryptographica. A research program comparing lex cryptographica with these adjacent systems — what’s portable, what’s blockchain-specific — would be a substantial contribution to legal theory. The Aneesh / Danaher algocracy literature is the right starting point.
  • Code-as-authoritarianism trajectory monitoring. Ch. 12’s Chinese social-credit concern is the most prescient single passage in the book and deserves systematic empirical follow-up. A research program tracking (i) which Chinese cities have integrated social-credit scores into smart-contract-style automated rule application, (ii) which countries beyond China are adopting similar architectures (Russia’s digital-ruble + sanctions enforcement, India’s UPI + Aadhaar integration, Singapore’s GovTech + digital-identity stack), (iii) which constitutional / legality-review pushback mechanisms remain available — would produce a 2026-2030 update to the book’s 2018 analysis.
  • DAO-legal-status comparative survey. The book calls for legislation recognizing DOs and DAOs as legal entities (ch. 8 p. 142, citing Houman Shabad). Eight years later, the legislation has appeared piecemeal: Vermont BBLLC (2018), Wyoming DAO LLC (2021), Tennessee DAO LLC (2022), Marshall Islands DAO LLC (2022). A comparative-statutory-design survey — what each statute actually does, how the choices differ, which DAO operators have adopted which structure, what enforcement experience has emerged — would close a real gap in the literature. The existing survey treatments (Wright et al., COALA model law) are first-draft; a 2025 comprehensive review is overdue.
  • CtR / fork-resistance dynamic at the blockchain layer. Drawing on Qiao’s Cooperating to Resist mechanism for state-society interaction, an analog at the blockchain layer — cooperating-to-fork — describes how user/validator/developer communities cooperate with a blockchain’s normal operation but withdraw that cooperation (fork the chain, run a competing client, refuse OFAC-compliant blocks) when state pressure becomes excessive. Modeling this dynamic formally would clarify when state coercion succeeds at the protocol level and when it fails. The Ethereum-merge OFAC-compliance debate (2022-2023) is the live empirical test case.
  • Permissioned-vs-permissionless regulatory comparison. Ch. 1 brackets permissioned blockchains as not analytically interesting. The 2018-2024 experience — extensive consortium-blockchain deployments in trade finance, central-bank settlement, supply chains — provides empirical material to test whether the dual-nature analysis applies symmetrically. Hypothesis: permissioned-blockchain dual-natures collapse onto the consortium itself (now a single regulatable entity), eliminating most of the seven chokepoints and converting blockchain regulation into a special case of consortium regulation. Testing this hypothesis empirically is a research project in its own right.
  • Teaching uses. The book is the right single text for a session in a law-and-technology syllabus on blockchain regulation. Pair with Werbach (The Blockchain and the New Architecture of Trust) for trust-architecture frame, with Lessig (Code v2) for the code-is-law foundational text, with Reidenberg (Lex Informatica) for the centralized-platform predecessor concept. The seven-chokepoint chart (ch. 11) is a clean classroom artifact: ask students to populate the rows for a specific application (NFT marketplaces? prediction markets? DeFi lending?) and discuss enforcement priorities.
  • Policy-memo target. For policymakers in Treasury / OCC / SEC / CFTC / FinCEN / OFAC drafting blockchain-related rulemakings, the book is the right reference for the analytical scaffolding. Specific use: ch. 11’s discussion of side-effects (Clipper Chip security degradation, mining migration, fork resistance) is the cleanest exhibit of regulation-induced collateral damage. Drafting comments to specific NPRMs benefits from systematically asking: which chokepoint is this targeting? what side-effects does the analysis predict? how can the rule be calibrated to minimize them?
  • Investment / cross-border deal-making. Yang’s policy-research consulting on counterparty regulatory exposure can use the seven-chokepoint analysis to assess crypto-related counterparties: which chokepoints touch this counterparty’s business model (exchange + custody + retail = exchange chokepoint primary; protocol-only DeFi = developer chokepoint primary; mining = miner-and-hardware chokepoint primary), and what 2026-2028 enforcement trajectory does the chokepoint analysis predict for that profile. The chokepoint-coordinates of a given counterparty’s business are a real input variable for due diligence.

Open questions

  • Will lex cryptographica fragment by jurisdiction or remain transnational? The book’s 2018 thesis is that lex cryptographica is fundamentally transnational. Eight years of regulatory divergence — MiCA’s permissioned-set of EU-compliant tokens, US state-by-state BitLicense variation, Chinese DCEP vs. Western blockchain ecosystem, the FATF Travel Rule’s uneven implementation — suggests fragmentation. If lex cryptographica fragments, the book’s analytical frame needs revision: the seven chokepoints become seven chokepoints per jurisdiction, and the regulatory-architecture problem becomes one of inter-jurisdictional coordination, much closer to traditional international-law problems than the book’s framing suggests.
  • Does code-as-law actually deliver promised efficiency gains? Ch. 12’s worked examples (AML, derivatives, taxation) assume that regtech smart contracts produce real cost savings. Empirically, eight years of regtech deployment has been mixed: some cost savings (continuous audit trails, automatic margin calls in DeFi), some cost increases (smart-contract audit costs, exploit-recovery costs, regulatory uncertainty about novel structures). A systematic accounting of regtech cost-benefit is overdue and would test the book’s central efficiency claim.
  • How resilient is fork-as-exit to coordinated state pressure? The book treats forks as a residual mechanism for community resistance to protocol-level regulation. The Ethereum merge survived the OFAC-compliance pressure with significant validator participation but also significant censorship-compliant block construction. Whether fork-as-exit remains viable under sustained coordinated state pressure (multi-jurisdictional sanctions on non-compliant validators, hardware bans on non-compliant miners) is empirically open.
  • Does the seven-chokepoint analysis miss layers that emerged post-2018? Specific candidates: MEV-extractor / MEV-relay / block-proposer separation post-merge; cross-chain bridges as regulable chokepoints; restaking / EigenLayer-style infrastructure; rollup operators / sequencers in L2 ecosystems; institutional-grade custodians (Anchorage, BitGo, Fidelity); intent-based architectures with off-chain solvers. The book’s seven chokepoints map well onto 2018 architecture but may need extension for 2024-2026 architecture.
  • What is the right legal-personhood scaffolding for AI-DAOs? Ch. 9’s AI-DAO speculation predates LLMs and the post-2022 AI-agent infrastructure. The European Parliament’s electronic-personhood proposal (2017) is one option; corporate-veil-piercing analogy is another; pure-instrumentality treatment (the device’s actions are imputed to whoever deployed it) is a third. As AI-DAOs become technically feasible 2026-2030 (post-LLM-agent infrastructure), the legal-personhood question becomes urgent rather than speculative. Empirically tracking which option courts and legislatures actually adopt is a research priority.
  • Does the customized-law / social-credit trajectory generalize beyond authoritarian regimes? Ch. 12 names China explicitly but the dynamic — coding individualized rule application via behavioral profile and oracle data — is technically possible in democratic regimes too. US predictive-policing algorithms, EU GDPR-compliant credit-scoring, individualized advertising-targeting all have some of the same structure. The question is whether democratic constitutional protections (equal protection, due process, non-discrimination) constrain code-as-law more effectively than authoritarian regimes. The first amendment + section 1983 + Equal Protection litigation track will produce empirical data 2025-2030.
  • What is the renter / migrant equivalent of crypto adoption? The book’s audience is implicitly the global-middle-class crypto user. But ~40-60% of megacity residents in major economies lack the documentation, banking access, or technical capacity to use crypto as designed. Whether parallel crypto-adjacent infrastructure (mobile-money, hawala-on-chain, agent-banking with crypto rails) develops for these populations — and how regulators treat it — is a major open empirical question.
  • Does the dual-nature thesis hold for the next-generation privacy primitives? Zcash and Monero (2018 examples) have been joined by: zero-knowledge rollups (Aztec, Polygon zkEVM, zkSync); fully homomorphic encryption emerging into practical use (Zama, FHE.org); secure enclave / TEE-based privacy (Oasis, Secret Network); MPC-based privacy (Penumbra). The dual-nature framework predicts each will be both privacy-enhancing-for-citizens and lawless-friendly-for-criminals. Whether the symmetry holds, or whether some primitives can be designed to be asymmetrically pro-civic (e.g. selective disclosure for compliance plus default privacy for everyone else), is a live engineering and policy question.
  • Methodological: Can De Filippi and Wright’s seven-chokepoint analysis be replicated for adjacent technologies — AI agents, autonomous-systems regulation, IoT, biotech? The structure (identify regulable participants in identifiable jurisdictions; map levers; analyze side-effects) is portable. Applying it systematically to comparable technologies would test whether the framework is blockchain-specific or generally applicable to autonomous-execution technologies broadly.

What I would miss if I only read this note

  • The full TCP/IP-stack diagram and its analytical work. Figs. 2.1 and 2.2 (pp. 48-49) place blockchain-as-application-protocols on top of the existing internet stack, and the diagram is what makes ch. 11’s chokepoint analysis legible — the stack is literally the map of regulable layers. The note flattens this into prose. Anyone working on protocol-stack regulatory questions should look at the diagram directly.
  • The Lessig pathetic-dot diagrams. Figs. 11.1 and 11.2 (p. 174-175) — the four-mode regulation diagram, then its blockchain extension — are the visual scaffolding for the regulatory chapter. The note describes the four modes but loses the geometric clarity. The diagrams are reproductive of Lessig’s Code v2 (2006) but the blockchain extension is De Filippi-Wright’s contribution.
  • The footnote density. The book cites ~600+ footnotes across the text. Many are pointers to court cases, regulatory documents, technical specifications, and primary-source manifestos (the Crypto Anarchist Manifesto, the Bitcoin whitepaper, Szabo’s “Formalizing and Securing Relationships on Public Networks”) that are essential infrastructure for further research. The note loses this entirely; for any deep follow-up work, the footnote tree is the bibliographic foundation.
  • The technical detail in chs. 1-2. The proof-of-work mechanics, the EVM gas pricing, the consensus-mechanism comparison, the overlay-network architecture (Color Coin, Swarm, Filecoin) — all are explained at a level that gives a non-technical legal reader operational understanding. The note compresses this to single-sentence pointers. For anyone teaching the material to a legal audience, the chapter-1-and-2 prose is the right level; the note is too compressed.
  • The case-study sequencing. Chs. 3-10 each open with an emblematic vignette (Alistair Darling and the 2008 banking crisis; the Berlin Airlift and EDI; the Internet’s 1990s commercialization; the Stop Killer Robots campaign). These are not decorative — they situate each technical chapter in a specific historical-political context that frames the dual-nature analysis. The note keeps the analytical content but loses the narrative texture; reading the openings is essential for understanding the book’s rhetorical posture.
  • The mining-pool concentration data and its source. The 4-pool / 2-pool figures (December 2017) cite specific industry sources whose tracking continues into 2026. The note keeps the figures but loses the methodology — anyone working on mining-decentralization questions should engage the original-source mining-pool tracking infrastructure (BTC.com, blockchain.com, miningpoolstats.stream) that the book references.
  • The cypherpunk genealogy in the introduction and ch. 1. Tim May’s 1988 Crypto Anarchist Manifesto, Eric Hughes’s 1993 Cypherpunk Manifesto, John Perry Barlow’s 1996 Declaration of the Independence of Cyberspace, Hal Finney’s RPOW, Wai Dai’s b-money, Nick Szabo’s bit gold — these are the canonical texts that animate the blockchain culture, and the book treats them with care. The note compresses this to “cypherpunks → Nakamoto”; the actual sequence and the texts are essential context for understanding the original cypherpunk position the book treats with measured skepticism.
  • The plantoid description and the blockchain-art research program. De Filippi’s own work on plantoids is more than an example; it is part of an ongoing research program at the intersection of blockchain, AI, and art that has produced subsequent work (the COALA model law, La Fontaine de Vaucluse residency, the Open Source Internet Research initiative). Anyone interested in the experimental edge of legal-engineering work should engage this directly.
