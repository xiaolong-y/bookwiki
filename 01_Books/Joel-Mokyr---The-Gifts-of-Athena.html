Joel Mokyr - The Gifts of Athena

Read chapter-by-chapter → Deep dive of The Gifts of Athena: Historical Origins of the Knowledge Economy (7 chapters)

One-sentence thesis

Modern sustained economic growth began when western European Useful Knowledge — split into Propositional Knowledge (omega) (knowledge “what,” Ω) and Prescriptive Knowledge (lambda) (knowledge “how,” λ) — entered a self-reinforcing positive-feedback loop, each iteration widening the epistemic base of the techniques on which production rested, lowering Access Costs (Mokyr) across society, and bridging the historical gap between the savants who studied nature and the fabricants who manipulated it.

Why this book matters

Most accounts of the Industrial Revolution explain what changed and where — the spinning jenny, the steam engine, the puddled-iron process, the cotton mill, the British Midlands. The Gifts of Athena is Mokyr’s attempt to explain why the change persisted past 1820 when every prior burst of macroinvention in human history — Hellenistic mechanics, the fifteenth-century clusters of printing and gunpowder and shipping, the Song-dynasty Chinese metallurgical surge — eventually fizzled at a higher technological plateau. His answer is operational, not literary: a society’s techniques rest on an epistemic base in propositional knowledge. When that base is narrow (“we know the technique works but not why”), progress runs into diminishing returns; when it is wide and tight, progress compounds. The Industrial Revolution, in this account, was the moment a critical mass of techniques in western Europe had transitioned to wide-base regimes, and the institutional surroundings (open science, the Republic of Letters, public lectures, the Society of Arts, eventually the corporate R&D lab) turned a one-off transition into a self-sustaining feedback loop between Ω and λ.

This book matters for the BookWiki growth shelf for three reasons. First, it provides the formal scaffolding underneath the cultural argument that Mokyr would expand fourteen years later in Joel Mokyr - A Culture of Growth. Where A Culture of Growth explains how the cultural and institutional preconditions for sustained innovation were assembled between 1500 and 1700, Gifts of Athena tells you what exactly the resulting feedback loop was and how to recognize its presence or absence in any particular industry, technique, or historical moment. The two books are meant to be read together — Gifts supplies the apparatus; Culture supplies the cultural-evolution case study. Second, it sits upstream of Robert J. Gordon - The Rise and Fall of American Growth in the causal chain. Gordon traces what happened to American living standards during the Special Century (1870–1970); Mokyr’s epistemic-base framework explains why the inventions of that century could compound rather than fizzle the way nineteenth-century macroinventions had done in earlier centuries. Third, the framework is portable. The Ω/λ split applies to firm-level R&D strategy, household behaviour change (hospital-style infection control, smoking cessation, dietary reform), software engineering (“can we extend this technique?” depends on whether we understand why it works), and the political economy of regulation. The book is short, formally precise, and easily one of the highest-leverage texts in modern economic history.

It is also methodologically important. Mokyr is one of the few economic historians who builds an explicit formal model — sets, mappings, feedback loops — without losing the historical texture that makes economic history valuable. His insistence on distinguishing discovery (addition to Ω) from invention (addition to λ), on distinguishing the epistemic base from the competence required to operate a technique, on distinguishing tightness from truth, gives later researchers vocabulary they actually need. The framework’s ability to accommodate both the Western Industrial Revolution and the failure of equally inventive earlier societies to break out of the singleton-base trap is its main empirical achievement.

Core argument

Mokyr’s argument unfolds in seven coordinated moves, each developed in one chapter.

  1. Useful knowledge splits into two analytically distinct sets, Ω and λ, and every technique rests on an epistemic base. Propositional Knowledge (omega) (Ω, knowledge “what”) is the union of all factual claims about natural regularities held by living minds or external storage. Prescriptive Knowledge (lambda) (λ, knowledge “how”) is the union of all executable techniques. A discovery is an addition to Ω; an invention is an addition to λ (the distinction is recognized in patent law). Every technique τ∈λ rests on an epistemic base Ωτ​⊆Ω — the subset of Ω that supports it. The base may be wide (deep theoretical understanding) or narrow (in the limit, a “singleton” base where all that is known is ”τ works”). This is the formal spine of the entire book.

  2. Narrow-base techniques fizzle; wide-base techniques compound. A technique with a singleton or near-singleton epistemic base has nowhere to go after its initial discovery. Brewers without microbiology can make beer but cannot perfect fermentation; ironmasters without metallurgy can make wrought iron but cannot manufacture consistent steel; physicians without bacteriology can wash hands but cannot persuade colleagues to do so. Each prior burst of human macroinvention — Hellenistic, Tang–Song Chinese, fifteenth-century European — produced impressive techniques but did not compound, because the underlying Ω was too narrow to support continuous extension. Cardwell’s Law is Mokyr’s name for the historical regularity that every prior society’s burst of technological progress eventually fizzled.

  3. Tightness — consensual confidence in Ω — is socially constructed but historically variable, and matters because techniques are adopted in proportion to the tightness of their epistemic base. Tightness depends on a society’s authoritative mode of demonstration: “Aristotle said” → “the experiment shows” → “the coefficient is 2.3 standard errors from zero.” The seventeenth-century scientific revolution did not directly invent many techniques, but it transformed the rhetorical conventions by which Ω was tightened, replacing scriptural and ancient-authority demonstrations with experimental, mathematical, and (later) statistical ones. Lister succeeded with antisepsis where Holmes and Semmelweis had failed two decades earlier because the underlying germ theory had become tight enough by the 1860s.

  4. The Industrial Enlightenment was the regime change that lowered access costs to Ω, widened savant–fabricant bridges, and bifurcated property regimes between Ω (open) and λ (proprietary). The institutional ferment of 1660–1800 — Royal Society, Académie des Sciences, Society of Arts, Encyclopédie, Lunar Society, Birmingham coffeehouses, Manchester philosophical societies, Edinburgh medical school, public lectures by Desaguliers and Cullen and Black — operated as a coordinated set of access-cost-reducing institutions. Knowledge-as-public-good (Ω) was placed in the open via priority-and-credit conventions; knowledge-as-rival-good (λ) was protected by patents and trade secrets. The bifurcation maximized social returns from Ω while preserving private incentives to invent in λ. The hybrid careers of figures like Franklin, Wedgwood, Watt, Black, Davy, Berthollet, and Rumford embodied the savant–fabricant bridge that the institutional infrastructure made possible.

  5. The Industrial Revolution was the moment that the Ω→λ→Ω feedback loop became strong enough to break out of negative feedback into positive feedback. Pre-1750 European economies operated in negative feedback: prosperity bred Malthusian pressure, predatory rent-seeking, guild resistance, and narrow-base diminishing returns. The 1760s–1790s wave of macroinventions (Arkwright, Watt, Cort, Boulton, the Montgolfiers) was important not because the gadgets were unprecedented — analogous bursts had occurred before — but because the second-wave microinventions of the 1820s (Neilson’s hot blast, Roberts’s self-actor, Stephenson’s locomotive, Chevreul’s fatty-acid chemistry) extended and consolidated rather than fizzled. The Second Industrial Revolution after 1860 — organic chemistry, electricity, internal combustion, microbiology, the industrial R&D lab — operated entirely in the wide-base regime, and the feedback loop became fully self-sustaining.

  6. Households also use λ — Mokyr calls the techniques “recipes” — and household-level Ω change drove a substantial fraction of the West’s nineteenth-century mortality decline that pure income or public-health stories cannot explain. Households convert market-purchased goods into final services (nutrition, hygiene, child care, longevity) using recipes that rest on epistemic bases of varying width and tightness. Households face weaker selection pressure on bad recipes than firms do (a poorly run household persists much longer than a poorly run firm), and worse verification environments (long lags, complex causal chains, small samples), so they rely heavily on authority and tradition. The 1900–1914 abrupt fall in infant mortality is hard to explain without invoking the propagation of germ-theory recipes (handwashing, milk pasteurization, bottle sterilization, isolation of the sick) into average households, a knowledge-effect channel that complements McKeown’s nutrition channel and Szreter’s public-health-infrastructure channel.

  7. Technology is selected by an aggregation rule that combines markets, delegated authority, and democratic voting; sustained progress requires pluralism with coordination, and is the historical anomaly rather than the norm. Resistance to new techniques is rational, not irrational — every change devalues someone’s specific human or physical capital. Workers, capital owners, bureaucracies, religious authorities, and regulators all have predictable incentives to slow or block techniques that would harm them. Frequency dependence and network externalities (QWERTY, Beta-vs-VHS, gauge wars) create additional friction. The escape from Cardwell’s Law requires pluralism (many decentralized aggregators so no single veto can stop a technique) plus coordination (open-science networks, professional associations, patent registries) so that successful innovations can diffuse across the pluralist landscape. Most societies have managed at most one of these. Western Europe in 1660–1800 uniquely combined both. The arrangement is reversible — every accumulation of veto-points and precaution-before-evidence regulation moves the aggregator back toward Cardwell’s Law gradient.

Argument map

A linear walkthrough of the argument structure:

  1. Definitional layer (chapter 1) — the formal sets Ω (propositional) and λ (prescriptive), the epistemic-base relation λ→Ωτ​⊆Ω, the auxiliary concepts of tightness, access cost, competence, and singleton vs. wide-base techniques.

  2. Phase-transition layer (chapter 2) — the eighteenth-century Industrial Enlightenment as a coordinated institutional reduction in access costs to Ω plus a widening of savant–fabricant bridges. Three legacies of the seventeenth-century scientific revolution: scientific method (experiment, measurement, reproducibility), mentality (nature as intelligible and lawful), culture (Baconian commitment to applied progress).

  3. Compounding layer (chapter 3) — why the Industrial Revolution did not fizzle the way prior macroinvention clusters had. Wide-base regimes in metallurgy (Bessemer + Mushet + Sorby), thermodynamics (Carnot–Joule–Clausius–Kelvin), electromagnetism (Oersted → Faraday → Maxwell → Kelvin), bacteriology (Pasteur → Koch → Lister), and chemistry (von Liebig → Hofmann → Perkin) made the second-wave microinventions of the 1820s consolidate rather than crystallize.

  4. Locus-of-production layer (chapter 4) — the factory system as a knowledge-access institution. As λ-techniques became more complex, the competence required to operate them outgrew what a single household could carry; specialization required physical co-location to minimize face-to-face access costs. The framework predicts partial reversal as IT lowers electronic access costs (telecommuting).

  5. Household layer (chapter 5) — recipes as household-level λ, the three-channel decomposition of mortality decline (income, prices, knowledge), the weak selection pressure and bad verification environment that make households conservative and authority-dependent.

  6. Political-economy layer (chapter 6) — the aggregation rule G=αGM​+βGD​+(1−α−β)GV​ for technology adoption, Cardwell’s Law as the historical baseline, and pluralism-with-coordination as the rare configuration that escapes it.

  7. Synthesis layer (chapter 7) — institutions explain cross-sectional income differences best (because Ω flows across borders); useful knowledge explains time-series trajectories best. Four channels through which institutions shape the growth of Ω and λ. The Cortesian-army principle: progress depends on a small elite of contributors, not on average years of schooling.

Key concepts

  • Propositional Knowledge (omega) — knowledge “what”; the set Ω of factual claims about natural regularities; supports techniques as their epistemic base.
  • Prescriptive Knowledge (lambda) — knowledge “how”; the set λ of executable techniques; rests on Ω.
  • Access Costs (Mokyr) — the marginal cost for an agent to acquire a piece of knowledge; jointly determined by information technology, codification, and cultural openness.
  • Useful Knowledge — the parent category combining Ω and λ; Kuznets’s term, redefined by Mokyr as knowledge of natural phenomena susceptible to manipulation.
  • Industrial Enlightenment — the eighteenth-century institutional ferment that lowered access costs to Ω and bridged savants and fabricants; Mokyr’s most influential coinage.
  • Republic of Letters — the transnational scholarly community whose open-science norms supplied the coordination mechanism for European pluralism (developed at length in the sister book A Culture of Growth).
  • Cultural Entrepreneur — agents who reshape the cultural menus others face; Bacon and Newton are the archetypes (also developed primarily in A Culture of Growth).

Best chapters/pages to actually read

For a first reading focus on chapters 1, 2, and 6. Chapter 1 is the conceptual hinge of the entire book — the formal definitions of Ω, λ, the epistemic base, tightness, access cost, and competence are the apparatus you will use everywhere afterwards. It is short and densely packed, and rewards a slow first read with notes. Chapter 2 introduces the Industrial Enlightenment, which is Mokyr’s most influential coinage and the single most useful section if you want to understand his bridge between the scientific revolution and the Industrial Revolution. Chapter 6 is the political-economy chapter — Cardwell’s Law, the aggregation-rule framework, the catalog of resistance mechanisms, and the pluralism-with-coordination thesis. This chapter is the most portable to contemporary policy debates (FDA precaution, NIMBY zoning, ESG capture, AI governance).

For a second reading add chapter 3 (the substantive history of how the feedback loop actually worked across nineteenth-century industries) and chapter 7 (the synthesis with the institutions-vs-culture debate). Chapter 3 is essential if you want concrete grounding for the formal apparatus — Bessemer, Carnot, Kelvin, Pasteur, Lister, Edison, von Liebig all appear here. Chapter 7 is the intellectual settlement: how Mokyr fits inside, around, and partly against the North-Acemoglu institutionalists and the Landes-McCloskey culturalists.

Chapters 4 and 5 are highly worthwhile for specialists but skippable on first pass. Chapter 4 (factory system) repackages a familiar story through the Ω/λ lens and adds the division-of-knowledge explanation as a fourth driver alongside fixed costs, information asymmetry, and effort enhancement. Chapter 5 (household recipes) is the freshest empirical contribution and contains the three-channel decomposition of mortality decline; it is the chapter most worth reading if your work touches health economics or behaviour change.

Important examples / cases

  • Cort’s puddling-and-rolling process (1785) — paradigmatic narrow-base technique that worked despite its inventor’s flawed phlogiston-era theory; shows that some Ω is necessary even for craft-driven progress.
  • Watt’s separate condenser — built on Black’s latent-heat work in Glasgow, an early demonstration of the savant–fabricant bridge.
  • Bessemer steelmaking (1856) — invented by a man with “very limited knowledge of iron metallurgy”; rescued and extended by Mushet’s manganese-carbon-iron decarburizer and Sorby’s microscopic crystal analysis. The wider epistemic base in subsequent decades is what made the technique compound.
  • Carnot–Joule–Clausius–Kelvin chain (1824–1850) — thermodynamics emerged from steam-engine practice as a λ→Ω feedback example; subsequently fed back into engineering through Rankine’s Manual of the Steam Engine (1859) and Elder’s compound marine engine.
  • Aspirin (1897) vs. electric generators (1865–1880) — Mokyr’s canonical contrast for narrow-base vs. wide-base regimes existing simultaneously in the late nineteenth century.
  • Lister’s antisepsis (1865) — succeeded where Holmes (1843) and Semmelweis (1840s) had failed because germ theory had tightened in the intervening twenty years; emblematic of the role tightness plays in technique adoption.
  • Appert’s food canning (1795) — narrow-base macroinvention that posed the puzzle eventually answered by Pasteur’s bacteriology; vivid example of λ acting as a “focusing device” for Ω.
  • Society of Arts (founded 1754) — the access-cost-reducing institution archetype; 6,200 prizes between 1754 and 1784; ruled out patentees from prizes until 1845, embodying the bifurcation of property regimes.
  • The 1900–1914 fall in infant mortality — the chapter-5 case for the household-knowledge channel; pure income and public-works explanations cannot match the timing.
  • Dupont’s nylon division blocking polyester — chapter-6 example of bureaucratic resistance even within ostensibly profit-maximizing firms; Celanese captured the polyester-tire-cord market because Dupont’s nylon department outmaneuvered its polyester department internally.
  • QWERTY, Beta-vs-VHS, OS/2-vs-MS-DOS — frequency-dependence cases illustrating that even superior techniques can fail when the installed base of complementary assets favors the incumbent.

Critiques and limitations

The book has four limitations worth taking seriously.

First, the framework is more retrospectively explanatory than predictively useful. Mokyr can show, after the fact, that any given technique either had a wide epistemic base (and so compounded) or a narrow one (and so fizzled). But the framework provides little guidance for distinguishing, in 1750 or 1850, which contemporaneous techniques would compound. The Bessemer process, on the surface, looked as narrow-base as a hundred prior metallurgical innovations; the eventual widening came from researchers Bessemer himself could not have predicted. This is a limitation Mokyr acknowledges but does not fully address — it makes the framework difficult to use as a forecasting tool for contemporary technology policy.

Second, the chapter on households is the freshest contribution but also the least empirically tested. The three-channel decomposition of mortality decline (income, prices, knowledge) is conceptually clean, but the actual quantitative weight Mokyr assigns to the knowledge channel is largely intuitive rather than estimated. Subsequent quantitative work has been mixed — the Szreter public-works channel and the McKeown nutrition channel both turn out to be more important than Mokyr suggests in some empirical settings, and the household-knowledge channel is harder to identify than the framework implies.

Third, the book is silent on what specifically widens an epistemic base. Mokyr is excellent on the consequences of wide vs. narrow bases but spends comparatively little time on the causal mechanism that takes a narrow base wide. Sometimes it is instrument-driven (microscope → bacteriology), sometimes mathematics-driven (Carnot’s thermodynamics), sometimes serendipitous (Faraday’s electromagnetism), sometimes institutional (Liebig’s research school). A more explicit theory of epistemic-base widening would strengthen the framework considerably.

Fourth, the political-economy chapter understates the cases where regulation has been technologically productive rather than obstructive. Mokyr’s emphasis on Cardwell’s Law and rational resistance is correct as a baseline, but the FDA, environmental review, and even occupational safety regulation have arguably accelerated certain kinds of technology (drug efficacy testing, pollution-control technology, automation that reduces injury) by raising the price of non-compliance. The aggregation-rule framework can accommodate these cases but Mokyr’s narrative does not develop them.

A fifth, lower-order critique is that the book’s tone is at times more oracular than the formal framework warrants. The closing observation that Athena’s gifts include both the olive tree and the wooden horse is well-earned, but several passages elsewhere read more as gestures toward Western exceptionalism than as careful empirical claims. Read alongside Joel Mokyr - A Culture of Growth, the framework becomes considerably more disciplined.

Links to other books

  • Joel Mokyr - A Culture of Growth — the sister book and the natural companion volume. A Culture of Growth (2016) develops the cultural and institutional preconditions of the Industrial Enlightenment that Gifts of Athena (2002) takes as given. Read Gifts first for the apparatus, then Culture for the historical case study; or read Culture first for the narrative motivation, then Gifts for the formal mechanism. They are designed to be read together.
  • Anton Howes - Arts and Minds — Howes’ Society of Arts is the practical-improvement institution that crystallized Mokyr’s Industrial Enlightenment in eighteenth-century Britain. Mokyr supplies the abstract epistemic-base apparatus; Howes supplies the on-the-ground institutional case study and the “improving mentality” propagating through middling-class artisans.
  • Robert J. Gordon - The Rise and Fall of American Growth — Gordon’s Special Century (1870–1970) is the downstream consequence of the wide-epistemic-base regime Mokyr describes. Read together, the books form a 500-year arc: Mokyr explains how the West acquired the capacity for sustained innovation; Gordon explains what those innovations did to standards of living and why the Special Century may be unrepeatable. Gordon’s “headwinds to growth” can be read in Mokyr’s terms as a partial collapse of the wide-base regime.
  • David Deutsch - The Beginning of Infinity — Deutsch’s defense of the Enlightenment as a tradition of “good explanations” is essentially Mokyr’s tightness-of-Ω thesis abstracted to a meta-philosophy of progress. Where Mokyr is the historian, Deutsch is the philosopher of the same phenomenon.
  • Will and Ariel Durant - The Lessons of History — the Durants’ civilizational arc complements Mokyr’s case; their generalizations about the role of religion, the dialectic of conservatism and innovation, and the role of contingency apply directly to Mokyr’s narrower European case. Mokyr’s framework gives the Durants’ grand history a tractable causal mechanism in the form of Ω growth.
  • Hamilton Helmer - 7 Powers — Helmer’s “Cornered Resource” (a body of asymmetric assets that confers persistent strategic advantage) maps onto Mokyr’s account of useful knowledge as the West’s compounding edge for two centuries. The fit is slightly forced (Helmer is writing about firms, Mokyr about civilizations), but the shared insight is that durable advantage comes from cumulative know-how that is costly to replicate without the supporting institutional substrate.
  • Leslie Berlin - The Man Behind the Microchip — the modern instantiation of the Mokyr feedback loop. Bob Noyce’s Silicon Valley combined the wide-epistemic-base regime (solid-state physics, semiconductor chemistry, integrated-circuit topology) with an institutional substrate (Stanford, Xerox PARC, the venture-capital ecosystem, open-information firm culture) that closely resembles a twentieth-century Industrial Enlightenment in miniature. Reading Berlin and Mokyr together makes the analogy precise.

Research uses

For research on the economics of innovation, the Ω/λ framework supplies a sharper apparatus than the standard endogenous-growth model. Researchers studying why some industries innovate continuously while others crystallize should ask: how wide is the current epistemic base? How tight? What instruments or methodological advances might widen it? What institutional arrangements lower the access cost? The framework is particularly useful for distinguishing industries where R&D will compound (wide-base, tight) from industries where R&D will hit diminishing returns (narrow-base, loose).

For research on AI and computing strategy, the analogy to the eighteenth century is sharp. The current moment looks more like 1660 than like 1860 — a technically sophisticated elite, fragmented institutions, an open scientific culture under stress from commercial enclosure, and uncertainty about whether the institutional substrate will survive. The Mokyr framework asks: which contemporary institutions play the role of the Republic of Letters, which AI techniques have wide vs. narrow epistemic bases, and what determines whether the current AI cycle will compound (Industrial Enlightenment 2.0) or crystallize (another fifteenth-century-style burst that fizzles when the easy gains are exhausted)?

For research on household behaviour change (smoking cessation, vaccination uptake, dietary reform, financial literacy), chapter 5’s recipes-and-priors framework is unusually portable. The three-channel decomposition (income, prices, knowledge) is directly usable; the asymmetry between firm-level competition and household-level inertia explains why behaviour-change interventions need to work harder than market-driven technology adoption.

For research on technology policy and political economy, chapter 6’s aggregation-rule framework gives a vocabulary for talking about regulatory capture, NIMBY blocking, ESG-style veto-points, and the precautionary principle. The pluralism-with-coordination thesis suggests where to look for institutions that escape Cardwell’s Law and where to expect the gradient back toward it.

Open questions

  • Can the Ω/λ framework predict, prospectively, which contemporary techniques will compound and which will fizzle? Or is it inevitably a retrospective tool?
  • What specifically widens an epistemic base? Is there a useful taxonomy of epistemic-base-widening events (instrument-driven, mathematization-driven, institutionally-driven, serendipitous)?
  • How does the framework apply to information technology and software, where the distinction between Ω and λ is fuzzier — software is both a technique and a description of itself?
  • Does the framework apply to social technologies — institutions, governance forms, organizational designs — or only to technologies that manipulate physical nature?
  • How robust is the chapter-5 quantitative claim about the household-knowledge channel of mortality decline against modern econometric scrutiny?
  • Is the modern Western pattern of permissive aggregator rules already eroding, and if so, at what rate?
  • What is the contemporary analogue of the Republic of Letters, and is it being weakened by commercial enclosure (private AI labs) or strengthened by the internet?
  • Is there a meaningful asymmetry between the kinds of Ω that the Mokyr framework captures well (physical natural philosophy) and the kinds it captures poorly (formal mathematics, social science, biology before genetics)?

What I would miss if I only read this note

I would miss the formal precision of Mokyr’s apparatus. The actual definitions of Ω and λ, the careful treatment of the epistemic-base relationship, the distinction between tightness and truth, the marginal-vs-average access-cost decomposition, the discovery-vs-invention split, and the competence-vs-epistemic-base distinction are all worth reading in Mokyr’s own words because they form a coherent toolkit. A summary that just gestures at “knowledge what vs. knowledge how” loses most of the analytical power.

I would also miss the density of historical example. Gifts of Athena is short — about 350 pages — but it is packed with concrete cases: Cort’s puddling, Bessemer’s blast, Carnot’s Reflections on the Motive Power of Fire, Holmes-vs-Lister-on-antisepsis, Appert’s champagne-bottle preservation, Edison’s Menlo Park, the Society of Arts’s prize lists, Wedgwood’s correspondence with Lavoisier, Davy and Faraday at the Royal Institute, the Cyfarthfa ironworks at 5,000 employees in 1830. The cases carry the argument in ways a summary cannot.

Finally, I would miss the methodological self-discipline that distinguishes Mokyr from his neighbours in cultural and intellectual history. He acknowledges where his synthesis depends on the work of others (Cardwell on the law of stagnation, Boyd-Richerson on cultural evolution, Greif on moral entrepreneurship, McCloskey on bourgeois virtues, Margaret Jacob on industrial culture, Slack on improvement). He is explicit about where the framework is hard to falsify. He resists the temptation to read the modern West as the inevitable culmination of human history. The book reads less like a polemic and more like an apparatus on offer to anyone who wants to think carefully about why some bursts of innovation compound and most do not. The closing reference to Athena’s gifts including both the olive tree (Athens) and the wooden horse (Troy) is the kind of grace note that earns its sentiment by being embedded in 350 pages of careful argument.
