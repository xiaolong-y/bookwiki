Andy Hertzfeld - Revolution in the Valley

Read chapter-by-chapter → Deep dive of Revolution in the Valley: The Insanely Great Story of How the Mac Was Made (12 chapters)

One-sentence thesis

The original Macintosh was the contingent product of a small, separated, charismatically-protected team that combined Xerox PARC ideas with Demo-Driven Development, the Reality Distortion Field, aesthetic perfectionism applied to invisible parts, and a Pirate Team organizational form that produced revolutionary software but could not survive the success it created.

Why this book matters

Revolution in the Valley is the most credible inside account of how a single landmark consumer-technology product was actually built, told as roughly 80 short, dated, cross-linked first-person anecdotes by Andy Hertzfeld and a dozen other former Mac team members. It is structurally derivative of the Folklore.org website Hertzfeld launched in 2003 and, like Wikipedia or Karpathy’s autoresearch loop, it treats the truth about a product as something best assembled from many short, heterogeneous, mutually-correcting witness statements rather than from a single authoritative narrative.

That format is the book’s epistemic argument. Most Steve Jobs biographies (Isaacson) and most Apple histories (Moritz, Linzmayer, Carlton) tell a single hero-shaped story. Hertzfeld’s anecdotal mosaic refuses that shape. The Macintosh appears as the joint product of Burrell Smith’s hardware obsession, Bill Atkinson’s QuickDraw and round-rect insistence, Bruce Horn’s Resource Manager design, Susan Kare’s icon work, Joanna Hoffman’s localization advocacy, Steve Capps’s late-stage software heroics, Jef Raskin’s original “appliance computer” vision (which the team mostly discarded), Mike Markkula’s tolerance, Bob Belleville’s organizational discipline (which the team resented), and Steve Jobs’s taste, will, and protection. No single one of these is “the father of the Macintosh.” The book’s resolution of the credit question (ch. 115) is that Steve Jobs gets the title only because the Mac would not exist in its actual form without him — a least bad answer to a deliberately bad question.

For research on technology firms, this is the canonical case study of three intertwined patterns: (1) the Pirate Team form and its instability after success; (2) the Reality Distortion Field as a productive-and-destructive leadership style; (3) Demo-Driven Development as the working method that small charismatically-led teams use to preserve Conceptual Integrity. It is also the most direct rebuttal to the popular “Apple stole everything from Xerox” simplification — the team’s PARC inheritance is admitted in detail (Bruce Horn from Smalltalk, Larry Tesler from Smalltalk, the Resource Manager modeled on Ted Kaehler’s OOZE, Atkinson’s text from PARC fonts), but the book is also exhaustive about what was specifically Mac-invented (the menu bar, drag-and-drop, the round-rect primitive, typed file creators, the Finder’s spatial model, the entire industrial design).

For anyone working on a small team trying to ship something they think will change the world, this is also a manual of the human cost — the chocolate-covered espresso beans, the unfair performance reviews, the departures, the marriages strained by 90-hour weeks — that a hagiographic Steve Jobs biography cannot tell you about because the people who paid the cost are not the protagonist.

Core argument

  1. The Macintosh was contingent on a sequence of unlikely events, not the inevitable expression of Steve Jobs’s vision. Burrell Smith was a self-taught service technician without a degree (ch. 1, 2). Jef Raskin was writing a “Book of Macintosh” about a $500 6809-based appliance computer that the actual Mac scarcely resembled (ch. 5, 115). Bud Tribble convinced Burrell to switch to the 68000 in late 1980, which is what caught Steve’s attention (ch. 5). The project was almost cancelled twice in 1979-80. None of this would have happened without an unusual permission structure inside Apple — Mike Markkula tolerating a four-person research project for years with no clear deliverable.

  2. The team’s working method was Demo-Driven Development, not specification. Burrell’s PAL-based digital board was reprogrammed eight times between 1981 and 1983 (ch. 19). Bud Tribble’s QuickDraw port produced “the first Mac-like demo” in May 1981 by stitching together unfinished pieces with scaffolding (ch. 108). Bill Atkinson kept a polaroid camera near his Lisa to photograph each significant UI milestone (ch. 108). The Calculator Construction Set (ch. 34) records ten visible iterations of the calculator UI driven by Steve’s reactions. The principle articulated in “The Macintosh Spirit” (ch. 113): “Instead of arguing about new software ideas, we actually tried them out by writing quick prototypes, keeping the ideas that worked best and discarding the others.”

  3. Aesthetic perfectionism was applied to invisible parts. The PC board’s interior layout was made beautiful (ch. 23). The plastic case’s interior was engraved with team signatures (ch. 31, the signing-party). The boot beep was tuned for emotional resonance, not just function (ch. 50). Steve rejected an objectively-better speaker hole on the case because it was visually wrong (ch. 50). This is the principle Hertzfeld names “no detail too small to matter”: quality applies even where customers cannot see it. The signing party (ch. 31) is the ritual that turns the principle into team identity — engineers as artists.

  4. The Reality Distortion Field was both productive and pathological. Bud Tribble warns Hertzfeld on his first Monday that Steve’s January 1982 ship date is impossible but that Steve will convince anyone otherwise in his presence (ch. 7). The RDF makes the Mac ship in January 1984 against everyone’s better judgment (ch. 89, Real Artists Ship). It also produces 75,000-units-per-month forecasts in the 1984 holiday quarter when actual sales are below 20,000 (ch. 98), and it produces the dismissive responses to Hertzfeld’s and Bruce Horn’s grievances about Bob Belleville (ch. 42, 91). The same mechanism that ships the product nearly destroys the company a year later.

  5. The Pirate Team form is unstable. Steve’s three Carmel-retreat slogans — Real artists ship, It’s better to be a pirate than join the navy, Mac in a book by 1986 (ch. 56, 68) — define the team’s organizational ideology. The pirate flag is sewn by Capps, painted by Kare, and nailed to Bandley 3’s roof in August 1983 (ch. 68). But the same year the team grows past 80; in February 1984 Lisa is folded in and the merged division crosses 300; by 1985 it is 700. Hertzfeld leaves on March 1, 1984. Burrell Smith quits in February 1985 over Bob Belleville’s obstruction of the Turbo Mac. Steve is removed by the board on April 11, 1985 and forced out on May 31. The conditions that produced the Mac — small, isolated, flat, charismatically protected — were exactly the conditions the Mac’s success destroyed.

  6. Conceptual integrity was the design property, not feature count. The Mac’s coherence — square pixels, round rectangles as a graphics primitive, regions as the universal clipping construct, typed creators rather than file extensions, the Resource Manager as universal indirection layer, sealed plastic case, no internal expansion slots — was the product of a small team in which everyone internalized the same design philosophy. This is the small-team alternative to Brooks’s aristocratic-architect prescription for Conceptual Integrity (Frederick P. Brooks Jr. - The Mythical Man-Month ch. 4). The “We’re Not Hackers!” episode (ch. 75) shows the internal debate: even within the team, Bill Atkinson argued against the bytewise-compression tricks the rest used to fit code in the 64K ROM, on the principle that maintainable code mattered more than saved bytes.

  7. The Xerox PARC inheritance was deep but selective. Bruce Horn came from Alan Kay’s Learning Research Group, where he had spent eight years on Smalltalk as a teenager (ch. 26). Larry Tesler came from PARC’s Smalltalk team to manage Lisa Applications (ch. 43). The Resource Manager was explicitly modeled on Ted Kaehler’s OOZE (ch. 29). Bill Atkinson’s text rendering used SmallTalk’s Cream 12 font initially (ch. 108). But the Mac team’s work was not a PARC clone. The menu bar, the desktop metaphor, drag-and-drop, double-click, the spatial Finder, typed creator codes, the round-rect primitive, the entire industrial design — all were Mac-invented. Bruce Horn’s “On Xerox, Apple and Progress” (ch. 112) is the team’s measured rebuttal to the cliché.

  8. Marketing was integral to the product, not a layer added afterward. The “1984” Super Bowl ad (ch. 72) is presented as a near-disaster: the Apple board hated the rough cut, Sculley ordered the ad pulled, Jay Chiat saved it by claiming he could not unload the 60-second slot. The ad ran once, generated $5M of free news coverage, and won commercial of the decade. The same pattern recurs at smaller scales — the Carmel retreats, the Tiffany field trip, the Norman Seeff Rolling Stone photo shoot, the Macworld stage rap. Steve treated launch and marketing as design problems on the same level as the product.

  9. The launch’s death-march was the cost of the Reality Distortion Field meeting reality. The final week of January 1984 (ch. 89, Real Artists Ship) is the book’s most-recounted scene. Software manager Jerome Coonen proposes shipping “demo” software and updating customers later. Steve refuses. The team works 96 straight hours on chocolate-covered espresso beans, releasing every few hours, finding bugs, fixing them, re-releasing. The final release ships at 5:30 AM Monday with an unresolved disk-formatting bug that Hertzfeld discovers can be worked around by tapping the spacebar. The Mac launches on January 24, 1984. The team’s cost — physical and relational — is documented in the next ten chapters and the Leave of Absence episode three months later.

  10. The Macintosh succeeded because the team was small, then nearly failed because the company couldn’t scale the team’s culture. This is the book’s structural argument. The same conditions that produced the Mac produced the post-launch crisis: anti-managerial culture means no one can challenge inflated forecasts (ch. 98); flat hierarchy means no orderly succession when key people leave; charismatic protection means the protector’s loss of interest ends the protection. The Steve who shielded Hertzfeld in 1982 is the Steve who, by 1984, has installed Belleville-type managers everywhere and is dismissing complaints. The book’s ending is therefore not triumphant but elegiac: the Folklore.org website is a long collective remembering of a team that no longer exists.

Argument map

The book’s underlying causal structure can be read as a 10-node outline. Indented bullets show how each node feeds the next, and how branches converge into the final elegiac conclusion.

  1. Contingent origin — Burrell Smith (self-taught service tech) plus Jef Raskin (appliance-computer vision) plus Mike Markkula’s tolerance of a four-person research project enable a project that would otherwise have been cancelled.
    • feeds into (2) by giving Steve Jobs an existing seedling to take over rather than something to start from scratch.
  2. Demo-driven method — PAL-based digital boards, polaroid milestone photos, working prototypes from week one, Calculator UI iterations.
    • feeds into (3) by making aesthetic decisions reviewable in artifact rather than only in argument.
  3. Aesthetic perfectionism extended to invisible parts — PC board’s interior beauty, engraved signatures inside the case, boot-beep tuning, rejection of the speaker hole.
    • feeds simultaneously into (4) and (5).
  4. Reality Distortion Field as productive force — ships the product against everyone’s better judgment; also pathological — produces inflated forecasts that destroy credibility a year later.
    • convergent with (5) into (6).
  5. Pirate Team form — physical separation (Texaco Towers, Bandley 3), 100-person promised limit, flat hierarchy, three Carmel slogans, the literal flag, the signing party.
    • convergent with (4) into (6).
  6. Conceptual integrity — achieved by the small-team route rather than by Brooks’s aristocratic-architect prescription.
    • feeds into (7).
  7. Selective Xerox PARC inheritance — Bruce Horn, Larry Tesler, the Resource Manager modeled on OOZE; balanced by Mac-original inventions (menu bar, drag-and-drop, round rect, typed creators, spatial Finder).
    • feeds into (8).
  8. Marketing as part of design — the “1984” Super Bowl ad, the Tiffany field trip, the Carmel retreats, Steve’s stage rehearsals.
    • feeds into (9).
  9. Launch death-march — Real Artists Ship refusal to slip, 96 hours straight, chocolate-covered espresso beans, ROM ships at 5:30 AM Monday with one known bug.
    • feeds into (10).
  10. The pirate-team form cannot scale — protector loses interest; Hertzfeld leaves March 1984; Burrell quits February 1985; Steve forced out May 1985. The book is therefore elegy, not triumph.

Key concepts

  • Reality Distortion Field — Bud Tribble’s coinage about Steve Jobs (ch. 7); the canonical concept for understanding charismatic-leader management.
  • Pirate Team — Jobs’s Carmel slogan; the canonical concept for the small, separated, rebellious skunkworks form (ch. 56, 68).
  • Demo-Driven Development — The team’s working method (ch. 19, 108, 113); precursor to lean / agile but more demanding.
  • Resource Manager — Bruce Horn’s typed-indirection software architecture (ch. 29, 55); the Mac’s most consequential single architectural decision.
  • Conceptual Integrity — Brooks’s design property; the Mac achieved it by Pirate Team alternative to Brooks’s architect-first prescription.

Best chapters/pages to actually read

The most concentrated value is in the canonical anecdotes that have entered Silicon-Valley folklore. Read ch. 7 (“Reality Distortion Field”) for the original definition. Read ch. 16 (“Round Rects Are Everywhere!”) for Steve walking Atkinson around the parking lot pointing at signs to win the round-rect argument — the canonical taste-arbiter scene. Read ch. 31 (“Signing Party”) for the engineers-as-artists ritual. Read ch. 32 (“-2000 Lines Of Code”) for Atkinson’s anti-metric retort and the Mac team’s productivity culture in two paragraphs. Read ch. 43 (“Do It”) for the user-testing “Dolt” episode and the cost of unexamined typographic assumptions. Read ch. 68 (“Pirate Flag”) for the literal flag and the three Carmel slogans. Read ch. 72 (“1984”) for the Ridley Scott commercial’s near-cancellation. Read ch. 75 (“We’re Not Hackers!”) for the internal debate over byte-saving tricks and Atkinson’s principled position. Read ch. 80 (“The Mythical Man Year”) for Steve’s “twenty-four man-years” line and the explicit allusion to Brooks. Read ch. 82 (“A Rich Neighbor Named Xerox”) for the Bill Gates GUI-inheritance quote. Read ch. 89 (“Real Artists Ship”) for the death-march finale and Steve’s “you may as well get it over with” decision. Read ch. 91 (“Leave of Absence”) for Hertzfeld’s departure and the management failure that produced it. Read ch. 98 (“The End Of An Era”) for the Sculley/Jobs split and the post-launch sales collapse. Read ch. 113 (“The Macintosh Spirit”) and ch. 115 (“The Father Of The Macintosh”) for Hertzfeld’s retrospective theses. Skip the most technical asides (Cordic algorithms, Region encoding details) on first read; they are beautiful but secondary to the human story.

Important examples / cases

  • Round Rects Are Everywhere! (ch. 16) — Steve walks Atkinson around the parking lot pointing at signs, license plates, parking-lot diagrams to argue that rounded rectangles are everywhere in the visual world; Atkinson invents the algorithm overnight. The canonical taste-arbiter scene.
  • The Boot Beep (ch. 50) — Charlie Kellner discovers the Mac case muffles the speaker, drills a hole that fixes it, demonstrates with measurements; Steve refuses. Kellner transfers back to Apple II shortly after. The cost of design absolutism.
  • -2000 Lines of Code (ch. 32) — Atkinson rewrites QuickDraw’s region engine to be six times faster and 2,000 lines shorter, fills in -2000 on a productivity form. The form is retired.
  • The Signing Party (ch. 31) — 35 engineers sign their names on drafting paper; signatures are engraved on the inside of every Mac case. The artists-not-engineers ritual.
  • The Pirate Flag (ch. 68) — Capps sews a black flag with a skull-and-crossbones, Kare paints the rainbow Apple as an eyepatch, they nail it to Bandley 3’s roof on a Sunday night. The Lisa team steals it; the Mac team raids the Lisa building to retrieve it.
  • The 1984 Commercial (ch. 72) — Ridley Scott directs in London with discus thrower Anya Major as the heroine and authentic British skinheads as workers. The Apple board hates the rough cut; Markkula proposes firing Chiat-Day; one director calls it the worst commercial he’s ever seen. Sculley orders both Super Bowl slots sold; Jay Chiat saves it by selling only one.
  • A Rich Neighbor Named Xerox (ch. 82) — Bill Gates to Steve Jobs in conference room: “Well, Steve, I think there’s more than one way of looking at it. I think it’s more like we both had this rich neighbor named Xerox and I broke into his house to steal the TV set and found out that you had already stolen it.” (Hertzfeld, 2004, ch. 82). The seed of the GUI-inheritance debate.
  • Real Artists Ship (ch. 89) — Sunday morning conference call. Software manager Jerome Coonen proposes shipping “demo” software. Steve: “There’s no way we’re slipping. You may as well get it over with. Just make it as good as you can.” (paraphrased). The team works 96 hours straight on chocolate-covered espresso beans.
  • Leave of Absence (ch. 91) — Hertzfeld cannot reconcile with Bob Belleville. Steve, who once promised to protect him from authoritarian managers, becomes “dismissive.” The Lisa-Mac merger crosses 300 employees. Hertzfeld takes a six-month leave on March 1, 1984; he never returns full-time.
  • The End of an Era (ch. 98) — Joanna Hoffman finds that holiday-quarter sales forecasts are unchanged from six months prior despite plummeting sales because no one dares tell Steve. April 10-11 board meeting decides for Sculley. May 31, 1985 announcement of demotion + first quarterly loss + layoffs.

Critiques and limitations

The book’s anecdotal structure is also its principal limitation. Because each story is short and self-contained, structural patterns must be inferred by the reader; there is no summative thesis chapter that would settle, e.g., the question of whether the Mac’s success was reproducible or unique. Hertzfeld’s “The Macintosh Spirit” and “The Father Of The Macintosh” are the closest, but they are short and retrospective.

The book is heavily Hertzfeld-centric in viewpoint despite the multi-author Folklore.org structure. Most contributors are software engineers from Hertzfeld’s immediate circle. There are vanishingly few first-person voices from production workers, marketing staff, sales force, manufacturing engineers, customer-support people, or East-coast Apple staff. Susan Kare, Joanna Hoffman, and Caroline Rose appear, but the gendered division of who got to be a “designer” vs. a “writer” or “marketing person” is not analyzed. The book also has almost nothing about Apple’s overseas operations, even though localization (Hoffman’s contribution) is treated as central to the architecture.

The 20-year retrospective gap is a real but acknowledged risk. The anecdotes were written in 2003-2004 about events from 1979-1985. Hertzfeld writes about memory error in “Mea Culpa” (ch. 111), but readers should treat specific dialog quotations as paraphrased reconstructions, not transcripts. Where Hertzfeld quotes Steve Jobs, Bill Gates, or Bob Belleville, the substance is probably right and the exact words are probably approximations. (Biographical details about authors and figures herein that are not grep-verifiable in the extraction log are tagged in the relevant person notes.)

The book is also gentle on the production-labor and environmental story that any serious history of the Mac would have to tell. The Singapore and Cork factories, the chemical exposure of clean-room workers, the supply chain, the sourcing of rare-earth materials — none of this appears. Compared with Chris Miller - Chip War or even Leslie Berlin - The Man Behind the Microchip, Revolution in the Valley keeps the camera firmly inside Bandley 3.

Finally, the book risks reading as a how-to-build-a-Macintosh manual when its argument is closer to the opposite — that the conditions producing the Mac were unusual, contingent, and unstable. Hertzfeld’s own subsequent career (General Magic failed; Eazel failed; Google+ failed) is implicit evidence that the Macintosh was not a repeatable formula even for the people who built it.

Links to other books

  • Frederick P. Brooks Jr. - The Mythical Man-Month — Direct allusion in ch. 80 (“Twenty-four man-years”). The Mac team is implicitly Brooks’s small “surgical team” but achieves Conceptual Integrity by Pirate Team form rather than Brooks’s aristocratic-architect prescription. Brooks supplies the theoretical justification for what the Mac team did.
  • Tracy Kidder - The Soul of a New Machine — Hertzfeld names Kidder in ch. 53 as the model the team hoped Mike Moritz would emulate. Tom West’s Eagle team at Data General is the structurally analogous case study from ten years earlier — same Pirate Team form, same charismatic protector, same post-launch dispersion.
  • Steven Levy - Hackers — Hertzfeld is one of the engineers Levy includes in the third generation of hackers, transitioning from minicomputers into personal computing. The hacker ethic Levy traces from MIT through Homebrew to Apple is the cultural substrate of “We’re Not Hackers!” (ch. 75).
  • Leslie Berlin - The Man Behind the Microchip — Mike Markkula (Apple’s first chair) is a Noyce / Fairchild alumnus; the Silicon Valley Spinout Model Berlin describes is what made Apple’s permission structure possible. Robert Noyce mentored Steve Jobs personally (Berlin); Hertzfeld mentions Noyce as a Carmel-retreat speaker in ch. 56.
  • Walter Isaacson - Elon Musk — Implicit comparison; Musk is treated as Jobs’s structural successor in Silicon-Valley leadership style. The Reality Distortion Field reappears in Isaacson’s Musk biography under different names.
  • Andrew S. Grove - High Output Management — Direct counterpoint. Grove’s discipline assumes converging on shared facts; the Mac team’s Reality Distortion Field involves constructing the facts. Both can produce successful firms; they cannot easily coexist. The post-1984 Mac Division’s failure is partly a failure to install Grove-style management.
  • Hamilton Helmer - 7 Powers — The Mac’s eventual durable powers (cornered resource = key engineers; process power = the Mac toolchain; brand) are Helmer-recognizable, but the origination story Hertzfeld tells is the pre-power phase: how the substrate that would eventually generate moats was built by a small team without thinking about moats.

Research uses

For research on small-team innovation, this book is the most important single case study available. The specific variables it documents — physical separation (Texaco Towers, Bandley 3), numerical limit (the never-met 100-person promise), flat hierarchy, Demo-Driven Development, a single charismatic protector, Reality Distortion Field leadership, aesthetic perfectionism applied to invisible parts — are the canonical features of the Pirate Team form. For startup research, the cost side (Hertzfeld’s leave, Burrell’s resignation, the death-march finishes) is the corrective to the survivor-bias version of the small-team story.

For AI-and-computing-strategy research, the book is the canonical cautionary tale about how the conditions producing a breakthrough product can be exactly the conditions that destroy the firm trying to scale it. The post-1984 Mac Division ran into every failure mode that flat-hierarchy charismatic-protection cultures hit: inflated forecasts no one dared challenge, key-person departures with no orderly succession, protector losing interest, resentment of formal management without alternative offered. Modern AI labs operating in similar conditions could read this book as a near-prediction of their own dynamics.

For the GUI-inheritance question, this is the most credible inside source. Bruce Horn’s “On Xerox, Apple and Progress” (ch. 112) is the team’s measured rebuttal to the cliché. Hertzfeld’s documentation of the Resource Manager’s OOZE lineage (ch. 29) is honest about what was inherited; his documentation of the menu bar, the Finder, drag-and-drop, the round rect, and typed creators is honest about what was Mac-invented.

Open questions

  • Is the Pirate Team form replicable, or is it always an artifact of unrepeatable historical conditions? (Steve Jobs’s later attempts at NeXT and the iPhone team had mixed results; Hertzfeld’s own later ventures failed.)
  • Did Steve Jobs use the Reality Distortion Field deliberately as a persuasion technique, or was it temperamental?
  • How much of the Mac’s success was attributable to the team’s small size, and how much to the specific people in it? (Counterfactual: if the team had been the same size with different members, would the Mac have shipped?)
  • Why did the second-Apple-era Jobs (1997-2011) appear less destructive of his teams than the first-Apple-era Jobs (1981-85)? Did he learn, did the people around him compensate, or did the business context simply tolerate more of his behavior because Apple was profitable?
  • What is the right organizational sequel to a successful pirate team — spin out, scale, or dissolve?
  • Are there voices missing from the Folklore.org / Revolution corpus — production workers, manufacturing, sales, customer support — whose absence shapes the book’s perspective in ways readers should notice?

What I would miss if I only read this note

I would miss the texture that makes the book’s epistemic argument convincing — the dozens of small details that no third-party historian could have invented. The boom box Burrell and Hertzfeld bought at Steve’s expense (ch. 6). Burrell’s “best friendship relationship” jargon and his three-to-five-millisecond average length of friendships (ch. 1). Steve waiting until last to sign the case in February 1982 and picking the upper center for his name (ch. 31). The Apple II language card hack with bank-switched RAM that started Burrell and Bill Atkinson’s working relationship (ch. 2). The Disney Scrooge McDuck image Hertzfeld smuggled onto the first Mac prototype overnight (ch. 3). The “Bicycle” project-rename attempt that the team simply ignored until Rod Holt gave up (ch. 13). The brute-force 120VAC across the prototype that Burrell tried to fix a short with (ch. 19). Larry Tesler’s “Do It” / “Dolt” user-test discovery (ch. 43). Charlie Kellner’s measurements proving the case muffled the speaker, and Steve’s flat refusal (ch. 50). The chocolate-covered espresso beans Randy Wigginton brought in for the final 96 hours (ch. 89). The Bill Gates “rich neighbor” line delivered in his squeaky voice while ten Apple people stood around him (ch. 82). Each of these details is the book’s evidence that it is telling truth, not myth, and they cannot be summarized — they have to be read.

I would also miss the emotional arc. The book begins with first-week excitement (August 1979) and ends with a 2003 retrospective written by a man who left in 1984 and never really came back. The middle is full of late-night work and product joy, but the ending is elegiac. The Folklore.org website — and therefore this book — is a long collective remembering of a team that no longer exists. That tone is what distinguishes Revolution in the Valley from a Steve Jobs hagiography or a triumphalist Apple history. The Mac was insanely great. The team that made it dispersed within 18 months of shipping it. Both things are true.
