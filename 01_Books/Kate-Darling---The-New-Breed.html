The New Breed: What Our History with Animals Reveals about Our Future with Robots

The New Breed: What Our History with Animals Reveals about Our Future with Robots

  • book
  • robotics

Kate Darling - The New Breed

One-sentence thesis

The dominant Western frame of “robot as quasi-human” is the wrong analogy and produces wrong conclusions about labor, liability, companionship, and rights; the historically accurate and ethically more useful analogy is “robot as domesticated animal” — an autonomous, sometimes unpredictable agent that supplements rather than replaces human capacities, sits squarely in someone’s purview, and whose harms have been governed by a thick body of pre-existing law since at least Hammurabi.

Why this book matters

Most robotics ethics books either chase the science-fiction superintelligence question (Bostrom, Tegmark) or focus narrowly on liability and product law. Darling does something different: she argues that the analogy itself is the bug. By substituting an animal-frame for the human-frame, she dissolves several pseudo-problems (the Asimov/trolley fixation, the panic over robot-replaced jobs, “electronic personhood”) and surfaces the real problems we are otherwise missing — emotional manipulation by companion robots, bias baked into anthropomorphic design, surveillance through chatty toys, and the moral-crumple-zone effect that lets companies offload blame onto human operators. For my reading on technology and institutions, this book is a model of how a single re-framing move can do more analytic work than a hundred new principles.

Core argument

  1. Robotics is currently theorized through a human-comparison frame. Mainstream Western science fiction (Frankenstein, Terminator, Ex Machina, Asimov), media coverage (“Will robots steal your job?”), and even academic legal scholarship implicitly cast robots as imperfect copies of humans whose progress is measured by closeness to human ability. Darling calls this default the “false determinism” of robot-as-quasi-human.
  2. This frame produces four predictable failure modes. It exaggerates the threat of total job replacement (when actual robotics is brittle and narrow per Rodney Brooks); it misroutes the responsibility-for-harm conversation toward “machine ethics” and Asimovian rule-stacks (which working roboticists do not take seriously per Maja Mataric, ch. 3); it stigmatizes emotional attachment to robots as a betrayal of human intimacy; and it pre-loads the rights debate with an assumption that robot-rights advocacy will track machine capability, which Darling argues the history of animal rights flatly contradicts.
  3. Animals are the better analogy because the structural features match. Like robots, animals (a) sense, decide, act, and learn, (b) perceive the world differently from humans, (c) supplement rather than substitute human skill (the honeyguide-Yao foraging partnership; the ox-drawn plow; pit ponies in mines; mine-detecting dolphins), (d) generate harm we cannot fully anticipate, and (e) admit a wide spectrum of human-nonhuman relationships from tool to weapon to family member. The animal frame is empirically richer than the human frame.
  4. Western legal history has already solved most of the “novel” liability questions. From the Code of Eshnunna and Hammurabi (ca. 18th century BCE) through the goring-ox rules of Exodus, Roman noxal surrender, English deodand, the trespass-with-beasts doctrine of Henry VII’s reign, the strict-vs.-foreseeable distinction between domesticated and wild animals, dog-license regimes, sheep funds, and “dangerous breed” registration in places like Vienna, we have a millennia-deep toolkit for assigning responsibility for autonomous nonhuman behavior. Darling lists explicit transposition candidates: robot registration, mandatory drone-pilot training, robot-class categorization (vacuum vs. autonomous car vs. armed underwater vehicle), insurance pools modeled on sheep funds, signage and disclosure, and Ryan Calo’s proposal for a federal robotics commission analogous to a state apiarist.
  5. The pig-trial precedent warns us against the alternative. Medieval Europe held formal trials for pigs, rats, weevils, and roosters, with defense counsel and ecclesiastical summons, because treating an autonomous nonhuman as a moral agent was a way to evade the puzzle of indirect responsibility. The 2016 European Parliament draft on “electronic personhood” repeats this logic. Darling reads it as a dangerous off-ramp: it lets corporations distance themselves from algorithmic harm, which her law-and-economics intuition reads as a catastrophic incentive failure.
  6. Companionate robotics is real, ethically loaded, and not the moral panic everyone is having. Section II shows that humans bond with robots (Sony AIBO funerals; the Jibo shutdown; people writing letters to Boomer the bomb-disposal robot; Roomba naming) in ways isomorphic to pet relationships. The animal frame normalizes this — humans have always extended sociality to nonhumans — but it also reveals what is actually worrying: subscription-cliff coercion (what if Sony charges $20K to keep AIBO running?), aggressive emotional marketing, anthropomorphic-design bias (the Caucasian-blonde-busty nurse avatar; female-coded subordinate voice assistants), privacy invasion via Hello Barbie / My Friend Cayla / smart-toy ecosystems, and Madeleine Clare Elish’s “moral crumple zone” deflecting blame onto human operators in human-machine teams.
  7. Robot-rights advocacy will not track machine capability — it will track empathy and selective bias, exactly as animal-rights advocacy did. The vivisection movement protected dogs because Mark Twain wrote a tearjerker about a dog, not because dogs are biologically special; Nazi Germany built the world’s strictest animal-cruelty laws while running death camps; the United States today protects hamsters in research while euthanizing surplus mice. Darling forecasts a similar empathy-driven, biologically-arbitrary patchwork for robot rights — and uses the analogy to argue that “but it’s not really conscious” will lose the policy fight to “but it looks sad.”
  8. Therefore: re-frame robots as a new domesticated species, not as a new failed human. This unlocks the full historical toolkit (legal, design, ethical, regulatory) and re-targets the policy conversation from “how do we punish the algorithm” to “how do we hold the puppet master accountable” — which, per Shoshana Zuboff, is where the real action has always been.

Key concepts

  • Animal-as-Analogy for Robots — the central re-framing move of the book; replaces the implicit human-comparison default
  • Anthropomorphization Bias — our tendency to project life and human stereotypes onto nonhuman moving agents; explains both companionate bonding and design-bias harms
  • Intentional Stance — adjacent to Darling’s design-ethics framing; she treats anthropomorphic projection as a practical liability problem rather than (as Dennett would) a philosophically interesting attribution puzzle
  • Moral Crumple Zone — Madeleine Clare Elish’s term for how human-robot teaming systematically deflects blame onto the human operator, screening corporate liability (concept-note candidate, not yet promoted)
  • Android Fallacy — Neil Richards and Bill Smart’s term for the courts’ habit of analogizing robots to humans when picking legal doctrines, with privacy-law (postcard vs. letter) consequences (concept-note candidate, not yet promoted)
  • Frankenstein Complex — Asimov’s label for negative cultural attitudes toward autonomous artifacts; Darling treats it as a culturally-specific Western frame contrasting with Japan’s Astro Boy lineage (concept-note candidate, not yet promoted)

Best chapters/pages to actually read

  • Introduction (ch. 1) — the cleanest statement of the analogy-substitution thesis. Frankenstein-Ingolstadt-Audi opening, Sophia-Saudi-citizenship as foil, the explicit move from “robots will replace us” to “robots will supplement us as animals always have.” Read this before anything else; it is the operating system for the rest of the book.
  • Ch. 3 “Trespassers” (responsibility for autonomous decisions) — the densest legal-historical chapter. Goring-ox rules from Eshnunna and Hammurabi, Roman noxal surrender, English deodand, Henry VII trespass-with-beasts, Vienna dangerous-dog regulation, sheep funds, and the medieval animal trials (Basel rooster 1474; St. Julien weevils 41-year case; Bartholomy de Chassenée’s rat-summons defense, ca. 1508). Closes with the Wanda Holbrook factory-arm fatality (2015) and the Tay/Tesla autopilot/moral-crumple-zone passages. This chapter alone will give you 80% of Darling’s policy toolkit.
  • Ch. 7 “The Real Issues with Robot Companionship” — the chapter that flipped my reading-list priorities. The Maddy-Jibo letter, AIBO subscription pricing, ELIZA, Hello Barbie/My Friend Cayla privacy violations, the female-voice-assistant gender research (BMW 5 Series recall; Nass and Yen), the Caucasian busty-nurse avatar default, Boxie the cute lab-extracting-data robot, and the Zuboff “puppet master” pivot. This is where Darling stops being descriptive and becomes prescriptive.
  • Ch. 9 “Free Willy” — the empathy-drives-rights-not-philosophy chapter. Frances Power Cobbe and the British Union for the Abolition of Vivisection; the Brown Dog Affair; Mark Twain’s “A Dog’s Tale”; Nazi animal-protection laws against the angora-rabbit-Buchenwald backdrop; selective-empathy mechanics. Indispensable for predicting how robot-rights advocacy will actually play out.
  • Final Thoughts — short and quotable. Amara’s Law as epigraph, the explicit rejection of human-replication as a goal, the prescription “look to the puppet master rather than the puppet.”

Important examples / cases

  • The Wanda Holbrook fatality (2015, Grand Rapids MI): factory robotic arm crossed safety-cell boundary and fatally struck a robot technician; lawsuit named five companies. Darling uses it to expose how media headlines (“Rogue Robot Blamed”) mis-apply human-agency framing to industrial product-liability cases.
  • The 1474 Basel rooster trial: a rooster prosecuted for the offense of laying an egg (basilisks were believed to hatch from rooster eggs); given a defense attorney; burned at the stake. The reductio illustrating where the moral-agent-for-robots line of argument leads.
  • Bartholomy de Chassenée’s rat defense (ca. 1508): French lawyer assigned to defend rats accused of destroying barley crops in Lucenay; argued the summons hadn’t reached all defendants; argued the trip to court was unsafe due to cats; later played by Colin Firth in The Hour of the Pig (1993).
  • Hello Barbie and My Friend Cayla (2015-17): Wi-Fi connected dolls that recorded child speech to Mattel servers; Darling’s own test produced an email “Your kid said something awesome this week!” with audio. Cayla was banned in Germany as an illegal surveillance device.
  • The Jibo shutdown (~2018): company insolvency disabled cloud-tethered home robots; eight-year-old Maddy wrote a letter offering her piggy-bank money to save the company. Darling’s worked example of subscription-cliff emotional coercion risk.
  • Vienna “dangerous dog” regime: pit bulls and rottweilers require licensed owners, mandatory training, sober-handling rules with the same blood-alcohol limit as drivers, and confiscation after a second violation. Darling’s preferred template for differentiated robot regulation.
  • Honeyguide-Yao partnership (southern Africa): zoologist Claire Spottiswoode’s research on humans and Indicator indicator birds locating beehives together for as long as ~1.9 million years. The book’s central image of supplementation rather than replacement.
  • Nazi angora-rabbit project (Buchenwald): SS personnel raised pampered rabbits next to starving prisoners; uncovered by Sigrid Schultz of the Chicago Tribune. Darling’s most extreme case for the selective-empathy thesis underlying animal/robot rights.

Critiques and limitations

The animal analogy is doing more work than Darling sometimes admits. Robots scale, copy, and update; animals do not. A precedent built for one ox in one Mesopotamian field does not obviously generalize to a fleet of identical autonomous cars whose shared software bug can produce 10,000 simultaneous failures — a regime closer to drug-recall or class-action product law than to the goring-ox rule. Darling acknowledges this in passing (ch. 3 closing pages) but never builds the corresponding doctrinal scaffolding. Second, the book is heavily Western-legal and Western-cultural; the brief contrasts with Japan (Astro Boy, sekkusu shinai shōkōgun) and the dog-meat-trade footnote are gestures, not full comparative analysis, and a serious cross-cultural treatment of robot integration in, say, Chinese or Gulf-state contexts would change parts of the argument. Third, the rights-trajectory chapter (ch. 9) succeeds as descriptive history but the prediction “robot rights will be selective and empathy-driven” is hedged enough to be unfalsifiable. Fourth, on the labor question, Darling underweights the aggregate job displacement argument — Brynjolfsson, Acemoglu, and others have documented that even narrow-specialist automation can hollow out middle-skill labor without ever reaching general intelligence; “robots are narrow” does not by itself defuse the labor concern. Finally, the animal frame may itself anthropomorphize — animals are sentient; robots, as Darling concedes, “suffer no differently than a kitchen blender.” The companion-robot ethics chapter occasionally conflates “robots are like animals to us” with “robots have animal-like moral standing,” which are different claims.

Links to other books

  • Shared concept Max Bennett - A Brief History of Intelligence — both books treat animal cognition as the right reference class for thinking about machine intelligence; Bennett supplies the evolutionary-neuroscience scaffolding Darling’s analogy presupposes.
  • Disagreement with Daniel C. Dennett - The Mind’s I — Dennett (and Hofstadter’s curated set) treat anthropomorphization of machines as a philosophically interesting puzzle about consciousness and the intentional stance; Darling treats it as a practical liability and design-ethics problem to be managed, not a metaphysical question to be solved.
  • Extends Sebastian Mallaby - The Infinity Machine — Mallaby’s history of compute and AI investment leaves the social-integration question open; Darling fills the human-robot-interaction gap downstream of the technology Mallaby narrates.
  • Disagreement with J. Storrs Hall - Where Is My Flying Car — Hall reads slow technology adoption as primarily a regulatory/cultural failure to be overcome; Darling reads it as substantively shaped by valid public concerns about autonomous-agent harm, and treats regulation as a legitimate inheritance to be mined rather than a brake to be released.
  • Shared concept Marvin Minsky - The Society of Mind — Minsky’s “society” framing of cognition and agency is the conceptual ancestor of treating robots as quasi-creatures rather than tools; Darling does for the social/legal frame what Minsky did for the cognitive frame.

Open questions

  • How should the animal analogy be modified for the scaling features unique to robots (software-update simultaneity, copyability, networked failure modes) that have no animal precedent?
  • If robot-rights policy will track empathy rather than capability, is design ethics obligated to suppress empathy-eliciting features in robots that should remain tools, or cultivate them in robots that should be treated as companions? Darling implies both at different points.
  • The “moral crumple zone” finding suggests blame deflects onto humans-in-the-loop. What does this imply for the design of intentional human-in-the-loop systems where the human’s role is partly to absorb liability?

Research uses

For my reading on technology and institutions, this book is most directly useful as (a) a case study in how a single analogy substitution can dissolve an entire literature’s pseudo-problems — relevant to my interest in how framings lock in and unlock policy options; (b) a usable legal-history toolkit for thinking about autonomous-agent regulation, applicable to any domain where “novel technology demands novel doctrine” is being asserted (driverless cars, drone delivery, generative-AI deployment, even algorithmic trading); (c) a corrective on the labor-replacement narrative that supplements the more macro Brynjolfsson/Acemoglu treatments — Darling’s robotics-floor-level account of what robots actually fail at is a useful reality-check for technology-pessimism takes; (d) a model for ethics writing that resists both moral panic and techno-utopianism. The “puppet master, not puppet” frame is portable to many other technology-policy debates I care about, particularly around platform liability and the agentic-AI safety conversation.

What I would miss if I only read this note

The book’s distinctive voice — Darling writes as a working researcher who has been the only feminist in too many robotics-conference rooms, and the personal anecdotes (the Munich Asimov-mansplainer, the dinosaur-costume meeting with Rodney Brooks, the horse-whispering class where she joked about eating horses to mortified Americans, her Lyft driver Debbie wondering when she’d be replaced) carry a lot of the book’s argumentative texture: the dry irony, the willingness to be embarrassing for the sake of clarity, and the working-roboticist-adjacent perspective that gives the analogy its credibility. The note also cannot reproduce the cumulative effect of the case-after-case structure — by the end of ch. 3 the reader has absorbed a dozen legal precedents in a way no summary table can replicate, and by the end of ch. 9 the empathy-and-bias thesis lands by sheer accumulation. Finally, the deep historical anecdotes (Abuwtiyuw the named Egyptian dog, Frederick the Great’s prisoner-exchange for his Italian greyhound, the Hour of the Pig film) are exactly the texture that make the animal analogy stick rather than feel forced.
