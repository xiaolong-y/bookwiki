Max Bennett - A Brief History of Intelligence

One-sentence thesis

Intelligence has emerged in five evolutionary breakthroughs — steering (first bilaterians), reinforcing (first vertebrates), simulating (first mammals), mentalizing (first primates), and speaking (first humans) — each adding a distinct computational capability to its predecessors, and modern AI’s task can be sharpened by asking which of these capabilities a given system has and lacks.

Why this book matters

A 2023 popular synthesis that does what most AI books do not: ground the question “what is intelligence?” in 600 million years of nervous-system evolution and in the specific computational mechanisms that biology has discovered. Bennett’s five-breakthrough taxonomy is genuinely useful — it cuts through the “AGI yes/no” sloganeering by making intelligence an additive set of distinct capabilities (a system can be a great steerer and a poor mentalizer; a great reinforcer and a poor simulator). It also closes the cybernetic-AI loop: Bennett uses the Pitts–McCulloch lineage (Warren S. McCulloch - Embodiments of Mind), reinforcement learning, and the Minsky–Papert symbolic-AI tradition (Marvin Lee Minsky - Computation Finite and Infinite Machines, Marvin Minsky and Seymour Papert - Perceptrons) as the pieces of his story; A Brief History of Intelligence is in effect the modern survey that ties batch 26’s neuroscience-AI books together. For Yang’s research uses, it is also the clearest mid-2020s primer on how current LLMs (ch. 22) fit into the longer evolutionary picture, and the most readable exposition of the Sutton-Barto Temporal Difference Learning / dopamine convergence (ch. 6) outside of Sutton & Barto’s textbook.

Core argument

Bennett’s argument is structured as a single phylogenetically-ordered claim with eight numbered components. Each numbered component carries one breakthrough’s biological setting, computational pattern, and modern AI counterpart; the cumulative point is that the popular question “is X intelligent?” is malformed and the productive question is “which of the five breakthroughs does X instantiate?“.

  1. The history of intelligence is best told as a sequence of evolutionary breakthroughs — discrete enough to teach, gradient enough to be honest about — each adding a computational capability that previous lineages lacked (Introduction). Bennett rejects MacLean’s triune brain model — “reptile brain” plus “limbic system” plus “neocortex” — as both anatomically wrong (reptiles already have a limbic system) and functionally too coarse to support reverse engineering (Introduction, “The Myth of Layers”). Bennett’s substitute partition is computational rather than anatomical.
  2. Breakthrough 1 — Steering (first bilaterians, ~600 mya). A valenced world model (good vs bad), with a basal-ganglia-precursor that selects approach vs avoid in a worm-sized nervous system. Bennett’s exhibit is the nematode C. elegans — ~302 neurons, no eyes, but already steers up gradients of food and away from heat, light, and sharp surfaces (ch. 2). The first brain is an action-selection brain. Modern AI counterpart: early reactive robotics, e.g., the Roomba (ch. 2). (chs. 1-4)
  3. Breakthrough 2 — Reinforcing (first vertebrates, ~525 mya). Sutton-Barto Temporal Difference Learning via dopaminergic prediction-error signals; the brain learns to predict future reward and updates predictions when reward arrives unexpectedly. Bennett spends ch. 6 (“Evolution of Temporal Difference Learning”) on the Sutton dissertation (1984), Tesauro’s TD-Gammon (early 1990s), and Wolfram Schultz’s monkey dopamine recordings (1980s) — the convergence that Peter Dayan and Read Montague identified in the 1990s, in which Schultz’s data are exactly Sutton’s TD signal. The Cambrian explosion is the visible evolutionary consequence of this learning trick. (chs. 5-9)
  4. Breakthrough 3 — Simulating (first mammals, ~200 mya). The neocortex as a Generative Model that simulates futures, enabling Model-Based Reinforcement Learning, planning, episodic memory, and curiosity. Bennett’s signature evidence is Redish & Johnson’s place-cell recordings in rat hippocampus — when a rat pauses at a maze fork and toggles its head, its hippocampal place cells visibly play out the two future paths in sequence (ch. 12, “Mice in the Imaginarium”). This is direct experimental confirmation of Tolman’s 1930s “vicarious trial and error” — and is the cleanest existing demonstration that a non-human mammal really does run forward simulations. (chs. 10-14)
  5. Breakthrough 4 — Mentalizing (first primates, ~30 mya). A model of other minds; the social brain enables coordination, deception, teaching, and political coalition. Bennett follows the social-brain hypothesis (Robin Dunbar): primate neocortex size correlates with social-group size, not foraging difficulty. The granular prefrontal cortex (gPFC), unique to primates, gets almost no direct sensory input — Bennett interprets it as constructing a generative model of the older mammalian aPFC’s generative model of intent, i.e., a model of one’s own model, which is the substrate for Theory of Mind. (chs. 15-18)
  6. Breakthrough 5 — Speaking (first humans, ~100 kya). Compositional language — the ability to externalize and combine arbitrary concepts — closes the loop between individual minds and a cumulative cultural record. Bennett’s surprising claim: Broca’s and Wernicke’s areas are not new; they exist in monkeys with practically identical connectivity. What is new is the hardwired learning curriculum in human infants — proto-conversations, joint attention, declarative labelling — that repurposes existing primate mentalizing tissue for language (ch. 21). Apes can learn rudimentary language because the cortical tissue is capable; what they lack is the instinct to engage in joint attention. (chs. 19-22)
  7. Each breakthrough is additive: humans retain bilaterian steering, vertebrate reinforcement, mammalian simulation, and primate mentalizing. Pathologies map onto the breakthroughs: anhedonic depression as B2 dysfunction; rumination as a B3 stuck loop; social anxiety as B4 hyperactivation. ChatGPT (ch. 22) inverts the order: it has Breakthrough 5 (statistical regularities of compositional language) without intact Breakthroughs 1–4 (no embodied valenced steering, no model-based simulation of physical consequences, fragile theory of mind).
  8. Therefore the question “is X intelligent?” is malformed; the productive question is “which breakthroughs does X instantiate?” This is the policy-relevant claim. The Conclusion (“The Sixth Breakthrough”) gestures at digital culture and artificial superintelligence as a possible sixth breakthrough, but Bennett is explicit that this is speculation and not part of the book’s evidence-supported claim.

Key concepts

  • Five Breakthroughs of Intelligence — Bennett’s organizing taxonomy
  • Model-Based Reinforcement Learning — the computational pattern of Breakthrough 3
  • Theory of Mind — the computational pattern of Breakthrough 4
  • Generative Model — closely tied to Breakthrough 3 (the neocortex as a generative simulator)

Best chapters/pages to actually read

The book is well-structured and roughly half can be skipped on a first pass. Bennett ends each Breakthrough section with a short standalone “[Breakthrough N] in retrospect” passage that gives the conceptual content of the preceding 4 chapters in 8-12 pages — these are the highest-leverage on-ramps if context is short.

  • Introduction — states the five breakthroughs cleanly, the Jetsons/Rosey framing, and the dishwasher-loading challenge. Read first.
  • Breakthrough 1 introduction + chs. 2-3 — the bilaterian / nematode / valenced-world-model story; Bennett’s “Roomba and the first bilaterian” parallel makes the action-selection point unforgettable.
  • Breakthrough 2 introduction + ch. 6 (“The Evolution of Temporal Difference Learning”) — TD learning explained for non-RL readers via the Sutton–Barto–Tesauro–Schultz–Dayan/Montague convergence. Single most pedagogically dense chapter and a better intro to TD than most ML textbooks for a reader who wants intuition first.
  • Breakthrough 3 introduction + chs. 11 (“Generative Models and the Neocortical Mystery”) and 12 (“Mice in the Imaginarium”) — the cleanest available popular summary of the generative-model view of cortex plus the Redish–Johnson place-cell evidence. Pairs naturally with Friston/Clark predictive-processing reading.
  • Breakthrough 4 introduction + chs. 15-17 (mentalizing, social cognition, gPFC) — the social-brain hypothesis and the bootstrapping-theory-of-mind-from-self-modeling argument.
  • Ch. 21 (joint attention and the language curriculum) — the strongest counter to “Broca’s area is what makes humans different.” The interesting move is locating human uniqueness in the learning instincts (joint attention, declarative labelling) rather than in cortical hardware.
  • Ch. 22 (“ChatGPT and the Window into the Mind”) — the modern-AI tie-in. Bennett’s diagnosis of what LLMs have and lack maps to the breakthroughs framework; the GPT-3 “I am in my windowless basement, and I look toward the sky, and I see [a star]” failure is a good worked example.
  • Conclusion (“The Sixth Breakthrough”) — speculative; worth reading because it explicitly asks what comes next and how to think about superintelligence in the framework’s terms.
  • Skip on first pass: chs. 5, 7, 8, 14, 18, 19, 20 (these are detail and supporting material; the breakthrough-introduction sections cover the conceptual content).

Important examples / cases

  • The nematode C. elegans (ch. 2) — Bennett’s first exhibit. ~302 neurons, no eyes, but steers up food gradients and away from light, heat, and sharp surfaces. The thinnest existence-proof of Breakthrough 1: action selection without a world model.
  • The lamprey basal ganglia — Bennett’s exhibit for the conservation of vertebrate-level circuitry. The lamprey, a jawless fish, already has the basic select/inhibit circuit that humans share — the structures (cortex, basal ganglia, thalamus, hypothalamus, midbrain, hindbrain) are remarkably similar across all modern vertebrates.
  • The Sutton–Schultz convergence (ch. 6) — the central pedagogical set-piece. Sutton (1984) proposed TD learning to solve the temporal credit-assignment problem; Schultz, working independently in the 1980s, recorded confused dopamine activity in monkeys (excited by reward-predicting cues, silent on expected reward, depressed when expected reward was omitted); a decade later Dayan and Montague at the Salk recognised that Schultz’s pattern is Sutton’s TD signal. The two fields had built the same algorithm.
  • The rat in the Imaginarium (ch. 12) — Redish and Johnson’s place-cell experiments. When a rat pauses at a maze fork, hippocampal place cells encoding future paths fire in sequence — direct experimental evidence that the rat is rendering forward simulations. This is the cleanest existing demonstration that mammals run model-based RL.
  • Andrew Ng’s helicopter acrobatics (ch. 14) — Abbeel, Coates, and Ng (2010) used inverse reinforcement learning to autonomously fly aerobatic maneuvers; Bennett uses this to illustrate model-based RL in modern AI.
  • GPT-3’s failures (ch. 22) — “I am in my windowless basement, and I look toward the sky, and I see a light, and I know that it is a star”; “He threw the baseball 100 feet above my head, I reached my hand up to catch it, jumped, and caught it”; “If 3x + 1 = 3, then x equals 1”. Each demonstrates the absence of an inner simulation that respects physics or arithmetic.
  • GPT-4 with chain-of-thought (ch. 22) — every commonsense question Bennett designed against GPT-3 is answered correctly by GPT-4. Bennett notes OpenAI did not add a world model; they trained the model with Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback and forced it to write out reasoning steps. The breakthrough framework’s response: GPT-4 is acquiring partial Breakthrough 3 by imitating the linguistic trace of human simulation, not by simulating itself.
  • Pinker’s “I’m leaving you” / “Who is she?” exchange (ch. 22) — Bennett uses Steven Pinker’s example to show how mentalizing is required to parse even mundane dialogue; the listener fills in an unspoken backstory by simulating Bob’s likely reason for the statement. (Steven Pinker - When Everyone Knows That Everyone Knows develops the same point at length.)

Critiques and limitations

  • The five-breakthrough taxonomy is rhetorically clean, empirically blurry. Real evolutionary transitions are gradual; the boundary between “first bilaterians” and “pre-bilaterian neural nets” is a million-year gradient, not a step. Bennett knows this and acknowledges it (Introduction footnote on gradualism), but the discrete-breakthrough frame may overweight crispness for pedagogical reasons. A reader inheriting the framework should resist the urge to argue about which species is the “first” anything; the breakthroughs are capability classes, not species labels.
  • Mammalian-cortex / generative-model story is one hypothesis among several. The active-inference (Friston) and predictive-processing (Clark) frameworks Bennett summarises in ch. 11 are not consensus; competing accounts (canonical microcircuit theories, deep-learning-style cortical models without explicit generative interpretation) exist. Bennett presents one view as if settled. Compare with the more cautious treatments in Daniel Johnston - Foundations of Cellular Neurophysiology (which holds biophysical grounding to a higher bar) and Simon Prince - Understanding Deep Learning (which treats generative models as a class of architectures, not a theory of cortex).
  • Causal claims about evolutionary order rest on phylogenetic reconstruction with substantial uncertainty. The ~600 mya bilaterian transition and the ~525 mya vertebrate transition are inferred-consensus dates; popular framing as crisp dates loses the error bars (typically tens of millions of years).
  • LLM diagnosis (ch. 22) is timestamped. The book argues LLMs lack Breakthrough 3 (simulation) and 4 (mentalizing); 2024-2026 LLMs with chain-of-thought, tool use, RLHF, and explicit world-modelling agents (e.g., agentic systems with planning loops) are partial counterexamples. The breakthrough framework survives the update; the specific diagnoses need refresh. Bennett himself flags this in the GPT-4 addendum at the end of ch. 22 — GPT-4 already passes most of the tests he wrote against GPT-3.
  • Origin-of-language story is contested. The joint attention is the human-unique part, not Broca’s area claim is provocative and well-argued, but it leans on the comparative chimpanzee literature (Tomasello) without surveying the alternative views (e.g., Chomskyan recursion-as-fundamental). A reader interested in the language story should triangulate.
  • The “Sixth Breakthrough” is genuinely speculative and labelled as such, but the chapter blurs the line between framework-grounded claims (B1-B5) and futurology (B6 = artificial superintelligence). This is fine if read as a coda; it is misleading if confused with the rest of the book’s evidence base.

Links to other books

  • Synthesises the lineage from Warren S. McCulloch - Embodiments of Mind — Bennett’s modern AI thread starts at Pitts–McCulloch 1943; the Logical Calculus of Neural Activity is the originating event.
  • Reinforcement-learning thread converges with Marvin Lee Minsky - Computation Finite and Infinite Machines — Minsky’s effective-procedure framework (Effective Procedure) is the formal infrastructure that Bennett’s TD-learning chapters take for granted; Minsky’s 1954 PhD thesis on neural networks is also explicitly cited by Bennett as the prehistory of Sutton’s TD work.
  • Symbolic-AI counterweight in Marvin Minsky and Seymour Papert - Perceptrons — Minsky and Papert’s perceptron critique is in the prehistory of why connectionism took until the 1980s to recover; Bennett’s TD story (ch. 6) inherits the post-AI-winter recovery context.
  • Cognitive-architecture continuity with Marvin Minsky - The Society of Mind and Marvin Minsky - The Emotion Machine — Minsky’s “society of agents” / “K-lines” / multi-resource architectures are an alternative, also-multi-component story for what Bennett packages as five breakthroughs. The Minsky books decompose the current mind; Bennett decomposes the evolutionary stack.
  • Educational implications adjacent to Seymour A Papert - Mindstorms — Bennett’s account of how mammals learn (model-based simulation) implicitly supports Papert’s constructionist claim that learning happens through simulation and manipulation, not passive transmission. (See Papert vs Bennett on Learning.)
  • Philosophy-of-mind triangulation with Daniel C. Dennett - Brainstorms, Daniel C. Dennett - The Mind’s I, and Daniel C. Dennett - I’ve Been Thinking — Dennett’s intentional-stance and multiple-drafts arguments about consciousness pair naturally with Bennett’s mentalizing-as-modelling-other-models account.
  • Cognitive-cousin to Steven Pinker - When Everyone Knows That Everyone Knows — Pinker’s common-knowledge and pragmatic-inference work is exactly the apparatus Bennett reaches for in ch. 22 (the “I’m leaving you / Who is she?” example is Pinker’s). Common Knowledge is the bridge concept.
  • Long-run AI / mind tie-in with Nick Bostrom - Deep Utopia — Bostrom’s exploration of post-instrumental human meaning operates in the world after a possible Sixth Breakthrough; Bennett’s book is its prehistory.
  • Quantum-mind adjacency with Carlo Rovelli - Helgoland — Rovelli’s relational interpretation of QM does not directly engage neuroscience, but the philosophical convergence (mind as relational pattern, not substance) sits alongside Bennett’s computational-functionalism.
  • Cellular-substrate grounding from Daniel Johnston - Foundations of Cellular Neurophysiology — Johnston’s biophysics is the bottom-up level that Bennett’s algorithm-level account assumes but does not derive.
  • Modern deep-learning textbook companion Simon Prince - Understanding Deep Learning — Prince formalises the architectures (CNNs, transformers, generative models, RL) that Bennett describes in popular language.
  • Vision-level relation to Licklider - Libraries of the Future — Licklider asked what a procognitive system should do for human researchers; Bennett’s evolutionary frame supplies the cognitive machinery (Breakthroughs 3 and 4) such systems need to support.

Open questions

  • The Sixth Breakthrough Bennett gestures at in the conclusion: is digital-cultural intelligence a real qualitative shift, or just Breakthrough 5 amplified? Cultural-ratchet theorists (Henrich) and AI-acceleration optimists give different answers; the framework itself does not settle the question.
  • What evolutionary gap explains animal language failures? Bennett’s account of why other primates lack productive compositional language (Breakthrough 5) rests partly on the FOXP2 story and partly on social-coordination preconditions (joint attention as the missing instinct). The cleanly comparative experimental work — apes raised with sign language (Nim Chimpsky, Washoe), dolphin and parrot vocalization studies, recent corvid studies — is not fully synthesised here.
  • Direct test of the additivity claim: if breakthroughs are genuinely additive, AI systems with Breakthrough 5 (LLMs) plus Breakthrough 4 (theory-of-mind training, RLHF) plus Breakthrough 3 (model-based RL via tool use, agent loops) should compose. Empirical research is mid-flight in 2024-2026; the breakthrough framework gives it a structured agenda.
  • Is “joint attention” the right gate, or a downstream effect? Bennett’s claim that the human-language difference is the joint-attention learning curriculum, not Broca’s area, is the strongest move in the language section. But it raises a chicken-and-egg question: is joint attention a primitive instinct, or is it itself enabled by some prior genetic change? Bennett gestures at FOXP2 but does not commit.

Research uses

  • AI-policy and AI-strategy frame. The which-breakthrough taxonomy is much more usable than the AGI/ASI binary in policy conversations. “This system has B5 and partial B3 but no B1 and weak B4” is a defensible diagnostic for any specific deployment, and it forces a granular conversation about which capability is dangerous in which context. For Yang’s tech-and-society / China-and-AI work, this maps directly to specific regulatory levers (e.g., agentic capability = partial B3, demands different oversight than pure B5).
  • Personal model of cognition. Bennett’s evolutionary stack is a useful mental model for one’s own cognitive architecture. Pathology mapping — anhedonic depression as B2 dysfunction (broken reward-prediction signal); rumination as a B3 stuck loop (the simulator running on default); social anxiety as B4 hyperactivation (over-modelling other minds) — is speculative but useful for self-diagnosis and for designing personal interventions.
  • Bridge between policy and neuroscience. For tech-and-society writing, Bennett gives a vocabulary in which arguments about AI capabilities are anchored to specific computational mechanisms rather than to capability sloganeering. Citing “GPT-4 acquiring partial B3 via chain-of-thought + RLHF” beats citing “GPT-4 is more general than GPT-3” because it specifies what kind of generality.
  • Evaluating procognitive AI tools. Combine with Procognitive System from Licklider — a productive AI research tool augments human B3 (simulation) and B4 (modelling other agents) rather than substituting for them. The breakthroughs frame helps separate “tool that augments simulation” (good) from “tool that replaces it without warning” (risky).
  • Research-question generation. The argument map’s third row (TD learning, generative models, theory of mind, language) is a list of AI subfields where neuroscience evidence is informative. For each, the book points to the specific empirical anchor (Schultz’s dopamine, Redish’s place cells, primate gPFC, joint-attention curriculum) — useful when looking for a thesis-scale neuroscience-AI question with concrete grounding.

What I would miss if I only read this note

The book’s narrative — Bennett is a vivid science writer, and the worked phylogenetic stories (lamprey, octopus, rat, monkey, infant human) are pedagogically dense in ways notes don’t capture. The book also has specific figures (brain anatomy by Mesa Schumacher; section-opener illustrations by Rebecca Gelernter) that compress concepts I cannot reproduce in text. The Schultz–Sutton convergence in particular is told as a multi-decade detective story across two fields, with named characters (Sutton, Barto, Tesauro, Schultz, Dayan, Montague), that is much more memorable in the original than in summary form. Finally, the rhythm of the five-breakthrough structure — each ~80 pages, each with its own introductory section followed by 4 chapters of detail — produces a kind of cumulative argument that reads better cover-to-cover than à la carte. Bennett’s biographical positioning matters too: he is a Bluecore co-founder and Alby founder (per the “About the Author”), not an academic neuroscientist, and his framing of the book as “I wrote this because I wanted to read this” (Introduction) lets him synthesise across primary literatures more boldly than a tenure-track author would. Reading the note alone gives the framework; reading the book gives the empirical texture and the why-this-particular-author-believed-this story.
