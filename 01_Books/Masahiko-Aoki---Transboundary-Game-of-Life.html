Masahiko Aoki - Transboundary Game of Life

Orientation: this is the primary-source memoir of the founder of Comparative Institutional Analysis (CIA). For Yang’s research it is unusually high-leverage — not as a casual biography but as a methodological text whose first chapter and final chapter define a research program (institutions-as-equilibria; transboundary methodological discipline) directly load-bearing for any China political-economy or comparative institutional analysis work. Read chs. 1, 13, 21, 22, 25, 26, 28, 31 first; chs. 2-12 supply the activist-to-economist biographical scaffolding that explains why Aoki rejected both Marxist orthodoxy and Anglo-American neoclassicism.

One-sentence thesis

Aoki frames his life as a sustained “transboundary game” — repeated institution-building ventures that each crossed a boundary (Marxist activism / mathematical economics; Japan / United States; pure theory / policy practice; East Asian fieldwork / formal model) — and argues that this boundary-crossing was not a personal eccentricity but the only methodological path by which a social scientist could escape the parochialisms that disable theory built inside any single tradition; the seven ventures (the Bund; Journal of the Japanese and International Economies; Stanford Japan Center; the Stanford CIA doctoral program; RIETI; CIDEG; VCASI) are presented as the operational shape this methodological commitment took.

Why this book matters

Aoki (1938-2015) was a central figure in twentieth-century institutional and comparative political economy: founding member of the Stanford Comparative Institutional Analysis program (1990), 1998 Joseph Schumpeter Prize, 2008-2011 President of the International Economic Association (succeeding Kenneth Arrow, Paul Samuelson, Amartya Sen), founding director of Japan’s RIETI think tank (1997-2001), Adjunct Professor at Tsinghua University (2003), Director of CIDEG at Tsinghua. His technical books — Information, Corporate Governance and Institutional Diversity (Oxford 2000), Toward a Comparative Institutional Analysis (MIT 2001), Corporations in Evolving Diversity (Oxford 2010) — are the formal apparatus the memoir contextualizes.

The memoir is a primary window on three things readers cannot get from his technical work alone. First, the intellectual genealogy of CIA: ch. 13 names the 1960 Arrow-Hurwicz “Decentralization and Computation in Resource Allocation” article as the moment Aoki shifted from Marxist political economy to mathematical economics, and ch. 25 traces the 1990 Stanford CIA founding to specific colleagues (Avner Greif, Paul Milgrom, Yingyi Qian, Marcel Fafchamps, John Litvack) and specific funding (NTT Data president Shirō Fujita). Second, the political-economy of academia itself: the Yingyi Qian tenure denial at Stanford (ch. 25) is one of the most candid published accounts of how research-program politics inside elite American economics departments shaped the boundary between mainstream and heterodox economics in the 1990s. Third, the institutional infrastructure of China-economics intellectual networks: ch. 21 (1976 visit to Closed China during the Gang of Four era) and ch. 28 (1994 Jinglun Conference with Zhou Xiaochuan, Lou Jiwei, Guo Shuqing, all later top Chinese financial regulators) document how Aoki’s Stanford program became one of the conduits through which Western institutional economics influenced China’s banking and corporate-governance reforms.

For readers of Meg E. Rithmire - Land Bargains and Chinese Capitalism, Meg Rithmire - Precarious Ties, and Angela Huyue Zhang - High Wire, Aoki’s CIA framework is the intellectual water their work swims in even when the cited apparatus is more recent. For readers of Joseph E. Stiglitz - Globalization and Its Discontents Revisited, Aoki’s memoir documents the Asian counterpart to Stiglitz’s Western-insider critique of Washington Consensus shock therapy — the World Bank “Corporate Governance in Transitional Economies” project Aoki helped lead (ch. 28, 1994) explicitly warned that simple privatization risked “insider control” asset-stripping, the same point Stiglitz makes about Russia. Compared to alternative biographical sources on twentieth-century institutional economists (Constance Reid - Hilbert, Constance Reid - Courant for the Göttingen-tradition mathematical predecessors; Sebastian Mallaby - The Power Law for Silicon Valley financial-institutional design), Aoki is the one who explicitly thematizes transboundary East-Asian / Western methodological work and treats it as the central organizing principle rather than a backdrop.

Core argument

The memoir is organized as a single integrated argument with seven moving parts:

  1. Career as repeated ventures rather than human-capital ramp. Ch. 1 frames a scholarly career as a sequence of institution-building bets (the seven ventures), each with uncertain outcome — “commitment to a new undertaking → sense of accomplishment at success, or withdrawal due to setbacks → reset” (ch. 1). This explicitly rejects the smooth-CV theory of academic life: failure and withdrawal are normal, not anomalous, and venture variance is the price of institution-creating ambition. The taxonomy is sharper than memoir cliché: Aoki distinguishes changing the rules of the game (the Bund’s anti-Security Treaty struggle; RIETI’s attempt to reform Japanese think-tank structure) from creating new spaces within existing rules (JJIE journal; Stanford Japan Center; CIDEG) from experimenting with new ways of playing (CIA, VCASI, ch. 31). Different venture types have different success criteria — successful “new spaces” are now operating routinely (JJIE, SJC, CIDEG); successful “rule changes” achieved goals their authors did not anticipate (the Anpo struggle ended Marxist-orthodox political organization in Japan even though “the Bund as an organization” was judged a defeat at the time, ch. 31).

  2. Comparative observation as macro-experiment substitute. Ch. 1 states the methodological premise: “for the field of social sciences, which does not allow experiments at the macro level, the knowledge gained by comparative observation helps to avoid arbitrary viewpoints in formulating theories” (ch. 1). This is a more demanding claim than the truism that international experience broadens horizons. Aoki argues that theory itself is structurally vulnerable to parochial assumptions when its author works inside one institutional context, and that genuine cross-cultural observation is the only available falsification mechanism — a position with operational consequences for Critical Rationalism applied to social science. He explicitly cites Benedict Anderson’s “vertigo” image and notes the asymmetry that integration of cross-cultural experience is psychologically expensive in ways that mere accumulation is not (the Jung-Kawai missionary anecdote, ch. 1).

  3. From Marxist activism to mathematical economics via Arrow-Hurwicz. Chs. 2-12 narrate Aoki’s involvement in the Anpo (anti-US-Japan Security Treaty) student movement: leadership in the Communist League “Bund,” month of solitary confinement at Sugamo Prison in 1960 (ch. 10), the June 15 Diet demonstrations and the death of Michiko Kanba, the post-defeat dissolution of Bund (ch. 11). Ch. 13 names the precise intellectual moment when Aoki “outgrew Marxism and shifted to mathematical economics that incorporated comparative system analysis” — the 1960 Arrow-Hurwicz “Decentralization and Computation in Resource Allocation” article, which Aoki interprets as resolving the Mises-Hayek-Lange debate over socialist planning theoretically in favor of decentralized market mechanisms because central planners lack the computing power and cannot induce truthful information revelation from corporations and consumers. The biographical pivot is methodologically substantive: Aoki carried into mathematical economics the question Marxism had asked (which institutional configuration delivers efficient resource allocation under what conditions) but adopted the formal apparatus that gave the question precise answers.

  4. The cooperative-game-theoretic firm and stakeholder governance. Ch. 22 describes Aoki’s 1976-1980 work modelling the firm as a cooperative-game between investors and labor, in which traditional Anglo-American shareholder firms and Yugoslav labor-managed firms appear as boundary cases (one or zero on a continuum). Aoki claims this is the foundation of what is now called “stakeholder theory of corporate governance” (ch. 22). The 1984 Oxford UP volume The Cooperative Game Theory of the Firm and the 1984 North-Holland Economic Analysis of the Japanese Firm (with Mervyn King, Harvey Leibenstein, Richard Freeman) are the technical anchors. Substantively, the chapter ties Hayao Kawai’s “hollow center structure” of Japanese mythology to Aoki’s economic model: Japanese executives historically functioned to maintain equilibrium between firm and investor interests rather than implementing a single principle (ch. 22), an analogy with the Kojiki’s Tsukuyomi-as-mediator-between-Amaterasu-and-Susanoo structure. Aoki is candid that the analogy is suggestive rather than identifying — the operational point is that one institutional configuration cannot be reduced to mere different “form” with the same “function.”

  5. The 1990 Stanford CIA program: Greif, Milgrom, Qian, Fafchamps, Litvack. Ch. 25 narrates the 1990 founding of Comparative Institutional Analysis as a doctoral field at Stanford Economics. Aoki names each founding member’s distinct contribution: Avner Greif tying game theory to medieval-trade historical documents; Paul Milgrom’s institutional-complementarity formalism (Aoki recalls Milgrom scribbling formulae on a paper napkin at a Stanford cafe); Yingyi Qian’s Sino-Soviet comparative work; Fafchamps’s African fieldwork on trust and reputation; Litvack’s planned-economy mechanism analysis. NTT Data president Shirō Fujita supplied the launch funding. Douglass North visited from Washington University each winter, won the 1993 Economics Nobel, and his presence “was a great encouragement to those of us in institutional analysis research” (ch. 25). The 1994 Beijing Jinglun Conference (ch. 28) connected the Stanford CIA group to the architects of China’s banking and SOE reform — Wu Jinglian, Chen Qingtai, Zhou Xiaochuan (later People’s Bank governor), Lou Jiwei (later China Investment Corporation chair), Guo Shuqing (later China Construction Bank chair), Premier Zhu Rongji. Aoki frames this as the CIA program’s policy demonstration of comparative institutional reasoning.

  6. The Qian tenure denial and the political economy of mainstream economics. Ch. 25 records Aoki’s “worst birthday in my life,” April 1, 1998, when the Stanford Economics Department voted against Yingyi Qian’s promotion to tenure despite “some dozen selected scholars in the front lines of research” recommending it. Aoki cannot disclose meeting content under American academic ethics, but states that “there were people outside the proceedings who suspected that the opposition was based on non-scholarly emotions against this international field, which was overwhelmingly popular among foreign students” (ch. 25). Qian moved to Berkeley, then became founding-era Dean of Tsinghua’s School of Economics and Management (founding Dean: Zhu Rongji, while serving as Chinese Premier). The CIA program at Stanford “discontinued its formal structure” after about a decade and is “being continued on a more compact scale by Greif” (ch. 25). Aoki interprets the episode as evidence of “cutthroat competition in American academia for the expansion of influence of research methods within various fields” — institution-building at the doctoral-program level is itself an Institution-Building as Mathematical Craft-style game with winners and losers. The candor about institutional competition is methodologically important: Aoki’s own framework explains why mainstream-economics resistance to CIA is itself a CIA-style equilibrium.

  7. Institutions are equilibria of integrated political-economic-norm games. Ch. 30 (Social Game and Virtual Research Institute) generalizes the framework: previous economics, sociology, and political science treated either society or individual as primary actor and the other as residual; CIA dissolves this chicken-and-egg debate by treating both as outcomes of repeated games whose equilibrium expresses the cyclical mutual constitution of individual and society. Aoki cites Plato, Hume, Smith (the moral philosopher), Huizinga (Homo Ludens 1938), von Neumann-Morgenstern, and Nash as predecessors. Ch. 31 (Conclusion) ties it back to the seven ventures: each is an attempt either to change rules, create new spaces under existing rules, or experiment with new ways of playing — and the trans-boundary game itself is the integrating frame. “Victory and defeat alternate ‘like the strands of a rope.’ If it were settled either way, there would be no point to living” (ch. 31). The closing line is autobiographical but operationally serious: institution-building under uncertainty is irreducibly path-dependent and the equilibrium-selection problem is not a defect of the framework but a feature of the social world.

Argument map

The reasoning of the memoir runs from political activism + Marxist political-economy through mathematical-economics methodological pivot via Arrow-Hurwicz to cooperative-game-theoretic firm and stakeholder governance to the 1990 Stanford CIA program with Greif, Milgrom, Qian, Fafchamps, Litvack to China-policy demonstration via the 1994 Jinglun Conference to RIETI’s bureaucratic-wall encounter to the trans-boundary-game generalization. The structural move is biographical — failure of one game (the Bund 1960) supplied the methodological hunger for a better-formalized equilibrium-of-multiple-games framework that the post-Marxist Aoki then spent forty years building both intellectually (CIA) and institutionally (seven ventures across Stanford, Tokyo, and Beijing).

Key concepts

  • Comparative Institutional Analysis — Aoki’s signature framework: institutions as equilibria of repeated games, multiple equilibria + complementarities explain cross-country diversity, requires comparative-historical fieldwork because game theory alone underdetermines the equilibrium.
  • Transboundary Scholarship — Aoki’s master metaphor: boundary-crossing as methodological discipline with real psychological cost; institution-building as scaffolding that protects integrated work from disintegration.
  • Mechanism Design — Aoki’s seven ventures are mechanism-design exercises in the institutional sphere; ch. 13’s Arrow-Hurwicz revelation is foundational to mechanism-design thinking.
  • Game Theory Concepts — Nash equilibrium, repeated games, and equilibrium-selection are the formal apparatus CIA imports.
  • Subnational Comparative Political Economy — within-country variant of CIA that holds country fixed and treats sub-national institutional variation as primary; Rithmire’s three-city Northeast China design is the cleanest example.
  • Institution-Building as Mathematical Craft — Klein-Courant pattern of treating institute-design as research activity; Aoki’s Stanford CIA, RIETI, CIDEG, VCASI ventures are the institutional-economics analogue.

Best chapters/pages to actually read

For methodology and intellectual identity, ch. 1 (Seven Intellectual Ventures) is non-skippable: it states the master metaphor, names the four-mode taxonomy of cross-cultural integration (deny / curry / average / integrate), and introduces the Anderson-Jung-Kawai psychological-cost frame. Ch. 13 (Toward Modern Economics) is the precise pivot from Marxism to mathematical economics — the Arrow-Hurwicz article appears here as the operational catalyst. Ch. 22 (Thoughts on Corporations East and West) develops the cooperative-game firm model and the Kawai hollow-center analogy; this is the formal-substantive heart of Aoki’s contribution to corporate governance. Ch. 25 (Toward a Comparative Institutional Analysis) is the founder’s-own narrative of the 1990 Stanford CIA program: founding members, intellectual genealogy, the 1998 Qian tenure denial, the program’s contraction and Greif’s continuation. Ch. 28 (Engaging with China) is the China-policy chapter — the 1994 Jinglun Conference, Wu Jinglian, Zhou Xiaochuan, Lou Jiwei, Guo Shuqing, Zhu Rongji, the World Bank “Corporate Governance in Transitional Economies” project, the Tsinghua Adjunct Professorship 2003. Ch. 31 (Conclusion) is the explicit theoretical close — the typology of venture types and the “trans-boundary game” framing. For comparative-politics readers, ch. 21 (Visiting Closed China) documents the 1976 Gang-of-Four-era trip including a meeting with Tsinghua’s Revolutionary Committee Chief Chi Qun in the same room where, 25 years later, Aoki would receive his Adjunct Professorship — useful texture for Meg Rithmire - Precarious Ties readers. Chs. 26-27 (To Kasumigaseki / Light and Shadow of Independent Administrative Institutions) record the RIETI venture and the bureaucratic-capture failure mode of policy-think-tank reform. Skim chs. 4-9 (childhood, school years, mentors) when continuity matters less than thematic anchors.

What I would miss if I only read this note

I would miss the scene-level texture: Marrakesh, the 180-degree Mendocino headland where Aoki wrote the CIA book in summers, Kornai showing him the Anti-Equilibrium manuscript secretly at Serra House (ch. 17), the prison-cell hierarchy at Sugamo (ch. 10) — primary-source specificity flat-summary loses. I would miss the specific networks and sponsorship chains: who introduced whom (Okazaki to Zhou Enlai for Aoki’s 1976 China visit; Tatsuhito Tokuchi at the Beijing airport in 1994; Xiao Meng / Peng Xiaomeng as the editorial-network hub for Comparative Studies), which Stanford colleagues contributed which papers, which donors (Fujita / NTT Data) underwrote which ventures. I would miss the Japanese-academic intellectual context that frames Aoki: Kōzō Uno’s economics, Yoshirō Tamanoi’s seminar, Banri Asanuma’s Hurwicz-style mechanism work that influenced Milgrom-Roberts (ch. 22), Kazuo Koike’s labor-organization fieldwork. I would miss the emotional honesty about the Bund “defeat” (ch. 31): Aoki neither claims victory nor accepts defeat but reframes the question. I would miss the physical disability the memoir is written through — the 1982 writer’s-cramp injury from typewriter overuse (ch. 22) that left him unable to write right-handed, which he treats not as inconvenience but as a permanent condition shaping his work since. Finally, I would miss the unresolved theoretical questions: whether CIA’s commitment to game-theoretic vocabulary actually distinguishes it from the culturalism it was designed to replace once Kawai-style depth-psychological claims are pulled inside as common-knowledge conditions; whether the program’s 1998 contraction at Stanford reflects a genuinely defensible methodological objection or “non-scholarly emotions” against an internationally-popular research direction. The memoir’s honest under-determination on these points is more useful than my paraphrase.

Critiques and limitations

The memoir genre under-determines causal claims that Aoki’s technical books actually defend with rigor; readers who use the memoir as a substitute for Toward a Comparative Institutional Analysis (MIT 2001) will systematically undervalue the formal complementarity-supermodularity apparatus. Western readers may overfit “Japan story” frames to Aoki’s specific Japanese examples (main-bank finance, lifetime employment, hollow-center executive role) when those examples are presented as instances of the general multi-equilibrium machinery rather than as Japan-specific claims. Activist-period chapters (chs. 2, 10-12) risk nostalgia and require triangulation against critical histories of the Anpo struggle; Aoki himself notes that his interpretation of the Bund’s “defeat” as “evolution in my game of life” is potentially “self-rationalization with the advantage of hindsight” (ch. 31). The transboundary-scholarship rhetoric celebrates risk-taking that depends on elite sponsorship networks (Arrow at Stanford; Kornai’s Serra House office; Okazaki’s Zhou Enlai connection; Fujita’s NTT Data funding) others lack; class and gender gradients in academic mobility are absent from Aoki’s frame. The Jung-Kawai psychological-vertigo motif risks substituting evocative anecdote for operational psychology of academic migration. The memoir’s treatment of the 1998 Qian tenure denial is honest about the existence of the political-economy-of-academia question but constrained by professional norms from documenting it fully; readers should treat Aoki’s account as one side of a dispute that mainstream Stanford economists would frame differently. CIA’s equilibrium framing is technically agnostic about whose interests the equilibrium serves, an analytical silence Marxist and critical-political-economy critics charge systematically obscures power asymmetry; Aoki acknowledges (chs. 10-12) that he came to CIA from Marxist activism without fully resolving whether the equilibrium frame translates or replaces class-conflict frames. Finally, the memoir is a survivor’s document — we do not have memoirs from the disintegrated cases the Jung anecdote warns about, so the concept’s honesty about cost is real but partial.

Links to other books

  • Comparative political economy and CIA-adjacent work: Meg E. Rithmire - Land Bargains and Chinese Capitalism (CIA at subnational scale within China; three-city design); Meg Rithmire - Precarious Ties (CIA-style typology of regime-firm dyads across Asian authoritarian capitalisms); Angela Huyue Zhang - High Wire (Chinese platform regulation as multi-equilibrium game); Acharya and Lee - The Cartel System of States (states-as-Cournot-cartel using same Nash-equilibrium machinery).
  • Globalization and shock-therapy critique: Joseph E. Stiglitz - Globalization and Its Discontents Revisited (Western-insider analogue of Aoki’s Asian-CIA critique of Washington Consensus; both diagnose 1990s Russian shock therapy as ignoring institutional complementarities).
  • Methodological complement: Gary Goertz and James Mahoney - A Tale of Two Cultures (qualitative-quantitative culture distinction; CIA sits in the qualitative culture but uses set-theoretic and game-theoretic apparatus the qualitative culture rarely formalizes); Karl Popper - The Logic of Scientific Discovery (Critical Rationalism applied to social science requires the comparative observation Aoki insists on).
  • Microeconomics and game-theoretic foundations: Daron Acemoglu - Microeconomics (ch. 13 game theory + ch. 17 mechanism design supply CIA’s formal apparatus); Bryan Caplan - The Myth of the Rational Voter (rational-irrational voting in institutions complements CIA on political-game equilibria).
  • Institution-building craft analogues: Constance Reid - Hilbert and Constance Reid - Courant (Klein-Hilbert-Courant Göttingen-tradition institution-building as the mathematical-discipline analogue of Aoki’s CIA program-building); Carol Parikh - The Unreal Life of Oscar Zariski (Zariski’s Harvard institutional modernization of algebraic geometry).
  • Japanese postwar history complement: Lin Zhongjie - Kenzo Tange and the Metabolist Movement (Japanese postwar architectural-modernist institution-building roughly contemporaneous with Aoki’s 1960s Anpo period and 1970s economic reformulation).
  • Career-narrative contrast: Reid Hoffman - The Start-Up of You — individual skill-portfolio optimization vs. Aoki’s institutional-venture variance; the contrast is sharp.

Research uses

  • Methodological frame for any Yang research on China political economy. Aoki’s CIA framework, plus the Subnational Comparative Political Economy research design Rithmire instantiates, plus the Transboundary Scholarship career posture, jointly supply the full methodological stack for Yang’s potential work on Chinese platform regulation, family-enterprise governance, or US-China comparative institutional analysis. Treat ch. 1 + ch. 25 + ch. 30 of the memoir as the methodological reading list, paired with one technical CIA paper (Aoki-Milgrom complementarity; Greif on Maghrebi traders) as formal companion.
  • Reading-list construction for graduate-level comparative-PE syllabi. A full graduate week could pair: (a) memoir chs. 1, 25, 28, 31; (b) one chapter from Aoki’s Toward a Comparative Institutional Analysis (MIT 2001); (c) Rithmire’s Land Bargains chs. 1, 9-10; (d) Stiglitz on Russian shock therapy (Globalization and Its Discontents Revisited ch. 9). Students write a short paper translating one memoir narrative observation (e.g., the Bund’s unintended-consequence “victory” via political game-rule change) into formal CIA vocabulary — failure to translate reveals comprehension gaps in either direction.
  • Career-design diagnostic. Aoki’s typology of ventures (rule-changing / new-space / new-way-of-playing) supplies an operational frame for evaluating Yang’s own future commitments. A reading group, an open-source library, a regulatory-policy commentary, a venture-structuring proposal each fall into different cells of the typology with different success criteria; treating them all the same is a category error the memoir helps diagnose.
  • Network reading for China-economics intellectual history. Ch. 28’s roster (Wu Jinglian, Chen Qingtai, Zhou Xiaochuan, Lou Jiwei, Guo Shuqing, Zhu Rongji, Tatsuhito Tokuchi, Xiao Meng / Peng Xiaomeng) is a valuable primary-source index for anyone tracing how Western institutional-economics ideas reached Chinese banking and corporate-governance reform via specific bilateral conferences and personal connections — useful for Yang’s potential AI-governance work where cross-border intellectual-policy networks matter.
  • Critique-of-shock-therapy material. Aoki’s account of the World Bank “Corporate Governance in Transitional Economies” project (ch. 28, 1994) and the “insider control” warning anticipates Stiglitz’s later Russia critique. Citable as Asian-side evidence that the post-1990 Washington Consensus was contested inside the international-institution apparatus, not only outside it.
  • Pedagogy on institutional failure modes. Chs. 26-27 (RIETI / independent administrative institutions) document a specific venture failure mode: the bureaucratic wall, in which formally autonomous policy-research institutions remain captured by ministerial budgetary and personnel logic. Useful counterweight to optimistic think-tank-design literature; pairs with Sebastian Mallaby - The Power Law for the contrasting Silicon Valley institutional-design success story.

Open questions

  • How much does the 1998 Stanford Qian tenure denial generalize? Is research-program politics inside elite American economics departments structurally hostile to internationally-popular heterodox programs, or did the CIA-program contraction reflect specific contingencies that more recent institutional-economics work (Acemoglu-Robinson; behavioral institutional economics) has overcome?
  • Where does CIA’s commitment to game-theoretic vocabulary draw the line against the culturalism it was designed to replace, once depth-psychological claims (Kawai’s hollow-center; Confucian-cultural inheritance in Asian capitalism) are pulled inside as common-knowledge conditions or focal-point coordinators?
  • Which of Aoki’s seven ventures failed in ways that matter methodologically rather than merely personally, and which succeeded in ways that depend on contingent elite sponsorship rather than replicable design? The memoir is candid about specific failures (Bund; RIETI’s bureaucratic-wall encounter) but does not systematically distinguish replicable from contingent success conditions.
  • How should syllabi balance activist-memoir chapters (chs. 2, 10-12) with graduate-level game-theoretic models without letting either genre pretend it has already proved the other? The Bund-to-CIA transition is the key load-bearing biographical move and the easiest one to read flatly.
  • Where does Aoki’s comparative instinct overlap with contemporary computational social science — agent-based simulation of institutions versus analytic equilibrium narratives — and would CIA-style multiple-equilibria modelling benefit from the simulation toolkit’s ability to handle non-tractable equilibrium-selection problems?
  • How to read Aoki’s 1976-2003-quarter-century arc with China (ch. 21 → ch. 28) against Xi Jinping-era closure — the Tsinghua Adjunct Professorship Aoki received in 2003 belongs to a regulatory-academic environment that has substantially closed since, and the Jinglun-Conference-style intellectual-policy conduit may not be reconstructible.

Depth supplement

If you have only two hours, read chs. 1, 13, 25, 31 closely and skim ch. 22 and ch. 28 chapter titles to confirm the trans-boundary-venture pattern. Those four-plus-two anchors prevent the standard misreading of Aoki as a generic-Asian-success biography rather than the founder of a specific research program. If you have a semester, assign memoir chs. 1, 13, 22, 25, 28, 31 alongside one technical paper on institutional complementarities (Aoki-Milgrom 1996 Quarterly Journal of Economics or Aoki 2010 Corporations in Evolving Diversity ch. 1) and ask students to write bridge paragraphs translating narrative observations into formal variables. Failure to translate in either direction reveals whether students understood either side; success in both directions is the operational mark of CIA literacy. Discuss visa / deportation passages (ch. 1, ch. 19) explicitly when teaching international students; otherwise migration reads as adventure tourism and the structural-constraint argument is lost. Compare Aoki’s “vertigo” motif with other ethnographies of academic liminality and with Stiglitz’s theory-policy boundary-crossing in Joseph E. Stiglitz - Globalization and Its Discontents Revisited; the comparison clarifies that which boundary one crosses changes which psychological costs apply but not the existence of the costs themselves.
