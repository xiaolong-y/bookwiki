David Kushner - Masters of Doom

Read chapter-by-chapter → Deep dive of Masters of Doom: How Two Guys Created an Empire and Transformed Pop Culture (14 chapters)

One-sentence thesis

The first-person shooter genre and the modern game industry were built by the productive friction between two complementary halves of one founding partnership — John Carmack’s monkish engine engineering and John Romero’s extroverted level design and brand-building — and the same asymmetry that built Doom and Quake then destroyed the partnership at the moment commercial success removed every external constraint that had been forcing the two halves to cooperate.

Why this book matters

Kushner’s book is the foundational popular history of id Software, and through id, of the entire first-person shooter category, the Shareware Distribution business model, Mod Culture, the LAN-party-and-deathmatch competitive subculture that became e-sports, and the emergence of the Game Engine as a licensable, multi-game-amortized product line. Reading it as a business history, it is the closest thing the games industry has to Andy Hertzfeld - Revolution in the Valley for the original Macintosh team or Tracy Kidder - The Soul of a New Machine for Data General — a six-person-startup creation myth told through the engineers themselves.

But the book matters more as a study in founder dynamics than as a games history. The Two Johns are an unusually clean case of Conceptual Integrity in Frederick P. Brooks Jr. - The Mythical Man-Month’s sense: a small team where one technical voice (Carmack) and one design voice (Romero) hold the product’s coherence, with all other contributors subordinate to one or the other. Brooks’s claim is that conceptual integrity is the single most important quality in software design and is achievable only by very small teams with enforced architectural authority; Kushner’s id case shows what conceptual integrity looks like when it works (Wolfenstein 3D, Doom) and what happens when one of the two voices loses interest in execution (Quake’s troubled development; Daikatana’s collapse).

The book also matters as a counter-case to the standard Silicon Valley founder narrative. Carmack and Romero are not Stanford-educated, not Bay Area, not venture-funded, not surrounded by Sand Hill Road advisors. They drop out of college, work in shareware out of a Louisiana bedroom, move to a Mesquite, Texas office park, and build a category-defining company on mail-order checks and royalties. Compare to Walter Isaacson - Elon Musk for the founder-obsession parallel and to Steven Levy - Hackers for the descent: id are second-generation Hackers in Levy’s sense, philosophically downstream of MIT’s TX-0 and Homebrew but unaware of the lineage.

Finally, the book is a warning. The phase from Doom’s release (December 1993) to Romero’s firing (summer 1996) shows what happens when a productive partnership receives more money and more fame than its constraint structure can absorb. The Ferraris, the penthouses, the rock-star T-shirts, and the press tours are not red herrings — they are the mechanism by which the partnership broke. For founders thinking about their own dynamics, the book is required reading on a failure mode that no founder advisor will warn you about.

Core argument

  1. The Two Johns were a structurally complementary, not redundant, pair. Carmack and Romero are not two of the same kind of person — they are different kinds. Carmack is the engine programmer (low-level, mathematical, allergic to ambiguity), Romero is the designer-promoter (level layout, audio, marketing, social capital). Their early work succeeded because each occupied a non-overlapping role and recognized the other as essential. The .plan-file metaphor is exact: Carmack ships features, Romero ships excitement. Either alone produces less than half the joint output.

  2. The id business model was structurally novel for the early 1990s. Shareware Distribution (Apogee’s variant, codified by Scott Miller) gave away episode 1 of Wolfenstein 3D and Doom for free, sold episodes 2 and 3 by mail order, and bypassed retail entirely. The gross margin on a registered episode was essentially the cost of a duplicated floppy disk; the marketing budget was zero because the free episode was the marketing. This let a six-person studio outearn many hundred-person retail-distributed studios. Apogee was already at 7M/yearbeforeid;idatpeakshipped100K/day on Doom. The structural advantage was distribution disintermediation a decade before SaaS made the same point in B2B.

  3. The Game Engine is a productized form of Process Power. Carmack’s engines (Keen 1990, Wolf3D 1992, Doom 1993, Quake 1996, Quake III 1999, Doom III 2004) embed multi-year graphics and systems-programming know-how that competitors cannot easily replicate. Carmack chose to license each engine to other studios for 250K−500K plus royalties — turning the engine itself into a recurring revenue line independent of game sales. The engine-licensing market underwrote thirty-plus shipped commercial games in the late 1990s and 2000s, and seeded the Unreal/Unity dominance of the modern industry.

  4. Mod Culture is intentional architecture, not an accident. Carmack documented the WAD file format, tolerated third-party level editors, and later released the full source code of Doom, Quake, Quake II, and Quake III. This created an apprenticeship system: every successful 2000s shooter studio (Valve, Bungie, Epic, Activision-acquired studios) hired heavily from the Doom and Quake mod communities. Tim Willits, eventually id’s co-owner, was hired off his Doom mod portfolio. The mod ecosystem extended Doom’s commercial life by decades and became Carmack’s most durable industry contribution after the engine itself.

  5. Conceptual integrity broke the moment one founder lost interest in execution. Romero shipped six of thirty-two Doom II levels — the rest were filled by American McGee and Sandy Petersen. By Quake (1996), Romero had stopped being the lead designer in any meaningful sense; Tim Willits and American McGee made most of the levels; the original Hellgate Cube / time-travel / melee-combat design collapsed into “Doom III with rocket launchers.” Carmack tracked the decay through his .plan files (a public commit log avant la lettre) and eventually called the meeting at which Romero was fired in summer 1996. Brooks would say: when conceptual integrity fragments, the product fragments.

  6. Excess success is its own failure mode. Romero’s post-id Ion Storm — 25MfromEidos,22,500−square−footTexasCommerceTowerpenthouseat550K/month, three games in flight simultaneously, “Design Is Law” motto, “John Romero’s About to Make You His Bitch” magazine ad — is the book’s case study in what happens when founder hubris meets external capital that does not impose constraint. Daikatana shipped four years late on an aging engine and sold 41K copies. The Carmack analog at id during the same years is austerity by choice: id stays small, refuses outside capital, ships on a slow but steady cadence, and quietly builds a balance sheet measured in tens of millions.

  7. Regional clusters self-organize around an anchor. The Dallas first-person-shooter cluster (“Silicon Alamo”) had id at its center, with 3D Realms, Ion Storm, Rogue, Ritual, Ensemble Studios, and Terminal Reality forming the second tier — most of them spun out of id or 3D Realms, most of them licensing the Quake engine, most of them hiring from the Doom mod community. The pattern is the Silicon Valley Spinout Model in miniature, applied to a single product category in a single mid-sized American city.

  8. The cultural-moral panic around violent games (Columbine, Lieberman hearings) was largely evidence-free. Kushner devotes a chapter to the 1999 backlash and concludes, with citations to Gerard Jones’s Killing Monsters, that the link from playing Doom to school shootings was a media construction without supporting data. Federal courts later threw out the Columbine families’ lawsuits against the game industry. The chapter is one of the book’s more journalistically substantive sections.

Argument map

  1. Two Johns founding (1990 Softdisk meeting). Carmack the engine programmer plus Romero the level designer plus the supporting cast (Tom Hall, Adrian Carmack, Jay Wilbur, Kevin Cloud) form a structurally complementary, not redundant, six-person team.
  2. Engine generations as a recurring backbone. Adaptive Tile Refresh (Commander Keen, 1990), raycasting (Wolfenstein 3D, 1992), BSP trees (Doom, 1993), arbitrary 3D polygons with surface caching (Quake, 1996), OpenGL-accelerated shipping (Quake III, 1999), stencil shadow volumes (Doom III, 2004) — each generation sells its own games and licenses outward to other studios as recurring revenue.
  3. Shareware distribution as the structural disintermediation. Apogee’s Scott Miller’s free-episode-1 model bypasses retail entirely; gross margin per registered episode is roughly the cost of a duplicated floppy disk; Doom grosses about $100K per day at peak; the six-person studio outearns hundred-person retail-distributed studios.
  4. Mod culture as intentional architecture, not accident. Carmack documents the WAD file format, tolerates third-party Doom editors, releases full source code for Doom (1997), Quake (1999), Quake II (2001), and Quake III (2005); the resulting modder community becomes the apprenticeship system that produces the next generation of professional designers (Tim Willits, Counter-Strike at Valve, founders at Bungie and Epic).
  5. Doom released December 1993, the inflection point. Commercial success removes the external constraints that had been forcing the Two Johns to cooperate; both founders’ personalities expand into the new freedom.
  6. Romero’s rock-star arc, 1994-1996. “Wrote It” T-shirt, yellow Ferrari Testarossa, dark hair to the waist, deathmatch tournaments, fan worship in CompUSA parking lots; Doom II level output drops to six of thirty-two; Quake design collapses mid-development from Hellgate Cube and time-travel ambition to “Doom III with rocket launchers.”
  7. Carmack’s monkish arc, 1994-1996. Daily .plan-file public commit log; continued engine work; ports to Atari Jaguar and SNES for the low-level practice; war-room move that exposes Romero’s disengagement to the rest of the team; firing meeting summer 1996 with pre-drafted resignation.
  8. Post-split divergence, 1996-2001. id stays small, ships Quake II (1997), Quake III (1999), Doom III (2004); Carmack pivots to amateur rocketry and founds Armadillo Aerospace (2000). Ion Storm takes 25MfromEidos,leasesa22,500−square−footTexasCommerceTowerpenthouseat550K per month, ships Daikatana four years late on Quake 1’s engine; Daikatana sells 41K U.S. units; Eidos shuts the Dallas office in 2001.
  9. Reconciliation as coda. 2001 Mesquite parking lot outside a Quake III tournament; Carmack’s Ferrari engine won’t start; Romero pulls up in his yellow Hummer with jumper cables; the two work on the car together. The deathmatch is over.
  10. The structural moral. Conceptual integrity in a complementary partnership is fragile to disinterest by either half; commercial success without external constraint accelerates the disinterest; the same asymmetry that built the genre destroyed the partnership at the moment it stopped being needed.

Key concepts

  • Game Engine — Carmack’s career as the canonical case of engine-as-product-line: each id engine generation embeds multi-year know-how, ships multiple games, and licenses to outside studios.
  • Shareware Distribution — Apogee/Miller’s free-episode-1 model that bypassed retail and enabled six-person id Software to outearn hundred-person studios.
  • Mod Culture — Carmack’s deliberate architectural choice to expose data formats and release source code, creating the apprenticeship pipeline for the next generation of game studios.
  • Silicon Valley Spinout Model — Dallas’s “Silicon Alamo” cluster as a regional version: id as Shockley/Fairchild analog, 3D Realms / Ion Storm / Ensemble / Rogue / Ritual as the spinout tier.
  • Conceptual Integrity — id under the Two Johns as a working example of Brooks’s small-team architectural-authority thesis, and id after Romero’s disengagement as the failure mode.
  • Process Power — Carmack’s engine as the canonical Hamilton Helmer - 7 Powers Process Power case in software: the cumulative know-how of six engine generations is hard to replicate even with comparable headcount.
  • Cornered Resource — Carmack himself as a Cornered Resource for id, with Romero, Adrian Carmack, and Kevin Cloud all explicitly recognizing in the book that “if Carmack walks, the company is over.”

Best chapters/pages to actually read

Read Ch. 4 (Dangerous Dave In Copyright Infringement) for the founding myth: Carmack’s adaptive-tile-refresh Mario demo, the seventy-two-hour lake-house Keen sprint, the Apogee shareware deal, and Carmack’s parting line to Big Al Vekovius that establishes his lifetime willingness to walk from any institution. Ch. 6-7 (More Fun Than Real Life and Spear of Destiny) are the Wolfenstein 3D and Doom design chapters — read them for the engine architecture (raycasting → BSP), the death-of-the-Doom-Bible scene where Romero builds an abstract level in one night that destroys Tom Hall’s realistic-military-base aesthetic, and Tom Hall’s firing as id’s first founder departure. Ch. 9 (Quakes / QuakeTalk) is the longest chapter and the saddest: Quake’s eighteen-month slip, Romero’s disengagement, the war-room move, the .plan-file dueling, and Carmack’s eventual firing of Romero. Ch. 12 (Straight Out of Doom) is the Columbine chapter and the most journalistically rigorous in the book — read it for the moral-panic media analysis if you skip the rest. Ch. 13-14 for the divergence (Daikatana flop vs. Quake III triumph) and the rocket-launching epilogue that resolves Carmack’s character arc. The prologue is worth the ten-minute read for the framing alone; the Romero and Carmack childhood chapters (2-3) you can skim if you remember the structural-mirror beats (broken home / minister’s-son household; arcade rescue / juvenile detention).

Important examples / cases

  • The adaptive-tile-refresh Super Mario Bros. 3 demo (1990) — Carmack’s PC port of a Nintendo console exclusive, packaged as “Dangerous Dave In Copyright Infringement,” is the founding artifact of id. Mailed to Nintendo, politely declined, but proves the team can do what others say is impossible.
  • The seventy-two-hour Keen lake-house sprint (December 1990) — five people, pizza, a rented Louisiana lake house, and one weekend produce a complete shareware game. The endurance-and-immersion model becomes the id template for the next decade.
  • Wolfenstein 3D (1992) — Carmack writes the raycasting engine alone in two months; the team chooses Nazis as the only universally acceptable enemy; Apogee ships shareware; episode 1 grosses $200K in the first month.
  • Romero’s abstract level (spring 1993) — one all-night session in his office, Dokken on the speakers, that overthrows Tom Hall’s realistic Doom Bible and sets the genre’s visual grammar.
  • Tom Hall fired (summer 1993) — id’s first founder firing under the unanimous-vote / full-equity-forfeiture by-laws; the rule will recur for Romero three years later.
  • Doom shareware release (Dec 10, 1993) — uploaded to a University of Wisconsin FTP server that crashes within minutes; the system administrator phones id in panic.
  • The .plan file innovation (1996) — Carmack publishes his daily work log to the public Internet, prefiguring the engineering blog and structurally outflanking Romero’s grand pre-announcements with shipped-feature evidence.
  • The Quake war room (winter 1995-1996) — shades drawn, cables from the ceiling, no privacy; Carmack’s deliberate forcing function that reveals Romero’s disengagement to the rest of the team.
  • Romero fired (summer 1996) — Carmack hands him a pre-written resignation in a conference room. Romero signs, walks out, calls Tom Hall on the way to the parking lot.
  • The 22,500 sq ft Texas Commerce Tower penthouse ($550K/month) — Romero’s Ion Storm office, on the top floors of downtown Dallas’s tallest tower, with a sixty-foot arched glass ceiling. The book’s emblem of post-id founder hubris.
  • Daikatana ships (May 2000) — four years late, on Quake 1’s engine when Quake III is on the market; sells 41K U.S. units against a $25M budget.
  • Thresh wins Carmack’s Ferrari (May 1997) — Microsoft Judgment Day tournament; Carmack returns half an hour later with $5K cash for shipping.
  • Carmack’s first amateur rocket launch (November 2001) — Day-Glo orange model rocket, a cow pasture east of Mesquite, the line “there’s value in coming out here and dealing with the wind.” The book’s closing image.

Critiques and limitations

The book is essentially unsourced narrative nonfiction. There are no chapter-by-chapter endnotes, no documentary apparatus, and no bibliography of the trade-press articles, internal memos, and emails that the text leans on. Direct quotes are presumably from interviews; reconstructed dialogue and interior thoughts are necessarily inferred. The result reads as well as a Robert Caro biography but cannot be cross-checked the way Caro can. For specific factual claims (dates, dollar figures, who said what when), a careful reader should treat Kushner as a starting point and verify against the trade press of the period (Computer Gaming World, PC Gamer, Wired) before citing.

The book is also visibly limited by access asymmetry. Carmack and Romero gave extended on-record interviews; Tom Hall, Adrian Carmack, Kevin Cloud, Mike Wilson, Jay Wilbur, and most ex-id staff appear primarily through the Two Johns’ recollections. Tom Hall’s firing scene is rendered from Tom’s interior monologue, which is the closest the book gets to a non-Two-Johns viewpoint, and the Ion Storm collapse is told largely from Romero’s perspective. The book cannot tell us, for instance, what Adrian Carmack’s experience of the Ion Storm period was like, or what Sandy Petersen thought when he was let go from id, with anything like the depth it gives the Two Johns themselves.

The technical content is also limited. Kushner is a journalist, not a programmer; his explanations of BSP, raycasting, and surface caching are correct in outline but lack the precision that would let a working programmer learn anything from them. Readers who want the technical history at depth should pair Kushner with Fabien Sanglard’s Game Engine Black Book: Wolfenstein 3D and Game Engine Black Book: Doom, which reverse-engineer the engines line by line.

Finally, the cultural framing is dated. The book was published in 2003, before the rise of free-to-play mobile, before Twitch and esports streaming, before the Activision/Take-Two/Microsoft consolidation reshaped studio economics, and before the Gamergate / industry-labor / harassment issues that have dominated game-industry coverage since 2014. Read it as a primary document of the 1990s shooter era, not as a current map of the industry.

Links to other books

  • Andy Hertzfeld - Revolution in the Valley — small founding-team creation myth; Macintosh team’s early years are the closest structural parallel to the id lake house, with similar founder personality dynamics.
  • Tracy Kidder - The Soul of a New Machine — engineer-driven team biography; the Eclipse MV/8000 team and the Doom team share the all-nighter / dedicated-engineer / outside-the-org-chart pattern.
  • Steven Levy - Hackers — Carmack and Romero are second-generation hackers in Levy’s lineage; the Apple II teenage hobbyist culture they came up in is Levy’s third-wave (Apple II / Atari 800) chapter.
  • Frederick P. Brooks Jr. - The Mythical Man-Month — id’s Two Johns are a textbook Conceptual Integrity case; Brooks predicts both the success of the Doom era and the failure of Ion Storm’s distributed design authority.
  • Hamilton Helmer - 7 Powers — Carmack’s engine as canonical Process Power; Carmack himself as Cornered Resource; Doom mod community as nascent Network Economies.
  • Andrew S. Grove - High Output Management — explicit anti-management contrast: Grove’s discipline is exactly what Romero’s Ion Storm lacked, and Carmack’s instinctive engineering-driven veto culture is closer to Grove’s “high-output” than to Romero’s “design is law.”
  • Walter Isaacson - Elon Musk — founder-obsessive parallel; both Carmack (rockets, AGI) and Musk are recognizably the same personality type, with the difference that Carmack has no interest in being CEO of anything.
  • Leslie Berlin - The Man Behind the Microchip — Noyce’s Fairchild-to-Intel arc and Carmack’s Softdisk-to-id arc both show small founding teams creating new product categories; both bios pair an introverted technical lead with extroverted partners.

Research uses

For founder-dynamics research, the book is the cleanest natural experiment available on what happens when a complementary partnership receives more capital and fame than its constraint structure can absorb. The Two Johns’ productive period (1990-1995) and destructive period (1995-1996) are bounded by clear external events (Doom’s release, the war-room move, the firing meeting), and both founders gave on-record interviews that let the same events be reconstructed from both sides. Useful for theorizing why some startups outgrow their founding partnerships and some don’t.

For business-model history, the book is the foundational source for the Shareware Distribution model that prefigured SaaS by a decade — free-tier-to-paid conversion, viral organic distribution, retailer disintermediation, and high-margin direct-to-customer sales. Useful for arguing that distribution disruption is older and more general than the cloud era.

For software-engineering education, pair Kushner with Fabien Sanglard’s Wolfenstein 3D and Doom Black Books to get both the human story and the line-by-line engine archaeology. Useful for teaching the Game Engine as an example of the architectural separation between platform and content, and for teaching Mod Culture as a case of intentional user-innovation architecture.

For technology-and-culture analysis, the Columbine chapter is one of the better journalistic dissections of a tech moral panic and provides a template for analyzing later panics (loot boxes, screen time, social-media-and-teens). Useful for arguing that mainstream media moral panics about technology are predictably evidence-free and predictably subside as the technology becomes economically central.

Open questions

  • Was the Two Johns’ breakup avoidable? Could a stronger operations partner (a Carmack-style Andy Grove figure) have absorbed the conflict and kept Romero productive at id?
  • How much of Carmack’s Process Power in engines was technical-IP and how much was pure individual talent? Could id have built the same engine generations without Carmack specifically, given the same hiring budget?
  • Why did id refuse outside capital and stay tiny when peers (3D Realms, Ion Storm, Origin Systems) all scaled headcount and burned through publisher money? Was this Carmack’s instinct or Adrian Carmack’s and Kevin Cloud’s preference?
  • What would Doom and Quake have looked like if Romero had not had the level-design lead — would the genre’s visual grammar exist?
  • Did the open-source Doom and Quake releases (1997-2005) accelerate or retard the engine licensing market?
  • The book ends in 2002. What does the post-2002 history (id sold to ZeniMax 2009, Carmack to Oculus 2013, Carmack to Keen Technologies 2022, Romero to Galway) tell us in retrospect about the founder-personalities the book diagnoses?

What I would miss if I only read this note

I would miss the texture that makes the book a page-turner. The eleven-year-old Romero pedaling to the Roundtable Pizza Parlor in defiance of his stepfather’s beatings; Carmack at thirteen removing his door from its hinges to break into his comic-book closet; Kevin Cloud’s lake-house cat Mitzi sleeping on Carmack’s monitor; Tom Hall peeling a sticker off the conference-room table while being fired; the Pac-Man machine that arrives uninvited in the back of id’s Wisconsin moving truck; the war-room cables hanging from the ceiling with the shades drawn; Carmack jogging through a cow pasture east of Mesquite to retrieve a Day-Glo orange model rocket. The book is constructed scene by scene, and the scenes are what make the structural claims memorable. A summary can name “founder dynamics broke after Doom”; only the book makes you feel why Carmack walked into the conference room that day with the resignation pre-drafted.

I would also miss the contingency. Reading this note, the Two Johns sound like an inevitable historical pair. Reading the book, the partnership is built on a thousand near-misses: Romero’s stepfather almost catches him out of the arcade; Carmack’s juvenile-detention judge almost gives him longer; the Softdisk job is a fluke; the Apogee deal happens because Scott Miller’s home address is on the back of a magazine; the Doom shareware launch crashes one University of Wisconsin server and another; Romero is almost not fired; Daikatana almost ships before its 1998 walkout; Carmack almost stays at id; Romero almost reconciles. Kushner’s book is fundamentally a story of how much of what looks structural in retrospect was contingent at the time, and how the productive partnership of 1990-1995 was always a few weeks from breaking apart even when it was working.
