High Wire — How China Regulates Big Tech and Governs Its Economy

High Wire — How China Regulates Big Tech and Governs Its Economy

  • book
  • china-political-economy
  • tech-policy
  • regulation

Angela Huyue Zhang - High Wire

One-sentence thesis

Chinese tech regulation is structured like a pyramid balanced on a high wire — a four-tier hierarchy (top leadership, regulators, firms, platform participants) with severe principal-agent problems at every layer, oscillating through volatile cycles of lax → harsh crackdown → easing, and producing fragile policy outcomes whose disorder is not a bug of authoritarian governance but its load-bearing operating principle; understanding the dynamic-pyramid mechanics is the only way to make sense of why specific Chinese tech-regulatory actions land when they do, with the force they do, against the firms they do.

Why this book matters

The first English-language full-book synthesis of Chinese big-tech regulation written by a legal scholar (Angela Zhang, University of Hong Kong) with primary-source access to Chinese regulatory documents and direct interviews. Published 2024, after the 2020-2022 platform crackdown stabilised enough to be analysed retrospectively but before the post-ChatGPT regulatory rebalancing fully played out — the timing is unusually informative.

The book is more reliable than the journalistic accounts (which read the crackdown either as Xi-personal-vendetta or as Chinese technocratic-genius), and more accessible than the political-science literature (which fragments across antitrust / data / labor / cybersecurity sub-fields). For Yang’s purposes — blockchain-regulation research, China-tech-policy work, and the broader question of how authoritarian states navigate market-creating regulation — Zhang is the right primary reference for the next several years. Her dynamic-pyramid framework is portable beyond tech: she explicitly notes (Introduction §I.1) that the model could illuminate non-tech Chinese regulation (Covid control, the 2021 energy crisis, property crackdown, one-child policy — Chapter 3 walks through each).

The book also matters because it is honest about both critic and supporter framings of the crackdown and rejects both as incomplete. Zhang’s reframe — that the regulatory action is rationally explained by neither Xi-as-mastermind nor China-catching-up-with-the-West but by structural features of the regulatory pyramid itself — shifts the conversation from politics to mechanics.

Core argument

  1. Two dominant readings of the 2020-2022 Chinese tech crackdown exist — critic (the crackdown is CCP-political-control reasserting itself; tech is collateral damage) and supporter (China is correctly catching up with the West on platform regulation). Zhang argues both miss what is structurally going on (Introduction).
  2. The actual structure is a dynamic pyramid model with three interlocking features (§I.1):
    • Hierarchy — four tiers (top leadership / regulators / firms / platform participants), three nested principal-agent problems
    • Volatility — three-phase cycle (lax regulation → harsh crackdown → easing) driven by tipping-point dynamics, agency overreach, and the leadership’s complex utility function
    • Fragility — outcomes are unstable; quick reactions amplify volatility (Donella Meadows on systems)
  3. Hierarchy detail (Chapter 1): the Politburo Standing Committee delegates to regulatory agencies (filled with technocrats, evaluated through nomenklatura), which delegate to firms via selective enforcement, which in turn delegate platform-governance to themselves as quasi-regulators (“platforms are the government we didn’t elect” — Zuboff, quoted at I.1.1). Each layer has misaligned incentives.
  4. Volatility detail (Chapter 2): the leadership’s three legitimacy sources — growth, stability, nationalism — are not jointly consistent. Economic growth produces market chaos that threatens stability; heavy-handed state intervention damages confidence and growth; techno-nationalism deters foreign investment. The leadership oscillates among these like an acrobat balancing on a tightrope. Crackdown phases are triggered by specific tipping points (Ant Group’s IPO in November 2020 was the canonical one) plus agency overreach (regulators competing for political credit).
  5. Fragility detail (Chapter 3): non-tech case studies (Covid control, energy crisis, property crackdown, one-child policy) exhibit the same dynamic-pyramid pattern. Quick policy reversal is a feature of the regime, not a defect — the lack of formal checks and balances enables fast action, which then introduces large policy errors that need further correction.
  6. Applied analysis (Part II, Chapters 4-6): the framework is applied to three platform-regulation domains — antitrust, data, labor — with case studies (Alibaba $2.8B fine; Didi cybersecurity review; Meituan; food-delivery worker protections). Each domain shows the four-tier hierarchy in action and the volatility in specific timing.
  7. Self-regulation (Part III, Chapters 7-8): large Chinese platforms (Taobao especially) act as quasi-regulators, building their own reputation and enforcement mechanisms; some decentralise governance to their users (the Law of Taobao is a concrete instance). Zhang traces three forms of decentralisation (weak, semi-strong, strong) and the legitimacy crises (user riots, corruption, case ambiguity) that drive them.
  8. Comparison with the West (Chapter 9): Western (US/EU) regulatory trajectories on antitrust, data, labor are increasingly converging with Chinese moves — though the form (Chinese campaign-style enforcement vs Western adversarial litigation/legislation) remains distinct.
  9. Impact assessment (Chapter 10): the crackdown produced an unintended consequence — the retreat of the private sector combined with advancement of the administrative state (golden share investments, state ownership, capital outflow). Zhang views this as the deeper consequence of the crackdown, not the headline antitrust fines.
  10. Predictive implication: future Chinese tech regulation will continue oscillating between vectors. Specific outcomes are predictable from which feature of the pyramid is currently dominant. The 2022 ChatGPT moment shifted the technological-self-sufficiency vector forward, which has produced the post-2023 partial-rehabilitation of platform firms — but this is a phase shift, not a return to the pre-2020 lax era.

Argument map

The book’s reasoning runs from a critique of two existing readings of the crackdown to an alternative analytical framework, which is then applied empirically to specific platform-regulation domains and finally to non-tech cases. The structural move is replace the ideology-driven explanations with the structure-driven explanation; the dynamic pyramid is the structure.

Each downward arrow is a chapter-level argumentative move in the book.

Key concepts

  • Dynamic Pyramid Model — Zhang’s central framework (concept stub to be promoted; for now treated within this book note)
  • Selective enforcement — regulators choose which firms to enforce against based on political signals, not uniform rule
  • Campaign-style governance (运动式治理) — regulatory action in concentrated bursts, driven by political-cycle factors
  • Three-phase regulatory cycle — lax → crackdown → easing
  • Three-source legitimacy — growth, stability, nationalism (the leadership’s competing utility-function components)
  • Black Swan — Zhang directly invokes Taleb (footnote 31 of Introduction) to characterize the randomness in Chinese regulatory tipping points
  • Skin in the Game — the principal-agent problem at every layer of the pyramid is an instance of Taleb’s framework
  • Heterarchy — useful complementary framing: the four-tier hierarchy in Zhang interacts heterodromically; the McCulloch heterarchy concept names this lateral interaction directly

Key arguments created from this book

  • Chinese tech regulation oscillates by structural design, not by ideology — the dynamic-pyramid framework is the structural-design argument

Best chapters/pages to actually read

  • Introduction §I.1 (Dynamic Pyramid Model) — the central conceptual chapter. ~30 pp. Read this first; the rest of the book applies it.
  • Chapter 2 (Volatility) — the three-phase cycle stated cleanly with the tipping point analytical move (the Ant Group IPO is the canonical exhibit). Most useful for predicting when the regulatory mood shifts.
  • Chapter 3 (Fragility) — non-tech case studies (Covid, energy, property, one-child). Demonstrates that the framework generalises beyond tech; the most efficient way to internalise that the dynamic-pyramid mechanics are general not tech-specific.
  • Chapter 4 (Antitrust Regulation) — the cleanest application of the framework to a single domain. Alibaba and Meituan are the worked exhibits.
  • Chapter 7 (Platforms as Quasi-Regulators) — the Law of Taobao analysis. Useful even for non-China readers thinking about platform-regulation more generally.
  • Chapter 10 (Assessing the Impact) — the unintended-consequences chapter. The most policy-actionable for foreign investors and policymakers.
  • Skim — Chapter 5 (Data Regulation) and Chapter 6 (Labor Regulation) unless those are your immediate research domain; they apply the framework but don’t add to it. Chapter 9 (Is China Exceptional?) is mostly recapitulating Western-trend material that can be skipped if you already follow that literature.

Important examples / cases

  • Ant Group IPO suspension (November 2020) — the tipping point of the entire crackdown cycle. Two days before the IPO, regulators held the now-famous meeting with Jack Ma; the listing was cancelled. Zhang traces the immediate chain of regulatory escalation that followed. This case is the empirical anchor of the volatility chapter.
  • Alibaba $2.8B antitrust fine (April 2021) — the headline-large fine. The State Administration for Market Regulation (SAMR) used the new platform-economy enforcement framework. Notably, Alibaba did not appeal and publicly thanked the regulator — illustrating the cooperation-with-overwhelmed-state equilibrium Zhang identifies as a structural feature of the hierarchy.
  • Didi cybersecurity review (July 2021) — two days after Didi’s NYSE IPO, the Cyberspace Administration of China launched a cybersecurity review. Zhang reads this as a cross-vector enforcement: the cyber watchdog and the financial regulator coordinated against a firm that had pursued foreign-listing despite domestic-regulator concerns about cross-border data flows.
  • The Meituan algorithmic-pricing case — used in Chapter 6 (labor regulation) to illustrate platform-as-employer dynamics. Food-delivery workers’ pay structure is set algorithmically; the firm’s labor-cost optimisation produces accident rates and worker-welfare consequences regulators eventually attack.
  • Online-tutoring industry shutdown (July 2021) — Zhang reads this as part of the common prosperity campaign’s preproduction-stage attack on inequality (Blanchard-Rodrik framework, footnote 29 of Introduction): narrowing the education gap by removing private after-school tutoring.

Critiques and limitations

  • The framework explains everything, which is a worry. A model with three features (hierarchy, volatility, fragility) plus a “complex utility function” for the leadership can post-hoc rationalise any policy outcome. Zhang’s predictions are therefore harder to falsify than her descriptive analysis. The book’s strength is its retrospective analytical clarity; its predictive power should be discounted.
  • Tech is taken to mean consumer tech specifically. Zhang explicitly excludes hardware (semiconductors) from her scope (Introduction). For Yang reading Chris Miller - Chip War alongside, the combined picture matters and Zhang covers only half.
  • The agency-overreach claim under-theorises which agency. Zhang treats agencies as homogeneous (“agencies have an incentive to maximize their bureaucratic interests”) but the empirical pattern shows that specific agencies (Cyberspace Administration, SAMR, Ministry of Industry and Information Technology) lead specific waves. A more granular agency-by-agency political-economy analysis would strengthen the framework.
  • The “Volatility” frame may be over-fitted to 2020-2022. Zhang’s three-phase cycle is empirically grounded in the recent crackdown. Whether the same phase structure recurs (vs the regime moves to a new equilibrium with permanent harsher regulation) is empirically open. The framework assumes oscillation; the 2024+ data may fit return-to-equilibrium better than next-cycle better.
  • Light on causal identification. The book is observational. The counterfactual (“what would Chinese tech regulation look like without the dynamic-pyramid features?”) is hard to evaluate because no comparison case exists. Some of Zhang’s causal claims are stronger than her evidence supports.
  • The “fragility” feature is the least-well-developed of the three. Zhang lists case studies but the mechanism by which the system is fragile (vs simply varying) is under-specified. Chapter 3 is shorter than chapters 1-2 and doesn’t fully integrate with the rest.
  • Underweights the role of foreign-policy environment. US export controls (the Chris Miller - Chip War story) and Western data-sovereignty rules shape Chinese regulatory choices. Zhang treats these as exogenous; they may be more endogenous (Chinese regulation responds to and anticipates US moves).

Links to other books

  • Direct empirical complement to Meg E. Rithmire - Land Bargains and Chinese Capitalism and Meg Rithmire - Precarious Ties — Rithmire on subnational variation in Chinese capitalism (cities; firms); Zhang on national-level regulatory architecture (top leadership; agencies). Reading the three together gives the full vertical of Chinese political economy: city-level land bargains (Rithmire I), firm-state precarious ties (Rithmire II), national regulatory pyramid (Zhang). Yang’s institutional research can sit at any of the three levels; the books triangulate.
  • Geopolitical context from Chris Miller - Chip War — Miller documents the external pressure (US chip-export controls) that reactivates Chinese technological-self-sufficiency vector. Zhang covers the consumer-tech crackdown; Miller covers the strategic-tech response. The two together explain Chinese tech-policy in 2020-2025.
  • Methodological complement to Gary Goertz and James Mahoney - A Tale of Two Cultures — Zhang is firmly in the qualitative-case-tracing tradition Goertz-Mahoney describe. Her dynamic-pyramid model is set-theoretic (necessary/sufficient conditions for a crackdown to occur); her cases are within-case process-tracing exhibits. Useful exhibit for understanding what high-quality qualitative China-PE research looks like in 2024.
  • Implicit Talebian framing — Zhang explicitly cites Taleb (Black Swan) on tipping points (Introduction footnote 31). Her volatility feature is a structural account of why Chinese tech regulation is Black-Swan-distributed — outcomes dominated by rare crackdown events. The fragility feature is a structural account of why robustness fails and why Antifragility is the right design target. Reading Zhang through the Incerto lens deepens both.
  • State-theory framework from Acharya and Lee - The Cartel System of States — Zhang focuses on internal Chinese regulatory dynamics; Acharya-Lee on the international state-cartel dynamics within which China operates. Useful for thinking about external constraints on Chinese regulation (e.g., why US-listed Chinese firms face cross-border-data-transfer rules).
  • Methodological cousin to Joseph E. Stiglitz - Globalization and Its Discontents Revisited — Stiglitz on IMF/World Bank technocratic failures; Zhang on Chinese technocratic failures within a different institutional context. Both insist that institutional structure shapes regulatory outcomes regardless of stated intentions. The IMF and Chinese SAMR are, in Zhang-Stiglitz framework, both tightly-coupled bureaucracies with weak feedback loops.
  • AI-policy adjacent to Nick Bostrom - Deep Utopia — Zhang documents the regulatory state’s shift toward technological-self-sufficiency post-2022. Bostrom asks what happens when machine-superintelligence is achieved; Zhang shows how a specific authoritarian state is currently navigating toward that outcome. The two books are about different stages of the same long arc.

Open questions

  • Will the dynamic-pyramid model continue to fit post-2024? Zhang’s framework is calibrated against 2020-2022. The 2023+ rebalancing toward AI / generative-tech / hardware (the “two new vectors”) may reshape the pyramid in ways the model does not anticipate. A 2027-revision of the book would have to rebuild or significantly extend the framework.
  • Is the volatility trainable? Foreign investors and Chinese firms could in principle learn to predict the cycle and act counter-cyclically (sell into easing, re-enter during crackdown). Whether learning happens at scale, and whether learning would change the dynamics, is empirically open.
  • Does the model predict any non-tech policy areas Zhang doesn’t cover? Healthcare? Education (after the tutoring crackdown)? Real estate? Each has the dynamic-pyramid features and may already exhibit volatility cycles that Zhang’s framework would have predicted — testing this is open empirical work.
  • What is the political-economy cost of permanent volatility? Zhang documents the unintended consequence of private-sector retreat (Chapter 10). Whether the volatility is worth it (vs a more stable regulatory regime) is a normative question Zhang largely avoids; for any specific policy reform proposal, this is the central question.
  • How do foreign regulators learn from Chinese experience? US/EU regulators have noticed the Chinese moves but mostly framed them as something to avoid. Whether some of the Chinese mechanisms (campaign-style enforcement, decentralized platform governance) have lessons that transfer is a live question Zhang opens but doesn’t fully address.

Research uses

  • Yang’s blockchain-regulation research: Zhang’s framework is the right vocabulary for explaining why Chinese crypto regulation has oscillated since 2017 (multi-vector pressure: capital-flight prevention, technology-self-sufficiency in DCEP, social-stability against speculation, agency-overreach by PBOC, etc.). Several blockchain-regulation events Zhang doesn’t cover map cleanly onto the dynamic-pyramid framework — useful for new analysis.
  • AI-policy comparative analysis: contrast the multi-vector Chinese regulation Zhang documents with US (sectoral + judicial + state-AG) and EU (rights-based + AI Act) regimes. Each has different vector structures. Zhang gives the China side; Yang’s policy work needs the comparative.
  • China-business strategy: predicting Chinese regulatory action requires identifying which vector is dominant in the current political cycle. Useful diagnostic for any specific China-business decision (entering, exiting, restructuring).
  • Reading other people’s research on China: Zhang’s framework is now a benchmark — papers / journalism / commentary that don’t engage the dynamic-pyramid model are operating with a partial frame. Useful self-discipline for evaluating other people’s China-PE claims.
  • Methodological influence on lobbying-network research: the four-tier hierarchy Zhang describes is a vertical lobbying-network structure. Yang’s existing lobbying-network analysis (with In Song Kim) tends to be horizontal (firm-to-firm coalitions); Zhang’s framework suggests a cross-tier enrichment — modelling how different vertical layers interact lobbies.

What I would miss if I only read this note

The case-study detail. Zhang’s argumentative power comes from the cumulative weight of specific Chinese cases: not just the headline ones (Ant, Alibaba, Didi, Meituan, online tutoring) but the dozens of smaller ones she traces through to illustrate specific framework features (the Law of Taobao in Chapter 7, the cross-border-data-transfer rules in Chapter 5, the food-delivery worker protections in Chapter 6). The note compresses them; reading the cases one by one is the only way to internalise what specifically goes wrong at each layer of the pyramid, and to develop a trained intuition for which kind of regulatory action is in the current pipeline.

The note also compresses Zhang’s voice — she is an unusually clear writer for an academic in this domain, and her own legal training (she clerked at the Hong Kong Court of Final Appeal; was associated with the Anglo-American antitrust bar before turning to China) shows in the precise framing of each regulatory move. Zhang is not writing as an outsider explaining China to Westerners; she is a participant in the regulatory community describing structures she has personally negotiated. That insider authority is the kind of thing the note cannot reproduce.

Finally, the figures in the book — particularly the four-tier-hierarchy diagram (Figure I.1), the volatility-cycle diagram (Figure I.2), and the antitrust case-study flowchart in Chapter 4 — work pedagogically in ways the note does not. For a reader trying to internalize the framework as a working analytical tool rather than a remembered set of bullet-points, the visuals are the difference.
