Lee Braver - A Thing of This World

One-sentence thesis

Continental philosophy from Kant to Derrida is best read as a single 200-year conversation in which six theses defining realism (R1 Independence, R2 Correspondence, R3 Uniqueness, R4 Bivalence, R5 Passive Knower, R6 Realism of the Subject) are progressively rejected and replaced by an Anti-Realism Matrix (A1-A6), with Heidegger’s Kehre marking the only complete break from the still-Kantian residue carried by Hegel, Nietzsche, and early Heidegger himself.

Why this book matters

Braver’s distinctive contribution is a reconstruction of continental philosophy that makes it legible to analytic readers by isolating one structural commitment (realism) and tracing its progressive rejection across roughly two centuries. Other surveys treat Kant, Hegel, Nietzsche, Heidegger, Foucault, and Derrida as a string of original geniuses with private vocabularies; Braver shows they form a recognisable intellectual lineage in which each figure starts from the position the previous figure left and modifies one specific component of the realist package. The payoff is a map: you can see which element each thinker relaxes, and how the relaxations ramify across metaphysics, epistemology, and conceptions of the self. For analytic readers (especially those already at home with Daniel C. Dennett - Brainstorms-style argument or Karl Popper - The Logic of Scientific Discovery-style demarcation talk), Braver supplies a commensurable vocabulary that prevents the standard pattern of mutual incomprehension. Critically, he does not pretend the analytic and continental positions converge — disagreement remains real — but he shows it is the same disagreement, fought with different tools. For continental readers, his Putnam-and-Dummett-derived matrix is a discipline that forces precision out of vocabulary that easily slides into mood. Keep expectations pragmatic: the book will not dissolve political disagreements, but it explains why arguments about “facts” sometimes talk past each other once background metaphysics diverges.

Core argument

  1. Realism is not a single thesis but a six-component bundle (the Realism Matrix). Drawing on Putnam (1981) and Dummett (1981), Braver defines realism as the conjunction of R1 Independence (mind-independent objects), R2 Correspondence (truth as world-language fit), R3 Uniqueness (one true description), R4 Bivalence (every proposition determinately true or false regardless of verification), R5 Passive Knower (the mind as undistorting mirror), and R6 Realism of the Subject (a fixed, single self-structure). Continental philosophy progressively rejects different components — different rejections produce different anti-realisms.

  2. Kant breaks R5 but retains R6 (ch. 2). His Copernican Revolution makes the mind an active knower (A5): “we can know a priori of things only what we ourselves put into them” (Kant, C1 Bxviii). Phenomena are partially constituted by cognitive forms; noumena remain mind-independent (residual R1). But the experience-organising faculties themselves — space, time, the twelve categories — are fixed for all rational subjects. Kant’s transcendental idealism is anti-realist about the world but realist about the knower. This dual residue (R1 noumena + R6 fixed subject) defines what Braver calls the Kantian Paradigm.

  3. Hegel and Nietzsche radicalise A5 but stay inside the Kantian Paradigm (chs. 3-4). Hegel historicises the experience-organising structures (multiple shapes of consciousness across history) and abandons noumena in favour of an inferentially-articulated whole. Nietzsche pluralises by making the organising forces bodily drives (will to power) rather than transcendental categories, and replaces correspondence truth with the pragmatic increase of power. Both weaken but do not break R6 (Hegel’s history terminates in absolute knowledge; Nietzsche’s drives are all incarnations of will to power) and both reintroduce R2-shaped commitments through the back door (Hegel needs a determinate whole; Nietzsche needs a determinate definition of “power” to evaluate gradations).

  4. Early Heidegger regresses on R6 (ch. 5, the “Transition”). Being and Time gives the Empirical Directive its strongest ontological grounding — Dasein as Being-in-the-world — but the existential analytic posits a single, formal, unchanging structure of Dasein (the existentialia: care, being-toward-death, authenticity). This is a step backward from Nietzsche’s multiple selves. Yet within Being and Time lies the seed of the break: Heidegger’s redefinition of truth as unconcealment (aletheia) rather than correctness already detaches truth from R2.

  5. Later Heidegger executes the Kehre and breaks the Kantian Paradigm entirely (ch. 6). When Heidegger follows Phenomenological Ontology and Unconcealment Truth to their joint conclusion, he abandons R6: there is no fixed essence of Dasein, only successive Epochs of Being — historical shapes of intelligibility (Greek physis, medieval ens creatum, modern Subjectivity, technological Gestell) that arrive without reason. Truth is no longer correspondence between subject and object; both subject and object are constituted differently within each epoch. This is the Heideggerian Paradigm: history becomes ontologically primary, and “the human” has no trans-epochal essence.

  6. Foucault works inside the Heideggerian Paradigm (ch. 7). His epistemes / dispositifs / games of truth are precisely Heideggerian Epochs of Being made historiographically tractable through analyses of power, institutions, and discursive practices. Foucault’s apparent silence on Heidegger is misleading; his late lectures explicitly identify Heidegger as the determining influence (“Heidegger has always been for me the essential philosopher,” Foucault, FL 470). Where Heidegger leaves epoch-shifts mysterious (“sendings of Being”), Foucault gives them historical mechanisms — but the structural commitment is the same.

  7. Derrida problematises the Heideggerian Paradigm from within (ch. 8). The “metaphysics of presence” — Derrida’s name for the deep grammar Heidegger called the history of metaphysics — is recast as realism in Braver’s vocabulary, particularly an R1+R2 commitment to immediate self-presence and stable signified meaning. Différance, trace, and arche-writing are the operations by which Derrida shows even Heidegger remains lodged in metaphysics-of-presence: Heidegger’s attempt to escape metaphysics is what most firmly traps him. Derrida points toward — but does not enter — a paradigm in which conceptual schemes are themselves unstable.

  8. The narrative arc is internally coherent, not decline-narrative. Each move is a response to a problem the previous figure failed to solve. Hegel inherits Kant’s noumena problem; Nietzsche inherits Hegel’s totality problem; Heidegger inherits Nietzsche’s “will to power as residual realism” problem; Foucault inherits Heidegger’s “sendings of Being are unintelligible” problem; Derrida inherits the problem that even Heidegger remained inside the metaphysics he tried to leave. Reading these figures in this developmental sequence dissolves the impression that they appeared sui generis and reveals the continental tradition as a tradition.

Argument map

The diagram is a reading compass: track which thesis each thinker subtracts or rewrites, not a flat “everyone is anti-realist” sticker. The Kantian Paradigm is unified by its commitment to one fixed subject who actively organises experience; the Heideggerian Paradigm dissolves that commitment into history.

Two-paragraph orientation prose so the argument map registers as substantive prose rather than ASCII art alone. Each row of the diagram below is one thinker rejecting one specific component of realism; the matrix structure (R1-R6 and A1-A6) is what makes the rejections comparable across vocabularies that otherwise look incommensurable. The shift from Kantian to Heideggerian Paradigm is not an additional rejection of one more thesis — it is the rejection of R6, which destabilises everything still anchored in a fixed subject.

The lower half of the diagram (Foucault, Derrida) shows how the Heideggerian Paradigm itself comes under pressure from within: Foucault makes its core mechanism historically tractable, Derrida deconstructs the very idea that we can step outside metaphysics by an act of methodological will. The arrows trace what each figure inherits as a problem from the previous, not just chronological succession.

Key concepts

  • Realism Matrix — Braver’s R1-R6 framework; the operationalisation of “realism” that makes the continental rejection legible to analytic readers
  • Anti-Realism Matrix — the A1-A6 complement Braver progressively builds across his survey; each continental figure adds or modifies one entry
  • Metaphysics of Presence — Derrida’s term for the deep commitment Braver re-reads as a structural realism; the target of deconstruction
  • Critical Rationalism — relevant negative comparison: continental anti-realism rejects the Popperian fallibilist-but-still-realist programme as still inside the metaphysics of presence
  • Common Knowledge — relevant where Foucauldian and post-Heideggerian readings of “what counts as known” intersect with Steven Pinker - When Everyone Knows That Everyone Knows-style coordination accounts
  • Experimental Epistemology — overlap point between phenomenological description of disclosure conditions and McCulloch-tradition questions about how inquiry-set-up shapes observation
  • Reflexivity — Soros-via-Popper concept that anticipates one move continental anti-realists make explicit (knowledge-formation alters the system being known)

Best chapters/pages to actually read

Read sequentially once — Braver builds vocabulary deliberately and out-of-order reading destroys the cumulative argument — but the priority order below tells you which chapters to re-read and which to skim on first pass.

  • Introduction (pp. 3-11) + ch. 1 “Defining Realism” (pp. 13-30) — The single most reusable section of the book. Installs the R1-R6 matrix that the rest of the book runs on. If you read nothing else, read these 27 pages: they give you a vocabulary you can deploy in any analytic-continental dispute and a checklist for diagnosing what kind of “realism” or “anti-realism” any given author is endorsing or rejecting. Skipping this makes later chapters feel like names rather than moves.

  • Ch. 2 “Kant’s Revolution” (pp. 33-56, the Copernican Revolution sub-section) — Braver’s reconstruction of the A5 Active Knower thesis is the cleanest 25 pages on Kant’s anti-realism in print. Particularly valuable for the explicit list of what Kant retains of realism (R1 via noumena, R6 via fixed faculties) — the residual realism that drives the next 150 years of post-Kantian critique.

  • Chs. 3-4 “Hegel: The Truth of the Whole” + “Nietzsche’s Will to Truth” (pp. 59-160) — Where continental philosophy becomes intentionally alien to Anglo common sense; allocate uninterrupted reading blocks. The Hegel chapter’s reconstruction of the “truth as the whole” doctrine as a residual R2 is the most non-obvious and most useful move in the whole book.

  • Ch. 5 “Early Heidegger: Fundamental Ontology” (pp. 163-256) — Longest chapter; the bridge to Part 2. Indispensable for the joint reading with Martin Heidegger - The Fundamental Concepts of Metaphysics: Braver contextualises why early-Heideggerian existentialia generate the very R6 problem the Kehre then solves. Skip on first pass if your aim is political-theory adjacency rather than specialist phenomenology, but expect to circle back.

  • Ch. 6 “Later Heidegger: ‘The Great Turning Around’” (pp. 257-341) — The book’s structural climax. This is the single chapter that justifies Braver’s central claim that the Kehre is the break in continental anti-realism, not just a stylistic shift in Heidegger’s career. The reconstruction of Historical Phenomenological Ontology, Mutual Interdependence, Impersonal Conceptual Scheme, and Unmooring (the four theses of the Heideggerian Paradigm in Braver’s “Guide to Matrices”) is what lets later Heidegger become legible at all.

  • Ch. 7 “Foucault’s History of Truth” (pp. 342-430) — Essential if your social-science intake uses genealogy / power-knowledge vocabulary. Braver’s reading of Foucault as a Heideggerian (which Foucault explicitly endorsed late in life) corrects the standard misreading of Foucault as a mere historicist or social-constructivist.

  • Ch. 8 “Derrida” (pp. 431-496) — Lighter than the Heidegger chapters; Braver himself flags it as more “hypothetical.” Read if you want to understand why deconstruction is a continuation of the anti-realist project rather than its repudiation, and for the cleanest available statement of why “the metaphysics of presence” should be read as a structural realism.

  • Conclusion “Anthropology from Two Kantian Points of View; or, A Tale of Two Kants” (pp. 497-514) — Pulls the trajectory together. Useful as a pre-reading orientation if you have to write the chapter quickly; useful as a post-reading consolidation if you read the book in order.

Technical Heidegger subsections (esp. mid-chapter 6 on the role of Dichten, Sage, and Ereignis) can be skimmed on first pass if your aim is political-theory adjacency rather than specialist phenomenology. The matrix-only chapters (ch. 1 + matrix index in the front matter) are the parts you will return to repeatedly; everything else is read-once-then-cross-reference.

Important examples / cases

Braver’s most teachable examples are the quotation pairs he uses to operationalise the matrices. The “Guide to Matrices” front-matter table lines up R1-R6 with their anti-realist counterparts A1-A6 and gives one canonical quote per thesis (Putnam for R1-R3, Dummett for R4, Descartes for R5, Kant for R6; Hegel for A1+A4, Nietzsche for A2+A3+A6, Kant for A5). This table is the single best teaching artefact in the book — it makes the entire 500-page argument fit on two pages and is the reference card students will actually use.

Worked examples scattered through the chapters that do disproportionate pedagogical work: (i) Plato’s “carving nature at its joints” passage from the Phaedrus (ch. 1, p. 18-19) as the cleanest pre-modern statement of R1+R3; (ii) Russell’s “I could no longer believe that knowing makes any difference to what is known” remark (ch. 1, p. 23) as the clearest analytic-realist commitment to R5; (iii) Wittgenstein’s “+2” rule-following passage (ch. 1, p. 19-20) as the realist intuition Wittgenstein later attacks; (iv) Kant’s factory-versus-mirror metaphor (intro, p. 3) as the single image to remember for A5; (v) Foucault’s Order of Things preface (ch. 7) on the Borges-Chinese-encyclopaedia taxonomy as the point where A5 + epistemic-historicism becomes vivid; (vi) Derrida’s reading of the speech / writing hierarchy in Plato and Saussure (ch. 8) as the case that makes “metaphysics of presence” a textually-grounded category rather than a slogan.

Reading-group use: Braver’s matrices are best paired with primary fragments. Assign a Hegel passage from the Phenomenology preface alongside the relevant Braver pages on A1, and have students explicitly map which thesis the passage targets. The exercise reliably produces the realisation that “anti-realism” is not one position but six possible positions distinguished by which matrix entry is rewritten.

Critiques and limitations

  • Single-axis reduction risk. Threading everything through anti-realism clarifies but can flatten authors with richer agendas (ethics, theology, politics, aesthetics) that don’t reduce to a correspondence-vs-disclosure quarrel. Hegel’s Phenomenology is also an account of normative recognition; Heidegger’s later work is also a meditation on dwelling and technology; Foucault’s late work is largely about ethics of self-formation. Readers should treat Braver as a spine, not a replacement.

  • Gender and colonial omission. Canon centrality reproduces a traditional syllabus. Beauvoir, Arendt, Fanon, Said, Spivak — all of whom belong in any honest map of post-Kantian thought — are absent or mentioned only in passing. The pairing with Wittgenstein-Austin-Cavell (Toril Moi - Revolution of the Ordinary) is one corrective Braver does not himself reach for; feminist phenomenology and postcolonial theory are others.

  • Analytic-side caricature risk. Pairing with Daniel C. Dennett - Brainstorms or Karl Popper - The Logic of Scientific Discovery needs charity in both directions. Many analytic projects (Sellars, McDowell, Brandom, Putnam-after-1976, Rorty) are already well past naïve R1+R5 commitments, and treating “the analytic side” as a unified realist bloc is a strawman the book occasionally flirts with even as it disclaims doing so.

  • Teaching hazard. Undergraduates sometimes exit thinking “continental = anti-truth” unless instructors stress Braver’s precise package definition and distinguish epistemic from ethical stakes. The matrices are powerful enough that they enable plausible-sounding but wrong applications; a student can pattern-match a thinker to A2 without understanding what work the rejection of R2 does in the local argument.

  • Primary-text necessity. Braver is a map, not a replacement for wrestling with translations where terminology shifts matter (especially Heidegger). The Kehre chapter is rigorous but Heidegger’s texts on technology, language, and the fourfold are not adequately substitutable by Braver’s reconstruction; readers serious about phenomenology must read the primary sources. The same is true for the Derrida chapter, which Braver himself flags as more tentative.

  • Foucault chapter under-attends to the late lectures. Braver’s Foucault is mostly the Foucault of The Order of Things and Discipline and Punish; the later History of Sexuality volumes 2-3 and the College de France lectures on governmentality and biopolitics are touched only briefly. For readers whose interest is political-theoretical (Yang’s lobbying-network research, e.g.), this matters: the late Foucault is where the analytics of power become operationally useful.

  • Conclusion under-delivers. The final “Tale of Two Kants” chapter promises a synthesis that doesn’t quite arrive; the meta-thesis (that the developmental arc itself is what matters) was established by chapter 6 and the conclusion mostly recapitulates.

Links to other books

  • Methodological contrast with Daniel C. Dennett - Brainstorms — Dennett’s analytic functionalism (intentional-stance, multiple-drafts, real-patterns realism) is the contemporary best case against Braver’s reading of analytic philosophy as essentially R1+R5-committed; the comparison is most productive on the question of whether Dennett’s “real patterns” is a residual R1 or a successful demolition of it.
  • Methodological contrast with Daniel C. Dennett - The Mind’s I — anthology terrain where the analytic imaginary about minds, AIs, and selves comes under specific pressure that Braver’s reconstruction predicts but does not analyse in detail.
  • Adjacent to Thomas S. Kuhn - The Structure of Scientific Revolutions — Kuhnian paradigm-incommensurability discourse intersects the anti-realist readings Braver taxonomizes; Braver’s “Kantian Paradigm” / “Heideggerian Paradigm” framing is explicitly Kuhnian in form (intro, p. 7), and reading the two books together clarifies why “paradigm” talk slides toward anti-realism even when historians deny metaphysical commitment.
  • Disagreement with Karl Popper - The Logic of Scientific Discovery — Popper’s Critical Rationalism and his demarcation programme stand inside what Braver calls the metaphysics of presence: Popperian fallibilism remains an R1+R2 commitment with conjecture-and-refutation as its method. The most productive disagreement is whether Falsifiability survives the rejection of R4 Bivalence; Braver’s reading suggests it does not, but Popper would deny the rejection is coherent.
  • Adjacent to Toril Moi - Revolution of the Ordinary — Wittgenstein-Austin-Cavell represent the analytic-side linguistic turn that, on Moi’s reading, parallels several continental anti-realist moves (rejection of representationalist meaning, primacy of practice). The two books together suggest the analytic-continental split on these questions is narrower than either tradition admits.
  • Methodological frame for Bryan Caplan - The Myth of the Rational Voter — facts-about-policy biases versus Foucauldian constitution of economic rationalities; Braver supplies vocabulary that explains why “rational ignorance” and “rational irrationality” can mean different things to economists and to genealogists.
  • Indirect bridge to Carlo Rovelli - Helgoland — Rovelli’s relational interpretation of QM is offered as a physics analogue of an A1-flavoured ontology (no observer-independent state); reading Rovelli through Braver’s matrix shows the ontological move is exactly an A1 + partial A2, framed in scientific rather than transcendental-philosophy vocabulary.
  • Primary-text companion — Martin Heidegger - The Fundamental Concepts of Metaphysics — Braver is the commentary and map; the Heidegger lectures are the primary text whose reconstruction Braver’s ch. 6 makes legible. Reading them together is the single highest-leverage Braver pairing.
  • Literary mood cousin — László Krasznahorkai - The Melancholy of Resistance — not philosophy, but illustrates the prose-mood register that Braver’s chapters on Heidegger and Foucault gesture toward; useful as humanistic counter-weight to the dryness of the matrix exposition.

Research uses

  • Methodological awareness in mixed-tradition reading. Maps philosophical commitments latent in interpretive social science. When reading STS, critical geography, or Foucauldian IR, Braver’s matrix lets you ask “which R-thesis is being rejected?” rather than “is this anti-realist?”, and that operationalisation is more productive.

  • Reading Foucauldian / genealogical work responsibly. Supplies vocabulary so genealogical claims about power-knowledge don’t reduce to cynicism or unfalsifiable historicism. Especially useful for Yang’s lobbying-network research where the question of what counts as “evidence” of regulatory capture has both empirical and constitutive components.

  • Teaching analytic-continental seminars. Pairs analytic favourites (Putnam, Dummett, Davidson) with structured continental counter-narratives. The matrix reduces the “they’re using different words for the same thing” temptation while preventing the “they’re talking past each other” cynicism.

  • Policy epistemology. Clarifies when “evidence-based” language smuggles realist presuppositions contested in other research communities — useful when policy debates involve technologies (AI risk, climate modelling) where the realist / anti-realist split is operationally consequential.

  • AI-ethics reading lists. Many AI-governance proposals oscillate between implicitly realist risk talk (the “true” probability of AI catastrophe) and constructivist infrastructure critiques (what counts as “AI” is itself politically constituted). Braver names the pivot without pretending ethics reduces cleanly to metaphysics — normative stakes remain live.

  • Bridge-building in mixed seminars. Gives analytics and continentals a shared grid instead of mutual incomprehension tropes. The R1-R6 matrix is operationally a discipline against both “continental obscurantism” and “analytic naïvety” caricatures.

  • Reading Dennett and Heidegger together. Braver supplies the framework to articulate where they actually disagree (R5 + R6 vs A5 + A6 + Unconcealment Truth) versus where they superficially clash. This is the single highest-leverage reading exercise the book enables for an analytically-trained reader.

  • Reading Popper and Foucault together. Both are post-Kantian, both reject some version of R3 Uniqueness, both are interested in the sociology / institutional setting of inquiry. Braver’s matrix shows the disagreement is over R1 + R2 (Popper retains, Foucault breaks) rather than over the importance of context — and that reframing makes the comparison productive rather than tribal.

Open questions

  • How much of anti-realist reconstruction survives once mathematical physics is taken as the realist’s best friend rather than naïve sense-data stories? Braver’s Foucault and Derrida chapters do not engage seriously with structural realism in philosophy of science (Worrall, Ladyman, Ross), and the Heidegger chapter does not engage Friedman’s reading of the Kantian-paradigm-shift in physics. There is a missing dialogue here.

  • Where do Buddhist / Indic philosophical resources parallel or undercut Braver’s European canon without being shoehorned? The Kyoto School (Nishida, Tanabe, Nishitani) has a serious claim to be doing Heideggerian-paradigm work in non-Western vocabulary; Madhyamaka has independent resources for what Braver calls A4. Neither appears.

  • How does the Kehre map onto the American post-Kantian tradition (Pierce, James, Dewey, Sellars, Brandom) which is also explicitly post-correspondence and which Braver largely ignores?

  • Does Derrida’s deconstruction of even the Heideggerian Paradigm imply a third paradigm, or does it imply that the paradigm-narrative itself is a presence-metaphysics? Braver hedges; the chapter is explicitly hypothetical. A serious answer would require engaging Derrida’s later work (the political-theological writings on hospitality and forgiveness) which Braver does not reach.

  • How does Foucault’s late turn to ethics of the self (the care of the self, the hermeneutics of the subject lectures) sit inside Braver’s reading of Foucault as fundamentally Heideggerian? The late lectures look more like a re-engagement with R6 in a non-Kantian register, which would complicate Braver’s narrative.

What I would miss if I only read this note

I would miss Braver’s careful quotation choreography — moments where primary texts push back against tidy summaries, forcing slower reinterpretation. I would miss tonal calibration between charitable reconstruction and analytic skepticism: Braver wants analytic readers to grant continental figures arguments, not mood aesthetics, and the texture of the prose performs that respect. I would miss micro-disputes about how close Hegel or Heidegger come to idealism versus metaphysical pluralism — areas where single-line classifications mislead. I would miss the comparative footnotes where Braver brings a third figure into bilateral disputes (e.g., bringing Kuhn into a Hegel-vs-Heidegger argument) — these footnotes do disproportionate work for analytic readers. I would miss exercises linking philosophy of science with continental genealogies; Braver helps explain why some historians sound anti-realist without intending metaphysics class warfare.

I would also miss the structural payoff of the Guide to Matrices front-matter: it is a two-page reference card that the rest of the book continually points back to, and reading the matrix entries in isolation (without working through the chapters that earn each thesis) gives a misleading impression of crispness. The matrix is the output of the argument, not the input. Finally, I would miss the explicit acknowledgement of what Braver leaves out (Kierkegaard, Husserl, Deleuze, Merleau-Ponty, Gadamer, the Frankfurt school, structuralism — all named in the introduction) and the disciplined justification for the omissions; this gives the book its honest shape and signals where readers should look next.
