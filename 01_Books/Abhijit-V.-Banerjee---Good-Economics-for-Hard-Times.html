Abhijit V. Banerjee - Good Economics for Hard Times

One-sentence thesis

The polarized rich-world debate over migration, trade, growth, automation, climate, taxes, and welfare is conducted at the wrong level — driven by ideology and identity rather than evidence — and the rebuilding requires a patient empirical reconstruction of how rich-world labor markets, consumption preferences, growth dynamics, and political coalitions actually behave; that reconstruction reveals a master pathology, the sticky economy (workers, capital, firms, and ideas all move much more slowly than textbook models assume), and supports a policy program of dignity-preserving social transfers, place-based industrial support, revenue-neutral carbon taxes, progressive wealth taxation, and an active social state whose legitimacy is restored by visibly working — the rich-world successor and structural counterpart to the authors’ poor-world program in Poor Economics.

Why this book matters

Good Economics for Hard Times is the rich-world successor to Poor Economics (2011). Where Poor Economics applied the RCT-grounded, mechanism-focused, anti-grand-theory empirical program of the J-PAL school to poor-world problems (food, health, education, fertility, credit, savings, business, politics in developing countries), Good Economics applies the same methodological toolkit to rich-world problems where popular intuitions are equally wrong — immigration, trade, growth, climate, automation, government, social policy — in the post-2008, post-Brexit, post-Trump, post-Yellow-Vests political landscape. The methodological identity is exact (the same authors, the same RCT-and-mechanism framework, the same insistence that “good economics is the least strident”); the continents and the policies differ. Together with Poor Economics, the two books form the canonical popular statement of the J-PAL school’s worldview.

For Yang’s purposes, the book matters as the operating manual for empirically-disciplined liberal policy in an era of rich-world political dysfunction. The book diagnoses the pathologies of US/UK/French/Indian policy debate and supplies a programme that does not retreat into post-2016 despair: progressive taxation, place-based industrial policy, dignity-preserving social transfers, carbon-tax-as-redistribution, expanded TAA-style trade-and-automation-loser support. The book engages seriously with Trump-era politics (Mexican migrant rhetoric, China shock, AI displacement, Yellow Vests) without descending into either elite condescension (“the deplorables”) or fatalist accommodation (“populism is what voters want”). Its analytic posture — that voters’ preferences are context-shaped (ch. 4), that policy can shift the context, that public conversation can be restored if the substance is delivered with humility — is the most usable framework for thinking about democratic policy reform in 2026.

For specific live questions across Yang’s research domains: (i) the sticky economy concept directly applies to lobbying-network research where reforms produce concentrated regional winners and losers; (ii) the chapter on automation (ch. 7) is the canonical reference for AI-policy distributional questions; (iii) the chapter on government legitimacy (ch. 8) is the framework for analyzing why Chinese-tech-policy “social credit” interventions face legitimacy constraints similar to those of US welfare programs; (iv) the chapter on cash-and-care (ch. 9) supplies the dignity-preserving social-policy template for any context (US, China, EU) facing automation/trade displacement.

The book’s stylistic identity matches Poor Economics: dense, conversational, evidence-saturated, generous with field anecdotes (Pak Solhin, Oucha Mbarbk in Poor Economics; Esther’s grandfather Albert in Argentina, Pak Awan, the Hyderabad dosa-seller in both books). The voice carries argumentative weight that compresses badly into bullet points.

Core argument

  1. Economists have lost the public’s trust because of bad communicators and the disconnect between expert consensus and popular intuition. 25% of Americans trust economists in their own field; on 100% of the steel-tariff question the IGM Booth panel disagrees, but 33% of public agrees with Trump’s tariffs. Three causes: TV “economists” are firm spokespeople with market-optimism slant; academic economists rarely explain reasoning publicly; economists are fallible (IMF growth-forecast error 2.8 pp, no better than guessing 4%). The book’s project is to model good public economics — explain the path, acknowledge uncertainty, “frequently the least strident.”

  2. Migration is wrongly debated. Scale is overestimated; effect is wrongly assumed. International migrants 3% of world pop in 1960, 1990, 2017. EU non-EU intake 0.1-0.5%/year. Italian survey: actual 10%, perceived 26%. Le Pen: 99% adult-male claim (actual 58%). Two RCTs: information about true numbers does not change policy preferences. The “supply-and-demand” napkin economics fails empirically (Mariel boatlift, Danish refugee dispersal, Israeli Soviet 1990 influx, US 1910-1930 great migration — none show native wage decline). Five mechanisms refute the napkin model: demand-side offset, mechanization deferral (1964 California braceros expulsion → tomato-harvester adoption 0%→100%, no native wage gain), task complementarity, migrants take jobs natives won’t, efficiency-wage-based incumbency protection.

  3. Sticky economy is the master concept. The deeper migration puzzle: people don’t move even when wage gaps are huge. Visa-lottery winners (Tonga-NZ 3×, Indian H1B 6×). Iceland 1973 Westman lava (forced movers 3,000+richerdecadeslater).Finland1944forcedmigration(forcedmoversricher25yearson).Bangladeshseasonal−migrationRCT(11.50 grant → 22% migration; information alone has zero effect). Nepal mortality-perception experiment (10× over-estimate; correcting → more migration). Causes of inertia: connections (Munshi Mexico-US drought), comfort and informal-insurance networks, Akerlof lemons-market for the unconnected, Lewis (1954) family-free-rider problem, Knightian uncertainty, loss aversion, self-image protection. US internal migration: 7%/year (1950s) → <4% (2018). Rich states no longer attract more population since 1990.

  4. Trade theory’s predictions don’t survive empirical contact (Topalova). Petia Topalova (PhD student, MIT) used 1991 Indian liberalization as natural experiment: tariff exposure varied by district. Result: more-exposed districts had slower poverty reduction — opposite of Stolper-Samuelson. Replicated in Colombia, Brazil, US (Autor-Dorn-Hanson China shock). Topalova was rejected by top journals but eventually got the journal’s best-paper award years later. Sticky economy explains it: workers don’t move, capital is locked in declining firms, multi-product firms don’t shut declining lines.

  5. The China shock is geographically concentrated and irreversible. Autor-Dorn-Hanson commuting-zone analysis: more-exposed zones lost manufacturing employment AND total employment (clustering downward spiral). No reallocation. Young men less likely to graduate college. “Deaths of despair” from drugs, alcohol, suicide skyrocketed. Replicated Spain, Norway, Germany. Trade Adjustment Assistance (TAA) works empirically (lenient-vs-strict caseworker variation: 10-year earnings gain 50k)butwastiny(23¢ofevery58 in extra trade-affected transfers; disability insurance ate the rest, a one-way street out of labor force). Aggregate gains from trade for US: ~2.5% of GDP (Costinot-Rodríguez-Clare) — small. Tariffs don’t help: 2018 Trump steel tariffs save some steel jobs but hurt agriculture (16% of US ag exports go to China).

  6. Preferences are context-dependent, not stable (refuting Becker-Stigler). The chapters on migration and trade show that information alone doesn’t change policy preferences. Why? Because preferences are not stable — they’re partly invented in context. Mug/pen endowment (Kahneman-Thaler): WTA 2-3× WTP. SSN-anchor wine bid: 200-350% effect. Robbers Cave: random group identity creates animosity in days. Statistical discrimination + ban-the-box: removing felony-conviction box increased white-vs-black callback gap from 7% to 43% (employers used race as proxy when they couldn’t see felony status). Stereotype threat (Steele): black students score lower when test framed as IQ test. French biased supervisor RCT: minority cashiers underperform under biased supervisors not because of overt hostility but because supervisors don’t visit their cashier stations. Echo chambers: 84% of conservative followers conservative; partisanship-from-one-sentence rose 55%→83% from early 1990s to 2007-09.

  7. Last-place aversion explains the politics of the working-class crisis. Wilson 1997 on black inner-city ghettos and Vance 2017 on white Appalachia describe near-identical pathologies. Vance acknowledged the parallel directly. The white working-class response to economic convergence with the historically lower-status group has not been solidarity but caricature-intensification: Reagan’s “welfare queen” was a black single mother in the 1980s when whites weren’t on welfare; now that whites are, the trope must be ratcheted up to “gang member.” Calling people “racists” or “deplorables” worsens the syndrome by attacking self-worth (motivated belief / Bénabou-Tirole). Restoring dignity through visible policy delivery is the route out.

  8. Growth is mostly mysterious; chasing it is mostly futile; serving the worst-off is the policy lever we control. Robert Gordon vs Joel Mokyr: economists have no good predictive theory of growth. The Trente Glorieuses (1820-1970) were historically anomalous; post-1970 slowdown may be a return to normal. Reagan-Thatcher tax cuts didn’t accelerate growth in US/UK. Misallocation (Hsieh-Klenow) explains more than aggregate scarcity for poor countries. The malaria-net case (75% drop in deaths 2005-2016, 582M nets distributed mostly free, after Cohen-Dupas RCT settled the price debate) shows what targeted welfare-improving intervention looks like. Sri Lanka and Guatemala have similar GDP per capita; Sri Lanka’s child mortality is comparable to the US, Guatemala’s much higher. GDP is a means, not an end. “It may even be better for the world if we did not find that recipe [for growth].”

  9. Climate is massively inequitable and there is no free lunch. 50-10 rule: top 10% of polluters cause 50% of emissions; bottom 50% cause 10%. North American 22.5 t CO₂e/yr; South Asian 2.2 t. Pollution kills now: 9M premature deaths/yr (Lancet 2015), 2.5M in India. Air conditioning paradox: HFC ACs save lives but warm climate. Energy-efficiency “free lunch” largely a mirage: Greenstone Michigan WAP study (6% take-up, savings 1/3 of engineer estimates), Gujarat firm-audit RCT (rebound effect cancels savings). Carbon tax is right (Nordhaus 2018 Nobel) but only if structured revenue-neutral with progressive lump-sum rebate (Yellow Vests problem). Habit and default-design matter: peak-pricing default → 90% retention vs 20% active choice; energy choices are habits and once habits change, taxes don’t hurt much.

  10. Automation will likely worsen displacement; current US tax code subsidizes excessive automation. Acemoglu-Restrepo: one robot/zone → 6.2 worker reduction. Luddite reality check: real blue-collar wages in Britain fell ~50% 1755-1802; recovered to 1755 levels only in 1820 (65 years later). “So-so automation” thesis: robots just productive enough to displace workers but not productive enough to raise total productivity. Tax code biases: payroll on labor, accelerated depreciation on capital, interest deduction. Most R&D goes to labor-displacing rather than labor-augmenting innovation. South Korea 2017 robot tax: only existing policy response. Bill Gates supports.

  11. The Reagan-Thatcher experiment failed. US top marginal tax rate >90% (1951-63) → <30% under Reagan; never substantially recovered. Wages stagnated for the bottom; top-1% income share returned to 1928 levels by 2017; top-1% wealth share 22% (1980) → 39% (2014). Continental Europe didn’t see this — the change is institutional/policy, not just tech-driven (skill-bias would generate continuous distribution-widening but instead the very-top concentrates). High taxes do not reduce labor supply (Switzerland 1999-2001 tax-holiday natural experiment: zero effect). Wealth tax could raise 2.75Tover10years(Saez−Zucman250M plus 3% on >$1B = 1% of GDP).

  12. Government legitimacy is the binding political constraint. US tax revenue 27% of GDP vs Denmark 46%. Even Danish-rate income tax wouldn’t raise much new revenue (top earners would stop being paid astronomically). Restoring legitimacy requires (a) economists stop the anti-government snicker; (b) attract talent into public service; (c) design programs that visibly serve citizens; (d) experimentally validate before scaling; (e) tie revenue to outcomes (revenue-neutral carbon tax). Privatization is not a panacea (Indian private schools no better than public; French private placement services worse). Anti-corruption transparency creates its own pathologies (Italian Consip costs more than independent purchase). Latin American CCTs (Mexico Progresa) shifted public conversation about redistribution by experimentally demonstrating cash transfers work.

  13. Cash-plus-respect is the social-policy frontier. UBI is fiscally infeasible at universal/subsistence levels in rich countries (3.9T/yrfor1,000/month US). Conditional cash transfers (CCT) work but cost 10% in admin overhead and exclude the most vulnerable through complexity (Delhi widow pension: 2/3 eligible not enrolled; Morocco water-connection 7× signup increase from form-filling assistance; SNAP “Golden State Advantage Card” labeling beats “food stamps”). Cash recipients use cash well, don’t reduce labor supply, don’t waste on temptation goods (119 countries, ~1B participants). The graduation model (BRAC/Bandhan/CGAP, 7-country RCT, 10-year India follow-up) — asset transfer + 18-month consumption stipend + literacy + savings + coaching — produces durable improvement that pure cash doesn’t. The Sénart “young creators” program (RCT-tested) shows what dignity-respecting active labor-market policy looks like: take applicants’ proposed projects seriously; support them as agents, not as objects of intervention.

  14. The book’s master meta-thesis: the four I’s, all economies are sticky, GDP is a means not an end. Extending Poor Economics’s “three I’s” (ignorance, ideology, inertia), Good Economics adds intuitions as the fourth I — the false-but-stubborn intuitions about migration, trade, automation, growth that govern public discourse. The empirical reality across migration, trade, automation, growth, social policy is that all economies are sticky. The policy program must be designed for that reality, not for the textbook frictionless-reallocation fantasy. Good economics alone cannot save us, but without it we are doomed to repeat the mistakes of yesterday.

Key concepts

  • Sticky Economy — the master concept of the book. Workers, capital, firms, and ideas all move much more slowly than textbook models assume. Generated by efficiency wages, screening costs, lemons-market for the unconnected, Knightian uncertainty, loss aversion, network attachments, and clustering. The empirical generalization that dethrones the supply-and-demand model of immigration, the Stolper-Samuelson model of trade, and the frictionless-reallocation model of automation.
  • Last-Place Aversion — the political-economy mechanism connecting working-class economic convergence with rising support for ethno-nationalism. White-Appalachian working class faces convergence with historically-lower-status black community → instead of solidarity, intensifies caricature distinction (Reagan’s “welfare queen” → “gang member”). Calling them “racists” or “deplorables” worsens the syndrome.
  • Universal Ultra-Basic Income (UUBI) — small unconditional cash transfer to every adult in a poor country, fiscally feasible (~1.7% of Indian GDP) and politically robust (no targeting, no stigma, no admin overhead). Layered on graduation programs and health/education. The developing-country answer to the false dichotomy between rich-country UBI ($3.9T impossibility) and conditional cash transfers (admin-heavy, exclusionary).
  • Aspiration Failure — the perceived-S-shape of returns to investment under uncertainty (this concept exists from Poor Economics; Good Economics extends it to rich-world contexts: white working class loses aspiration after deindustrialization, parallel to the parental-expectations mechanism in Poor Economics ch. 4).
  • Three Is — Poor Economics’s ignorance/ideology/inertia diagnosis. Good Economics adds intuitions as fourth I.
  • Statistical Discrimination — Phelps-Arrow racial-discrimination concept, operationalized via the ban-the-box RCT (removing felony-status box raised white-vs-black callback gap from 7% to 43%).
  • Stereotype Threat — Steele’s mechanism: minorities perform worse on tests when group identity is salient. Princeton golf experiment: race-primed → black perform worse; “natural athletic ability” framing → black perform better; “sports intelligence” framing → white perform better. Self-fulfilling.
  • Echo Chamber Polarization — Sunstein 2001 framing operationalized: 84% of conservative Twitter followers conservative; 92% of liberal messages from liberal sources; partisanship-from-one-sentence rose from 55% to 83% from early 1990s to 2007-09. Not primarily caused by internet but by political-strategy professionalization (Newt Gingrich 1994 + Cambridge Analytica + Cable TV Fox).
  • Motivated Beliefs — Bénabou-Tirole framework: people protect self-image; argue from defended position rather than evidence; resist information that would force re-evaluation.
  • Trade Adjustment Assistance — works empirically ($50k 10-year earnings gain for participants, lenient-caseworker IV) but historically tiny. The model for expansion is GI-Bill-style retraining-plus-stipend with place-based supplements.
  • Carbon Tax — Nordhaus 2018 Nobel idea, but only politically robust if structured revenue-neutral with progressive lump-sum rebate (Yellow Vests cautionary tale).
  • Graduation Model (BRAC/Bandhan/CGAP) — cash + asset + coaching delivers durable improvement; pure cash alone doesn’t.
  • Negative Income Tax — Friedman/Tobin/Nixon proposal; tested in 1968-72 New Jersey RCT (Heather Ross’s “$5M thesis”); confirmed labor-supply effects are small.

Key arguments created from this book

  • All economies are sticky — the textbook frictionless-reallocation model is empirically wrong — the master generalization, ch.2-3-7.
  • Calling people racists worsens racism — the operational political-psychology lesson from ch.4, supported by Bénabou-Tirole motivated-belief theory and the post-2016 anti-immigration donation experiment.
  • GDP is a means, not an end — the explicit ch.5 thesis; growth recipes are mostly unknown; targeted welfare-improving interventions deliver verifiable gains we can implement now.
  • The Reagan-Thatcher trickle-down experiment failed — the explicit ch.7 thesis, supported by Saez-Zucman income/wealth-share data and the Switzerland tax-holiday natural experiment.
  • Cash plus respect, not just cash, is the social-policy frontier — the ch.9 graduation-model + Sénart-young-creators thesis.

Best chapters/pages to actually read

  • Ch. 1 (“MEGA”) — the framing chapter. Establishes the IGM-Booth-vs-public-survey divergence framework that recurs throughout. The trust-deficit diagnosis (“economists like plumbers, not physicists”) is the methodological key. ~30 pages, highest-leverage entry. Read first.
  • Ch. 2 (“From the Mouth of the Shark”) — the strongest single chapter for understanding the sticky economy master concept. The supply-and-demand-of-migration refutation, the Iceland-and-Finland forced-mobility natural experiments, the Bangladesh seasonal-migration RCT, the Akerlof-lemons-market story, the Lewis-1954 family-free-rider model, the Knightian-uncertainty-vs-loss-aversion mechanism, and the Esther’s-grandfather-in-Argentina anecdote together build the most thorough case in the book for any single thesis. Indispensable for anyone working on labor-market policy.
  • Ch. 3 (“The Pains from Trade”) — the Topalova rebellion chapter. The within-country tariff-exposure-variation methodology is now standard but originally heretical. The China-shock mechanism, the Trade Adjustment Assistance failure, the carpet-and-software case studies for the reputation barriers facing developing-country exporters, the cluster-collapse story (Bruceton, Tennessee). Read for the full empirical rebuttal of trade textbook orthodoxy.
  • Ch. 4 (“Likes, Wants, and Needs”) — the most consequential chapter for political-economy thinking. The Becker-Stigler refutation; the statistical-discrimination + ban-the-box result; the stereotype-threat experiments (Princeton golf, Steele); the homophily-and-echo-chamber documentation; the Bénabou-Tirole motivated-belief framework; the contact-hypothesis 27-RCT review; the cricket-league competition-undermines-contact subtlety; the Wilson-1997-vs-Vance-2017 last-place-aversion convergence. Read for the deepest engagement with why preferences shift in the way they do and how policy can shift them back.
  • Ch. 6 (“In Hot Water”) — the climate chapter. The 50-10 rule, the air-conditioning paradox (HFC vs lives), the energy-efficiency-gap mirage (Greenstone Michigan, Gujarat firms), the Yellow-Vests-as-policy-design-warning, the revenue-neutral carbon tax with progressive lump-sum rebate. Indispensable for anyone working on climate policy or environmental justice.
  • Ch. 7 (“Player Piano”) — the inequality and automation chapter. The Acemoglu-Restrepo robot-displacement evidence, the Luddite reality check (1755-1820 wage decline), the so-so-automation thesis, the tax-code-biases-against-labor argument, the Reagan-Thatcher experiment failure, the Saez-Zucman wealth-tax math, the Switzerland tax-holiday natural experiment, the Case-Deaton deaths-of-despair documentation. Read for the most consequential single political-economy chapter for thinking about US/UK/French inequality policy.
  • Ch. 9 (“Cash and Care”) — the social-policy chapter. The UBI fiscal-impossibility argument, the conditional-cash-transfer admin-overhead critique, the take-up-failure documentation (Delhi widow pension, Morocco water connection, SNAP-vs-Golden-State-Advantage labeling), the cash-doesn’t-cause-sloth evidence, the BRAC/Bandhan graduation model, the Sénart young-creators program, the dignity-as-the-missing-variable argument. Read for the operational template for what to actually build.
  • Skim: ch. 5 (growth — the punch line “GDP is a means, not an end” is in the conclusion paragraph; the chapter is a long detour through Solow/Lucas/Romer/Acemoglu); ch. 8 (legit.gov — important but largely a setup for ch. 9); the conclusion (~5 pages, useful synthesis).

Important examples / cases

  • Iceland Westman Islands 1973 lava eruption (ch. 2). Random house destruction → 42% of those whose houses were destroyed moved; by 2014 those forced out earned $3,000+ more per year. Forced mobility produced gains; voluntary mobility was suppressed below welfare-maximizing level.
  • Finland 1944 forced relocation (ch. 2). 11% of population evacuated. 25 years on, displaced people richer than the rest. Same pattern as Iceland — pure inertia keeps people from moving when moving pays.
  • Bangladesh seasonal-migration RCT (Mushfiq Mobarak) (ch. 2). 11.50(onebusfare+2daysfood)→22105, sent home $66, raised family calorie consumption 50%. Half didn’t repeat the next year. Information alone had zero effect.
  • Nepal mortality-perception experiment (Maheshwor Shrestha) (ch. 2). Migrants overestimated death rate by ~10×; correcting that estimate increased migration. Wage information reduced migration (overestimating earnings 25%).
  • 1964 California braceros expulsion (ch. 2). Tomato-harvester adoption went 0%→100% in three years; in Ohio (no braceros) no change. Wages didn’t rise after expulsion. California temporarily abandoned asparagus, lettuce, celery.
  • Mariel boatlift (Card 1990) (ch. 2). 125,000 Cubans arrived in Miami in 6 months. Native wages: no effect compared to four similar US cities. Borjas’s reanalyses contested but the consensus replicates the no-effect result across many natural experiments.
  • Topalova 1991 Indian liberalization within-country exposure analysis (ch. 3). Districts more-exposed to tariff cuts had slower poverty reduction — opposite of Stolper-Samuelson. The methodologically heretical paper that opened the door to the China-shock literature.
  • Autor-Dorn-Hanson China shock (ch. 3). US commuting zones exposed to Chinese imports lost manufacturing AND total employment; “deaths of despair” rose; young men less likely to graduate college; replicated Spain, Norway, Germany.
  • ATA-Egypt carpet program (ch. 3). Took 5 years to land first significant order despite US NGO + Italian designer + dedicated intermediary. Documents the reputation barrier facing developing-country exporters.
  • Tirupur knitwear cluster (ch. 3). 1925 origins; 1978 Mr. Verona’s first order; 1990 exports 142M;2016peak1.3B. Now under pressure from China/Vietnam. Documents the cluster-as-shared-reputation mechanism.
  • Bruceton, Tennessee, H.I.S. plant closure (ch. 3). 1700 jobs lost; “ghost town” cluster collapse. Used to anchor the place-based-destruction story.
  • Trade Adjustment Assistance lenient-vs-strict caseworker IV study (ch. 3). Workers approved earned 50kmoreover10years.Theinterventionworks;itwasjustseverelyunderfunded(only23¢ofevery58 in trade-affected transfers).
  • Robbers Cave (Sherif 1954) (ch. 4). 22 randomly-divided 11-12-yr-olds → animosity in days → joint task → animosity dissolved. Iconic for the constructibility-and-dissolvability of inter-group preferences.
  • Ban-the-box ban-the-box natural experiment (ch. 4). Removing felony-conviction box from US job applications increased white-vs-black callback gap from 7% to 43%. Statistical discrimination intensifies when individual information is removed.
  • Princeton golf race-priming experiment (ch. 4). Same students; race-primed → black perform worse; “natural athletic ability” framing → black perform better; “sports intelligence” framing → white perform better. Stereotype threat operates symmetrically.
  • French biased-supervisor RCT (ch. 4). Minority cashiers underperformed under biased supervisors not from overt hostility but because supervisors didn’t visit their cashier stations. Self-fulfilling prophecy through differential management.
  • Gentzkow-Shapiro partisanship-from-one-sentence study (ch. 4). 1873-early-1990s: 55% accuracy; 2007-2009: 83%. Partisanship in congressional speech grew explosively after 1994 (Newt Gingrich + political consultants).
  • Greenstone Michigan Weatherization RCT (ch. 6). 7,500 households offered $5,000+ in insulation/window investments. 6% take-up; energy savings 1/3 of engineer estimates; no rebound effect. Energy-efficiency “free lunch” is largely a mirage.
  • Acemoglu-Restrepo automation study (ch. 7). One robot per commuting zone → 6.2 worker reduction; depressed wages; effects strong for non-college workers in routine manual jobs.
  • Switzerland 1999-2001 tax holiday natural experiment (ch. 7). Income earned 1997-98 went untaxed when cantons transitioned to pay-as-you-earn. Effect on labor supply: zero. Confirms high marginal tax rates don’t kill labor supply.
  • Saez-Zucman wealth tax proposal (ch. 7-8). 2% on >50Mplus31B = $2.75T over 10 years (~1% of GDP). Plus anti-evasion: global financial registry, multinational profit allocation by sales, regulating tax havens.
  • Mexican Progresa CCT (ch. 8). RCT-validated child-welfare gains; durable across left-right governments (Progresa → Oportunidades → Prospera). Shifted public conversation about redistribution across the developing world.
  • Indian widow-pension take-up RCT (ch. 9). 4-arm experiment (control / info / info+help / info+help+accompaniment). Even the strongest intervention got only 26% take-up of free money. Bureaucratic complexity alone excludes the most vulnerable.
  • Ghana bag-making + asset-transfer RCT (Banerjee-Karlan-Osei-Udry, ch. 9). Asset recipients produced more bags, especially complex designs, despite extra workload. Cash relieves cognitive bandwidth → people work better, not less.
  • BRAC/Bandhan ultra-poor graduation 7-country RCT (ch. 9). Cash + asset + 18-month stipend + literacy + savings + coaching. India 10-year follow-up: durable improvement in consumption, assets, health, happiness.
  • Sénart Young Creators RCT (France) (ch. 9). Disadvantaged French youth offered help to start their own businesses. Outcome: more employment, higher earnings, but less self-employment. The project is the entry point for confidence-restoration, not the destination. Effect strongest for most-disadvantaged.
  • Becoming a Man (BAM, Chicago South Side) (ch. 9). CBT-based program for at-risk teen boys. Reduced arrests 1/3; violent-crime arrests 1/2; raised graduation 15%. Dignity-respecting design.

Critiques and limitations

  • The book’s own RCT toolkit is poorly suited to its rich-world questions. Most of the J-PAL canon’s RCT evidence is from poor-world contexts. Good Economics leans heavily on natural experiments (Mariel, Iceland, Finland, Westman, Czech-commuter, ban-the-box) and on within-country exposure variation (Topalova, Autor-Dorn-Hanson) — not pure RCTs. The methodological identity claimed with Poor Economics is partial; the rich-world toolkit is more eclectic. This isn’t a flaw but it should be acknowledged: the J-PAL RCT brand is doing some unearned credibility lifting in the rich-world chapters.
  • The policy program is more sweeping than the empirical evidence. Recommending a wealth tax, expanded TAA, revenue-neutral carbon tax, place-based industrial subsidies, UUBI, graduation programs, and active labor-market policies all at once is essentially the social-democratic program. The empirical evidence per intervention is strong; the integration into a coherent program is largely assertion. Cf. Poor Economics’s methodological humility — its conclusion was “we don’t have a sweeping conclusion.” Good Economics edges closer to “here is the program.”
  • Ch. 5 (growth) ends without a clear thesis. The chapter accumulates the Gordon-Mokyr disagreement, Solow vs Romer vs Acemoglu, Hsieh-Klenow misallocation, the China-Japan-India trajectory comparison, and the malaria-net case study. The take-away is “GDP is a means, not an end” — but the chapter doesn’t really argue for this; it asserts it after a long detour through growth theory. The strongest version of the chapter would be 5 pages instead of 50.
  • The book underweights digital-platform political economy. Cambridge Analytica, Facebook’s algorithmic feed, Modi’s hologram-rallies, and Twitter’s polarization-amplification are mentioned but not centrally analyzed. A 2026 update would have to engage with platform-driven echo chambers as a first-class mechanism, not as a footnote to the chapter on preferences.
  • The book is light on specifically AI-policy questions. Written 2018-19, it predates GPT-3. The automation chapter focuses on physical robots and routine-task automation. Generative-AI displacement of cognitive-knowledge workers (paralegals, software engineers, translators, designers, illustrators) is anticipated but not analyzed in depth. A 2026 update would have to be substantially rewritten.
  • The dignity argument can substitute for analysis. “Treat the poor with respect” is a true and useful program, but it can become a slogan that displaces hard distributional questions. Some social policies should be paternalistic (mandatory vaccination, compulsory schooling); the book does not engage seriously with when paternalism is justified vs not.
  • The book avoids hard fights with allies on the left. The minimum wage literature (Card-Krueger vs Neumark) is barely engaged. Rent control. The empirical case for or against industrial policy beyond TAA-style transition support. The book is more comfortable critiquing market-fundamentalism than engaging with progressive-policy controversies.
  • The Latin American CCT success story understates ongoing problems. The book celebrates Progresa-style CCTs as having “shifted the conversation” but underweights the fact that Latin American inequality remains very high, top-1% income shares are not falling, and the political backlash against “welfarism” continues (Bolsonaro 2018).
  • External validity remains an issue. A handful of natural experiments (Iceland 1973, Finland 1944, Mariel 1980) are doing a lot of evidentiary work. The Bangladesh seasonal-migration RCT is replicated at small scale, but extending it to “the world’s poor” is a leap. Goertz-Mahoney would point out that the configurational heterogeneity across the book’s cases is much larger than the variable-effects-large-N tradition acknowledges.
  • The “all economies are sticky” master concept does not differentiate among economies. Some economies are stickier than others (the US is more dynamic than India, which is more dynamic than Mexico). The framework would benefit from a typology of stickiness with policy implications.

Links to other books

  • Direct successor / structural counterpart to Abhijit V. Banerjee - Poor Economics — same authors, same J-PAL methodological program, same anti-grand-theory commitment, same dense-evidence-and-field-anecdote prose, same “five lessons + three principles” tone. Poor Economics (2011) applied the toolkit to poor-world problems (food, health, education, fertility, credit, savings, business, politics in developing countries). Good Economics (2019) applies it to rich-world problems (migration, trade, growth, climate, automation, government, social policy). The thesis arc — patient empirical reconstruction beats grand theory — is identical. The continents and the policies differ. Reading them together gives the canonical statement of the J-PAL school’s worldview: the experimental method as not just a research tool but a public-policy ethic. Specific cross-references: the “three I’s” (ignorance, ideology, inertia) of Poor Economics become the “four I’s” (adding intuitions) of Good Economics. The illusory-S-shape of Poor Economics ch. 4 becomes the last-place-aversion mechanism of Good Economics ch. 4. The good-job thesis of Poor Economics ch. 9 (steady middle-skill employment as the structural exit) becomes the place-based-payroll-subsidy + graduation-model thesis of Good Economics ch. 9. The Mexican Progresa CCT result of Poor Economics ch. 11 becomes the central social-policy template of Good Economics ch. 8-9. The ultra-poor graduation program (BRAC/Bandhan) of Poor Economics ch. 9 becomes the canonical social-policy answer of Good Economics ch. 9.
  • Macro-institutional complement to Joseph E. Stiglitz - Globalization and Its Discontents Revisited — Stiglitz’s macro-IMF/World-Bank critique of capital-account-liberalization-causing-East-Asia-crisis is the macro version of Banerjee-Duflo’s micro observation that trade liberalization without transition support produces concentrated local destruction. Both authors argue that institutional structure matters more than the textbook market-fundamentalist consensus admits. Stiglitz on macro-financial fragility under globalization complements Banerjee-Duflo on micro-labor-market stickiness. The two together form a unified critique of the post-1989 globalization consensus. Reading them together: Stiglitz explains why macro reforms imposed by Washington fail; Banerjee-Duflo show what micro-policy actually works in their wake.
  • Information-vs-preferences contrast with Bryan Caplan - The Myth of the Rational Voter — Caplan models voters as having stable systematically-biased preferences (anti-foreign, anti-market, make-work, pessimistic) over policy outcomes. Banerjee-Duflo (especially ch. 4) model voters as having context-dependent preferences shaped by motivated belief, statistical discrimination, stereotype threat, echo chambers, and last-place aversion. The Banerjee-Green-Pande UP “don’t vote on caste” street-play (cited in Poor Economics ch. 10 and reinforced in Good Economics ch. 4) is operationally a refutation of stable-preference Caplan. The deep-red Medicaid expansion ballot wins in 2018 (Utah, Nebraska, Idaho) confirm: voters can be moved by economic substance even while voting for ethno-nationalist congressmen. Caplan’s prescription (less democracy) and Banerjee-Duflo’s (better information delivery + dignity-restoring policy) diverge sharply, but the empirical disagreement is sharper than either author acknowledges.
  • Formal-econ counterpart to Daron Acemoglu - Microeconomics — Acemoglu’s textbook formalizes the same questions Banerjee-Duflo answer empirically. Acemoglu’s coverage of efficiency wages, statistical discrimination, search frictions, and labor-market dual-track equilibria provides the formal apparatus for the sticky-economy phenomena Banerjee-Duflo document. Reading them together: Banerjee-Duflo show that the formal models in Acemoglu do describe the world; the textbook intuition that markets reallocate is wrong. The two books are mutually validating.
  • State-theory frame for Acharya and Lee - The Cartel System of States — Acharya-Lee on the territorial state system as a cartel; Banerjee-Duflo on government legitimacy as the binding constraint on welfare expansion. Acharya-Lee predict that bad macro-institutions are self-enforcing through the state-cartel logic; Banerjee-Duflo show what reform within and around them looks like (Latin American CCTs, dignity-preserving social policy). Reading the two together: Acharya-Lee describe the institutional architecture; Banerjee-Duflo describe the policy moves possible within it.
  • Industrial-policy complement to Chris Miller - Chip War — Miller’s TSMC / South Korean / US Sun Belt good-job clustering is the success story Banerjee-Duflo’s good-job thesis (ch. 9 of Poor Economics, recurring in ch. 7-9 of Good Economics) describes in the abstract. Miller’s evidence: that good jobs at scale drove durable middle-class formation. Banerjee-Duflo’s evidence: that the loss of these jobs produced the China-shock destruction and the deaths-of-despair crisis. The two together confirm that industrial policy aimed at preserving good jobs is not a luxury but a necessity, and that the tools (place-based payroll subsidies for older workers, TAA expansion, public investment) are tractable.
  • Tech-regulation complement to Angela Huyue Zhang - High Wire — Zhang on Chinese technocratic regulatory failure; Banerjee-Duflo’s three-Is diagnosis applied to Chinese tech regulation would predict that SAMR/CAC failures are mostly inertia and ignorance, not deep oligarchic capture. The dignity-and-respect framing of Banerjee-Duflo ch. 9 applies to Chinese platform-worker / 996-culture / gig-economy regulatory questions. Reading the two together: Zhang gives the institutional ecology; Banerjee-Duflo give the design-detail toolkit.
  • Authoritarian-governance complement to Authoritarian Commons - Neighborhood Democratization in Urban China — Qiao on bounded democratic micro-governance under authoritarian regime; Banerjee-Duflo ch. 8 on micro-rule reform compounds even under bad regimes. Both arguing against institutional pessimism: Qiao on the grassroots level, Banerjee-Duflo at the program-design level. Both showing that careful design can deliver real welfare improvements under suboptimal macro-institutional conditions.
  • Methodological complement to Karl Popper - The Logic of Scientific Discovery — Popper’s falsificationism formalized; Banerjee-Duflo’s RCT program (and the natural-experiment / within-country-exposure-variation methodology in Good Economics) is the operational-empirical face of falsificationism applied to rich-world social policy. Each Topalova-style within-country comparison is a high-information experimental falsification test of a specific causal claim (Stolper-Samuelson, supply-and-demand of immigration, automation-rebound).
  • Behavioral-mechanism antecedent in Daniel C. Dennett - I’ve Been Thinking — Dennett’s intentional-stance framing supplies the philosophical scaffolding for “treat the poor (and the working class) as rational agents pursuing legible goals under their constraints” — the methodological commitment that distinguishes Good Economics’s ch. 4 + ch. 9 from older paternalist welfare literature. The Sénart young-creators dignity-respecting design is intentional-stance epistemology applied to active labor-market policy.
  • Future contrast to Lee Kuan Yew - From Third World to First (to be processed in B46) — Lee Kuan Yew’s developmental-state model is the explicit alternative Banerjee-Duflo do not recommend for the rich world. LKY’s argument: strong centralized state delivers fast growth and middle-class formation. Banerjee-Duflo’s counter: rich-world growth is mostly mysterious and not policy-controllable; the achievable program is dignity-preserving social policy. Reading them together will surface the deepest available disagreement on the appropriate role of the state in 21st-century policy.

Open questions

  • Does the sticky-economy framework generalize to digital labor markets? Remote-work, platform-gig, freelance, and crypto-DAO labor markets may have very different stickiness profiles than traditional employment. The book pre-dates the post-COVID remote-work transformation. A 2026 update would have to engage seriously.
  • What is the right specification of the four I’s? The book extends Poor Economics’s three I’s (ignorance, ideology, inertia) by adding intuitions. But “intuitions” largely overlaps with “ideology” — both refer to non-evidence-based mental models. A cleaner specification would be (i) information-deficit, (ii) ideology, (iii) inertia, (iv) identity-protection. The Bénabou-Tirole motivated-belief framework could ground this.
  • Is UUBI politically tractable in rich countries? Banerjee-Duflo recommend it for poor countries but argue rich-country UBI is fiscally impossible. But UUBI scaled for rich countries (e.g., $200/month per US adult universally) might be politically tractable as a partial cushion against trade/automation displacement. Worth thinking through.
  • Does the dignity-restoration thesis generalize to AI-displacement? The graduation model (BRAC/Bandhan) and the Sénart young-creators model assume that agents have or can develop legitimate plans. In an AI-displaced world where many workers’ plans are technologically obsolete, what is the analog?
  • What are the limits of “good economics” against motivated-belief politics? Once a population is in motivated-belief / echo-chamber lock-in (Trump 2024 voters, Modi 2024 voters, Bolsonaro 2026 voters), can patient empirical argument reach them? The book is hopeful but the empirical evidence on this is thin.
  • Why does Switzerland tax-holiday produce zero labor-supply effect? The natural experiment is striking but underexplained. Possible alternative interpretations (Switzerland is exceptional, taxes were already low, reference-dependence, etc.) deserve more careful engagement.
  • How robust is the BRAC graduation model to context variation? The 7-country RCT is impressive but the within-country variation is large. Implementation quality, local labor-market conditions, asset-type all matter. The book understates this.

Research uses

  • Yang’s lobbying-network research (with In Song Kim): the sticky economy master concept is the canonical reference for thinking about the political economy of policy reforms that produce concentrated local winners and losers. Trade-shock counties replacing moderate-with-extreme legislators (Autor-Dorn-Hanson) is operationally the same phenomenon as policy reforms producing concentrated lobbying responses. The book’s TAA-as-it-should-be (GI-Bill-style retraining + place-based supplements) is the right policy-design template for any reform that produces concentrated losers. The motivated-belief framework (ch. 4) supplies the political-psychology layer for understanding why losers’ preferences harden against the reform even when transitional support is available.
  • AI policy and labor-market displacement: ch. 7 (Player Piano) is the canonical reference. The “so-so automation” thesis (robots productive enough to displace but not productive enough to raise total productivity) is directly applicable to current generative-AI deployment in cognitive-knowledge work. The tax-code-biases-against-labor argument (payroll on labor; accelerated depreciation on capital) translates directly to AI-deployment incentives. The dignity-respecting active labor-market policy template (Sénart young creators, BAM Chicago) is the right model for AI-displaced-worker reskilling. South Korea’s 2017 robot tax is the operative policy precedent.
  • China-tech-policy research: Banerjee-Duflo’s “three-Is + intuitions” diagnosis applied to Chinese SAMR/CAC/PBOC reform predicts that most failures are inertia and ignorance, not oligarchic capture. The dignity-respecting framing of ch. 9 applies to platform-worker, 996-culture, and gig-economy regulatory design. The graduation-model (cash + asset + coaching) template applies to transition support for displaced manufacturing workers in China’s coastal cities (which the Chinese government is now experimenting with). The carbon-tax-with-progressive-rebate design (ch. 6) is directly relevant to Chinese carbon-pricing implementation.
  • Climate policy and environmental justice: ch. 6 (In Hot Water) is the canonical reference. The 50-10 rule grounds any progressive climate-policy design in distributional analysis. The Yellow-Vests-as-policy-design-warning is the cautionary tale every climate-policy designer should internalize. The default-design lever (Sacramento peak-pricing → 90% retention) is the operational handle. The air-conditioning paradox / Kigali 2016 phasedown is the case study for technology-transfer climate diplomacy.
  • Comparative-political-economy research (US-vs-Europe-vs-India inequality): ch. 7 (Player Piano) supplies the data and the framework for comparing how the same skill-biased technological change produced very different inequality trajectories in the US, the UK, continental Europe, and India. The institutional-policy explanation (rather than tech-determinism) is the operative thesis.
  • Personal-development / behavioral design: the dignity-as-policy-variable thesis from ch. 9 is portable to first-person design questions (career transitions, relationship rebuilding, personal-finance reform). The Sénart young-creators model — start with the agent’s own plan, take it seriously, support without judgment — is a usable template for self-coaching and for supporting friends in difficult transitions. The BAM Chicago CBT-based “gauge whether violence is warranted in this specific situation” is the personal-development analog of pause-and-reflect-before-reacting practices.
  • Education research and equality of opportunity: the stereotype threat + statistical discrimination + contact hypothesis triad from ch. 4 is the operational reference for designing inclusive educational institutions. The Delhi private-school RP (mixed schools dissolve playdate-aversion), the cricket-league-competition-undermines-contact result, and the METCO busing program are individually decisive case studies.
  • Public-policy communication and trust restoration: ch. 1 (MEGA) and ch. 8 (Legit.gov) supply the framework for communicating economic policy to a low-trust public. The “TV economist vs academic economist” distinction matters; the “explain the path, not just the conclusion” methodological commitment is the operational handle. The Latin American CCT precedent (RCT-validated programs survive across left-right governments) is the political-strategy template.

What I would miss if I only read this note

The book’s distinctive texture lives in its sustained engagement with field-vignettes that anchor each abstract claim — Esther’s grandfather Albert Granjon’s ten-year refusal to return from Argentina (loss-aversion at the family level); Pak Awan in Indonesia and his daughter at MIT (the entrepreneurial-pull side of the migration story); the Hyderabad dosa-seller and her three competitors who refuse to merge (the cluster-and-coordination story); the Mission District yuppie-eradication-project graffiti (gentrification’s failed resistance); Maheshwor Shrestha sitting in the Kathmandu passport office interviewing thousands of would-be Nepali migrants (the empirical labor of behavioral-economic field-work); Asaf-ud-Daula’s elites destroying-by-night-what-was-built-by-day at the Bada Imambara in 1784 (the perennial dignity question in social policy); Chantal and her husband in France losing custody of their disabled children for a decade because they asked for help (the punitive social state); the Sénart young-creators interviews where applicants spoke about dignity and self-respect rather than money. The book’s argumentative weight depends on the cumulative force of being shown actual people in actual specificity, then on Banerjee-Duflo’s restraint in not generalizing prematurely. The note compresses these to bullet points; the texture is irreducibly first-person observational. Reading the cases gives a trained intuition for which mechanism is operative in a new context.

The note also compresses Banerjee-Duflo’s distinctive voice — the dry self-deprecation about economists being “more like plumbers” than physicists; the affectionate-but-unsentimental Bengali-uncle observation that an Indian middle-class man washing his dishes for the first time abroad might “discover he quite liked doing something more hands-on”; Esther’s wry account of being cold all winter at the Paris School of Economics’s green building because she couldn’t manage to bring a sweater to the office until she did; the cool documentation of Steve in Ghana waiting for a job that exists only in queue. The voice carries the methodological commitment. The book is making a tonal claim that patient empirical work matters more than sweeping intellectualism, and the prose enacts that claim. None of this carries through into a derivative note.

The book also compresses, more than the note can, the cumulative force of repeated mechanism exposure. By the third or fourth time you encounter the sticky-economy mechanism — migration in ch. 2, trade in ch. 3, automation in ch. 7 — you have absorbed it as a way of seeing rather than as a labeled concept. Same for motivated belief (immigration in ch. 2, statistical discrimination in ch. 4, climate denial in ch. 6, anti-government rhetoric in ch. 8). The chapter-by-chapter table flags the recurrences; reading the book, you internalize the pattern. The note compresses ~400 pages of sustained pattern-training into a list of patterns, which is not the same thing as having the recognition.

Finally, the note compresses the quiet anger of the book — sharper here than in Poor Economics, because the rich-world chapters engage directly with policies the authors believe to have caused profound harm in the US, UK, and France over the last forty years. The Reagan-Thatcher tax-cut chapter, the trade-adjustment-failure chapter, the welfare-queen-ratcheting chapter, the Macron-leaked-tape moment, the Yellow-Vests-as-cautionary-tale: these are written by professionals who watch their guild’s bad ideas (trickle-down, trade-cures-all, growth-at-any-cost, government-is-the-problem) be deployed against vulnerable populations and produce predictable damage, and the prose has the controlled urgency of professionals trying to fix what their guild has gotten wrong. The note documents the policy positions; it cannot reproduce the controlled urgency. The book is best read once for the framework and a second time for the voice and the cumulative anger. The note can substitute for neither.
