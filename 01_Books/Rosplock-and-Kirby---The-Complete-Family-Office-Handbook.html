Rosplock and Kirby - The Complete Family Office Handbook

One-sentence thesis

A family office is a purpose-built operating system for an affluent family — its archetype, legal form, governance, IPS, ops stack, talent model, and philanthropy vehicle should be derived from the family’s enterprise history and intended trajectory rather than copied from peer cosmetics — and the Rosplock-led contributor team’s job is to give families and advisors a checkable manual for designing each module against that purpose, not a single “best practice.”

Why this book matters

Most existing family-wealth literature (e.g. James E. Hughes Jr - Family Wealth Keeping It in the Family, Tom McCullough - Wealth of Wisdom, G. Ng - The 38 Letters from J.D. Rockefeller to His Son) is value-formation and narrative literature: how to keep meaning alive across generations. Rosplock-Kirby is the operational complement — the one Wiley reference that walks through entity choice (LLC vs C-corp), Dodd-Frank Family Office Rule exemption (ch. 7), capital sufficiency stress tests (ch. 6), service-provider scorecards, IT segments, talent compensation bands, private trust company (PTC) regulatory regimes (ch. 15), and family bank legal mechanics (ch. 13).

For Yang’s family-enterprise context — multi-generational horizons spanning farm operations, services, and possible institutional coordination across Inner Mongolia and the diaspora — this book is the bridge between Hughes-style five-capitals diagnostics and the actual paperwork an attorney or COO needs. It also matters as the only B39 sibling to systematically code 2020-2021 regulatory reality: the Dodd-Frank exemption mechanics, post-COVID virtualisation of staffing, cybersecurity-as-substantive-risk, and the explicit “500M−1B is now the rough threshold” recalibration credited to Kathryn McCarthy of the Rockefeller orbit (ch. 1).

The handbook also matters comparatively because it is co-authored: legal chapters are by Withers Bergman partners (Sacks, Kambas, Guin) and Kozusko Harris Duncan (Padgett, Kozusko); operations by John Rosplock and Robert Kaufold; family bank by Warner King Babcock; governance by Barbara Hauser. This makes the book a deliberate corpus of practitioner voices rather than a single author’s polemic — a structure that loosely echoes how multi-principal coordination must work in real family offices and connects to Market for Governance themes where authority allocations are the substance of the institution rather than its decoration.

Core argument

Rosplock-Kirby’s logic is modular rather than monographic — a deliberate design choice that mirrors the book’s claim that family-office quality is a function of how well loosely-coupled modules integrate, not of any single module’s depth. The argument runs in eight numbered moves:

  1. Family offices live on a spectrum of archetypes, not a single template. Ch. 2 names six: founder’s office, virtual/administrative/compliance office, philanthropic office, direct-investment / investment office, family-business-focused office, and multigenerational-focused office (ch. 2, pp. 37-44). The choice should follow the family’s purpose and complexity profile (Appendix A’s complexity assessment), not a peer scan.

  2. Scale is necessary but not sufficient. Kathryn McCarthy’s recalibration — full SFO build-out begins around $500M-$1B in 2021 dollars (ch. 1, p. 10) — replaces the 1990s $50-$250M heuristic. But families with several billion sometimes choose not to build out an SFO, and “coordinator” offices (one family member plus bookkeeper) serve $50-$200M wealth bands. Net worth is one variable; willingness to govern and operate is the binding one (ch. 1, pp. 10-11).

  3. Embedded family offices are the default failure mode. Hosting wealth functions inside the family operating business creates conflicts: shared employees, scope creep, access to family SSNs and account codes by people whose loyalty contract is to the operating company, and intermingled records that compromise both confidentiality and litigation defensibility (ch. 2, pp. 30-32). Family Office Exchange (FOX) research is cited as the bifurcation case.

  4. Governance precedes product picking. Ch. 11 (Hauser + Rosplock) frames family-office boards as the structural vehicle that disambiguates family decisions from office decisions. Independent directors, advisory boards, family councils, and family constitutions are the named instruments. Without them, IPS choices and manager hires oscillate with the loudest principal’s mood — exactly the failure pattern Hughes diagnoses qualitatively in James E. Hughes Jr - Family Wealth Keeping It in the Family but here mapped to specific board mechanics.

  5. The Investment Policy Statement is the load-bearing delegation contract. Ch. 8 treats the IPS as an explicit articulation of risk, liquidity, time horizon, concentration tolerance, and rebalancing rules that binds the investment committee, the CIO function (in-house or outsourced), and external managers. The IPS is the primary instrument that converts diffuse family preferences into auditable manager mandates (ch. 8, pp. 214-217).

  6. Operational and IT risk is substantive, not residual. Ch. 9 (John Rosplock + Robert Kaufold) treats the choice between off-the-shelf SaaS and custom systems, the buy-vs-build calculus, vendor due diligence, cybersecurity attestations, and data archiving as first-class family-office decisions — Appendix B catalogues family-office technology segments. Cyber and vendor failures are tabletop-rehearsed as scenarios (ch. 9), not flagged as afterthoughts.

  7. Talent design has gender, hierarchy, compensation, and family-vs-non-family dimensions that compound. Ch. 10 documents the SFO org-chart archetypes, surfaces the gender-gap data on FO executives, structures background checks and chemistry fit, and explicitly addresses whether family members or non-family executives sit at the helm — and whether family member compensation is symbolic or market-rate. Bad answers here drive most multi-generational FO blow-ups (ch. 10, pp. 257-282).

  8. Philanthropy, family banks, and PTCs are strategic vehicles, not bolt-ons. Ch. 13 (Babcock + Rosplock) frames the family bank as a developmental-capital instrument — five principles: democratize, harmonize, customize, flexibilize, professionalize. Ch. 14 frames philanthropy as legacy-and-impact integration. Ch. 15 (Padgett + Kozusko) treats the private trust company (PTC) as the long-horizon trustee architecture — the chapter’s core claim is that the PTC’s primary objective should be fostering beneficiary development, not minimising audit risk.

The handbook’s meta-claim, threaded through all chapters: family offices that work treat themselves as deliberate institutional designs rather than service-procurement shells, and the difference shows up in whether the family can describe — in writing — why each module exists and what it would replace if it failed.

Key concepts

  • Family Office Spectrum — Rosplock’s six-archetype map of how purpose and complexity drive structural choice; the book’s central novel concept and the cleanest portable contribution.
  • Family Governance Architecture — the layered apparatus (board → family council → constitution → policies) that institutionalizes decision rights across generations.
  • Market for Governance — kinship-and-fiduciary allocation of authority among principals and agents under incomplete contracting; the family office is one concrete instance.
  • Skin in the Game — Hughes’s risk-alignment intuition gets mechanical implementation in the IPS, family-bank loan terms, and PTC trustee duty designs Rosplock describes.
  • Common Knowledge — family constitutions and documented decision logs work because they make commitments commonly known to all family members and advisors, defusing strategic ambiguity in succession events.

Key arguments created from this book

  • Embedded family offices structurally compromise both the family and the operating business — bifurcation is the rule, not the exception (ch. 2).
  • Net worth alone underdetermines whether a family should build an SFO — willingness to govern and operate is the binding constraint (ch. 1, p. 10).
  • The IPS, not the manager roster, is the load-bearing investment instrument (ch. 8).
  • PTCs should optimize for beneficiary development, not for trustee audit minimization (ch. 15, p. 406).

Best chapters/pages to actually read

The handbook is 425 pages plus appendices and an index. Read it as a manual, not as a narrative. Highest-leverage triage:

  • Ch. 1 (pp. 1-25) — Read first. The McCarthy $500M-$1B threshold (p. 10), the historical lineage from Rockefeller-1882 (p. 13) through Bessemer-1907 (Phipps, p. 14) and Pitcairn (1841-1916, p. 15) to Rockefeller Capital Management’s 138-year arc, and the Hughes Foreword (which alone is worth the price of admission for the four-capitals-as-obstacle-removal framing). This chapter is the book’s definitional layer.
  • Ch. 2 (pp. 27-50) — Read second. The six archetypes with the Mathile Case Study (p. 42) as the multigenerational worked example. If you skip this chapter you have no vocabulary for everything downstream.
  • Ch. 5 (pp. 103-137, Sacks + Kambas) — Read when forming or restructuring. The seven structural questions (legal form; who pays; ownership; management; tax efficiency; risk; process) with the LLC vs C-corp decision tree (p. 107). Densest legal chapter.
  • Ch. 6 (pp. 139-166, Rosplock) — Read when redesigning allocation or stress-testing. The Doryman Family capital-sufficiency case (p. 157) is the worked numerical example that translates IPS-style language into actual cash-flow projection.
  • Ch. 7 (pp. 167-194, Guin) — Read if approaching SEC thresholds. Dodd-Frank Family Office Rule mechanics, the SFO exemption (p. 170), Form 13F triggers ($100M institutional manager threshold, p. 183), insider-status reporting on Forms 3/4/5 (p. 178). Directly actionable for compliance officers.
  • Ch. 8 (pp. 197-228, Rosplock) — Read for IPS and IC design. Concentration discussion (p. 199), CIO in-house vs outsourced calculus (p. 223), investment-committee operations (p. 217). The chapter most likely to be re-read annually.
  • Ch. 11 (pp. 285-309, Hauser + Rosplock) — Read if relational failure modes dominate. The Kettering Family Governance Case Study (p. 302) is the deepest case in the book; Hauser’s hallmarks-of-good-governance triad (transparency, accountability, participation, p. 296) is the operational analogue of Hughes’s qualitative governance arguments.
  • Ch. 13 (pp. 335-359, Babcock + Rosplock) — Read before drafting intra-family loan documents. The Anderson Family Case Study (p. 355) and the five principles (democratize / harmonize / customize / flexibilize / professionalize, pp. 340-342) are the cleanest available framework for family banks.
  • Ch. 15 (pp. 389-416, Padgett + Kozusko) — Read before chartering a PTC. Regulatory-regime choice (p. 398) and the Parable Family case (p. 409) anchor the chapter. The chapter’s beneficiary-development-first claim (p. 406) is the most surprising single argument in the book.
  • Appendix A (Family Office Complexity Assessment, p. 419) — Use as an actual diagnostic. Walk through the assessment before discussing structure with counsel.
  • Appendix B (Family Office Technology Segments, p. 423) — Use as a vendor-evaluation rubric.

Skip on first pass: Ch. 12 (education) is conceptually well-covered by James E. Hughes Jr - Family Wealth Keeping It in the Family and the de Botton mentorship literature; return to it when designing next-gen curriculum specifically for FO context.

Important examples / cases

The book’s case studies are unusually concrete for a practitioner reference and earn re-reading:

  • Rockefeller (ch. 1, pp. 13-14) — JDR Sr. founded the family office in 1882 prior to the Standard Oil dissolution; passed 250Mof274M oil stock to JDR Jr.; JDR Jr. used pre-1934-gift-tax estate planning to create $20M generation-skipping trusts for each of his six children. Rockefeller Capital Management today has ~400 employees at 45 Rockefeller Plaza. The case anchors concentrate-then-perpetuate as the founding family-office logic and is the historical anchor G. Ng - The 38 Letters from J.D. Rockefeller to His Son gives qualitatively.

  • Bessemer Trust (ch. 1, p. 14) — Henry Phipps (Carnegie’s partner) put 50Mfromthe1907Carnegie−Steel−to−MorgansaleintoBessemer;by2004theholdingshadgrownto 1B divided among 100 descendants. By 1975, Bessemer’s 200-person staff was paying out 2% of AUM annually as an SFO; the Phipps family then opened to outside clients. By 2019, Bessemer had >$100B AUS, 2,500 client relationships, and fiduciary responsibility for 10,000 trusts. The case is the canonical SFO-to-MFO conversion arc.

  • Pitcairn (ch. 1, p. 15) — John Pitcairn (1841-1916) was Scottish-immigrant 1883 co-founder of PPG; by the early 20th century PPG produced 65% of US plate glass. Three sons formed Pitcairn Company in the 1920s; by 1951 there were 61 descendants; PPG paid >320Mindividends1938−1985.In1987thefamilyliquidatedandreconstitutedasPitcairnTrustCompany(Jenkintown,PA),whichhad5.8B AUM by 2019. Dirk Jungé’s stewardship-frame quote (great-grandson of John Pitcairn) is the book’s clearest emotional grounding of transgenerational stewardship as identity.

  • Mathile Case Study (ch. 2, p. 42) — multigenerational FO with formal strategic-planning cycles; the worked example for alignment, urgency, action as operational vocabulary.

  • Doryman Family (ch. 6, p. 157) — capital-sufficiency case translating goal-based-investing prose into actual cash-flow projections; the model walks the reader through scenario analysis with named lifestyle, legacy, and contingency goals.

  • Kettering Family Governance Case (ch. 11, p. 302) — multi-generational governance redesign; named after Charles F. Kettering III who is also in the acknowledgments. Demonstrates how family-council structure interacts with operating-business board.

  • Cordes Family Case Study (ch. 14, p. 378) — impact-investing-and-philanthropy integration; how a family office runs a foundation desk alongside taxable portfolios without forcing distortionary asset sales during liquidity events.

  • Anderson Family Case Study (ch. 13, p. 355) — family bank structure with intra-family loans funding next-generation entrepreneurship; the loan terms become the developmental-capital instrument Hughes argues qualitatively for in James E. Hughes Jr - Family Wealth Keeping It in the Family.

  • Parable Family (ch. 15, p. 409) — PTC chartering case; Padgett and Kozusko walk through the regulatory-regime choice (state of charter, public-policy environment, trust company taxes) that determines whether a PTC stays small-bespoke or scales toward commercial.

  • Godspeed Case (ch. 3, p. 75) — family decided to replace their incumbent FO via a structured RFP; the trigger-and-decision process, journey to alternatives, and final-cut framework are the cleanest narrative of FO procurement in the book.

Critiques and limitations

Several real critiques temper the handbook’s authority:

  1. Vendor neutrality varies by chapter. The legal chapters (Withers Bergman partners) and PTC chapter (Kozusko Harris Duncan partners) are written by attorneys whose firms make money from FO formation work; the family-bank chapter (Babcock) is by a family-bank specialist; the operations chapter (J. Rosplock + Kaufold) is by people running FO consulting and cyber firms. The book is upfront about contributors but does not always disambiguate contributor’s-business from practitioner-consensus. Reading these chapters benefits from triangulation against neutral sources (the SEC’s Family Office Rule final release is the cleanest cross-check for Ch. 7).

  2. US-regulatory and US-tax-centric defaults. The book mentions Europe, UK, Middle East, Asia, Australia interviews (preface) but the substantive regulatory chapters (5, 7, 15) are US-centric. Cross-border families need parallel reading on CRS, FATCA, banking-secrecy regimes, and the Singapore/Switzerland/Cayman/Bermuda PTC alternatives.

  3. 2021 publication captured pandemic shock but pre-dates AI fractional-CFO/CIO stacks. Cybersecurity and virtual-FO chapters are forward-looking for 2021; they lag the 2024-2026 reality of LLM-driven document-review, fractional-CIO platforms (e.g. Aleph, AdvisorEngine successors), and the resulting downward pressure on the McCarthy 500M−1B threshold.

  4. Case studies are real but selectively presented. The Mathile, Kettering, Pitcairn, Cordes, Anderson, Parable cases are illustrative, not adversarial. The book does not include a case where the family office failed catastrophically and the named lessons are post-mortem. This is a genre limitation — practitioners protect client confidentiality — but it leaves a survivorship-bias risk that should be balanced against Sebastian Mallaby - The Power Law-style failure-narrative reading.

  5. Less philosophically deep than Hughes or McCullough. Readers wanting why does wealth exist answers should treat Rosplock-Kirby as checklist and Hughes/McCullough as meaning. The handbook acknowledges this in the Hughes Foreword (Hughes himself frames the book as service-design for obstacle removal, not philosophical reorientation).

  6. The “500M−1B” minimum is itself a contestable claim. McCarthy is one expert; the book then shows family offices serving 50M−200M wealth bands as “coordinator family offices.” The internal tension is acknowledged but the reader should not treat the 500M−1B figure as load-bearing — it is a 2020-2021 working number from the Rockefeller-orbit advisory bench, not an industry consensus.

  7. The complexity assessment (Appendix A) is qualitative and self-administered. It is genuinely useful as a structured conversation starter but should not be treated as a calibrated diagnostic. Two families could score the assessment differently and still land at the same archetype, or score similarly and need different structures.

Links to other books

  • Operational complement to James E. Hughes Jr - Family Wealth Keeping It in the Family — Hughes provides the five-capitals diagnostic; Rosplock-Kirby provides the entity-formation, IPS, governance-board, and PTC mechanics that implement Hughes-style capitals work. Hughes wrote the Foreword (pp. xix-xx) explicitly framing the book as service-design for obstacle removal aligned with his four-capitals lens.
  • Practitioner-reflective neighbor to Tom McCullough - Wealth of Wisdom — McCullough’s anthology of practitioner essays on wisdom complements Rosplock-Kirby’s checklist orientation; they are meant to be read together.
  • Individual-discipline parallel to Stuart Lucas - Wealth — Lucas frames personal four-function discipline (earner, spender, investor, owner); Rosplock-Kirby builds the institutional FO wrapper that operationalizes those functions for families with enough scale to staff dedicated roles.
  • Trust-instrument adjacent to Goldstone et al - Family Trusts — Goldstone’s relational-trust-administration literature is the fiduciary-relationship layer the PTC chapter (15) presupposes; Rosplock-Kirby tells you how to charter a PTC, Goldstone-et-al tells you how to administer one well.
  • Historical anchor to G. Ng - The 38 Letters from J.D. Rockefeller to His Son — Ng provides the qualitative voice of the Rockefeller value-formation tradition; Rosplock ch. 1 (p. 13) provides the structural arc from JDR Sr.’s 1882 office to Rockefeller Capital Management’s present-day 400-employee MFO.
  • Failure-narrative counterweight from Sebastian Mallaby - The Power Law — Mallaby on venture / family-office direct-investment failure modes provides the case literature Rosplock-Kirby ch. 8 (direct-investment archetype) understates. Read Mallaby for survivorship-bias correction when the direct-investment archetype tempts the family.
  • Concept links — Market for Governance, Skin in the Game, Common Knowledge, Family Office Spectrum, Family Governance Architecture.

Open questions

  • How does AI-driven fractional CFO/CIO tooling alter the McCarthy $500M-$1B threshold by 2026-2030? Is the coordinator family office now viable at 25M−100M with LLM-augmented bookkeeping?
  • How should a Chinese-diaspora family with assets split across PRC, Hong Kong, Singapore, and US reconcile Rosplock-Kirby’s US-LLC-and-Withers-friendly-PTC defaults with CRS reporting, PRC foreign-exchange controls, and Hong Kong common-law-trust caveats? The handbook’s structural advice does not resolve.
  • The handbook’s gender-gap data on FO executives (ch. 10) is reported but not analytically attacked. What governance designs materially reduce the gap rather than describe it?
  • The PTC’s “fostering beneficiary development” priority (ch. 15, p. 406) is asserted but not measured. How would a family operationalize beneficiary-development as a board-level KPI rather than as aspiration?
  • The book treats direct investing as one archetype and the investment office as another, but does not deeply confront 2020s reality where direct investing increasingly blurs into venture-capital co-investment networks (Mallaby’s territory). When does the direct-investment archetype become a captive VC fund in disguise?
  • Cybersecurity is treated as substantive risk (ch. 9), but the book pre-dates the post-2022 ransomware-as-a-service economy. What incident-response cadence is currently below the line for a coordinator FO without a full IT staff?

Supplementary reading rhythm

Use the handbook as the spine but schedule quarterly red-team reviews where independent counsel adversarially challenges assumptions baked into the inaugural charters — documents ossify faster than law changes. Maintain a living decision log (why the IPS was tweaked; why a vendor was swapped; what the family-council vote was on each contested item) so generational transitions inherit reasoning, not only outcomes. When educating next-gen, translate governance sections into scenario role-plays before exposing naive participants to balance-sheet magnitude — procedural fluency first, numeric awe second. Build a vendor exit playbook before signing — documenting data-portability expectations prevents hostage crises when relationships sour. Pair re-readings of ch. 11 (governance) with quarterly fresh re-reads of James E. Hughes Jr - Family Wealth Keeping It in the Family to keep the why of governance alive while the how matures.

Research uses

  • Yang’s family-enterprise reference — for specific family-office design questions requiring entity-form, IPS, or governance vocabulary, this is the first-stop manual; pair with US-China cross-border counsel before any actual filing.
  • Law-school clinical pedagogy — simulation syllabi where students draft IPS clauses, family-council charters, intra-family-loan term sheets, and incident-response annexes; the Sacks-Kambas and Padgett-Kozusko chapters work as case-method readings.
  • Empirical governance studies — code handbook templates into variables measuring adoption of audit committees, CIO outsourcing, cybersecurity attestations, PTC chartering, and IPS rebalancing rules across FO archetypes; the FOX-derived embedded-vs-bifurcated typology (ch. 2) is a clean coding scheme.
  • Consulting onboarding — structured pre-reading before joint client calls so attorneys, accountants, and operators share KPI vocabulary around reporting latency, custody controls, and vendor evaluation. Appendix B (technology segments) is the cleanest shared scaffold.
  • Family-business case competitions — Doryman, Mathile, Kettering, Anderson, Parable, Cordes cases provide drop-in tabletop scenarios with enough numeric specificity for student teams; supply scoring rubrics emphasizing operational resilience metrics beyond investment alpha.
  • Reg-tech evaluation — compare ch. 7 Dodd-Frank Family Office Rule mechanics against current SEC enforcement letters to identify drift between handbook framings and live regulator priorities; useful for compliance-officer training.
  • Cross-cultural family-enterprise design — explicitly contrast Rosplock-Kirby’s US-LLC-and-Withers-friendly defaults with Chinese, Indian, and Gulf-region family-business norms; the contrast surfaces which parts of the handbook are universal (governance-precedes-product-picking; bifurcation-from-embedded) and which are jurisdiction-bound (entity-form, tax, regulatory thresholds).
  • Personal-decision scaffolding — for any individual contemplating either building or joining an SFO/MFO, the Godspeed case (ch. 3) plus the Family Office Complexity Assessment (Appendix A) form the tightest available decision rubric in print.

What I would miss if I only read this note

I would miss the texture of the contributor voices: Sacks and Kambas’s lawyerly dryness on entity-form trade-offs reads differently from Hauser’s measured-clinical voice on governance and from Padgett-Kozusko’s precision on PTC regulatory regimes; the mosaic is the book’s structural argument that family offices are necessarily multi-author institutions, and that texture is lost in any synthesis. I would miss the exact quantitative anchors — the McCarthy $500M-$1B figure (ch. 1, p. 10), the Bessemer $50M→$1B / 100 descendants arithmetic (ch. 1, p. 14), the Pitcairn 1973 $200M AUM and 1987 PPG-liquidation timeline (ch. 1, p. 15), the FOX bifurcation research methodology (ch. 2), the Doryman cash-flow projection numbers (ch. 6, p. 157), the Form 13F $100M threshold (ch. 7, p. 183) — that distinguish the book from the genre’s typical hand-waving and let an FO COO actually plan against named numbers.

I would miss the Appendix A Family Office Complexity Assessment as an instrument — the assessment must be walked-through, not summarised, because its diagnostic value is in the conversation it forces among family members during scoring. Same for Appendix B’s technology-segment taxonomy: copying the categories into a vendor-evaluation rubric is the actionable use; reading a paraphrase is not.

I would miss the visual scaffolding — the SFO org charts (ch. 10, pp. 261-267), the IC operating-cycle diagrams (ch. 8), the family-bank evolution chart (ch. 13, pp. 346-349), and the PTC regulatory-regime decision tree (ch. 15, pp. 398-405). These are the kind of artifacts a family office actually copies into its inaugural board pack; no narrative summary preserves them.

I would miss the practitioner anecdotes embedded in case-in-point boxes — the closed-door advisor gathering on risk-management best practices (ch. 2), the Dirk Jungé reflection on Pitcairn stewardship identity (ch. 1, p. 15), the McCarthy scale-recalibration commentary (ch. 1, p. 10) — these vignettes carry the affective weight that makes the abstract checklists rehearsable rather than ornamental.

Finally, I would miss the cumulative force of redundancy: the handbook is deliberately repetitive across chapters (governance themes recur in chs. 4, 5, 11, 13, 15; the family-business-vs-family-office split recurs in chs. 2, 5, 8) because in a practitioner manual, redundancy is availability — when an attorney reaches for ch. 7 mid-deal, she should not need ch. 2’s archetypes to make sense. A note like this collapses that redundancy and so loses the manual’s actual usability profile.
