Stuart Russell and Peter Norvig - Artificial Intelligence A Modern Approach

One-sentence thesis

The canonical 4th-edition AI textbook reorganizes the field around the rational-agent paradigm — every algorithm in the book (search, logic, probability, decision theory, MDPs, learning, deep nets, RL, NLP, robotics, vision) is presented as a way to compute or approximate the rational action under a particular environment regime — and then revises its own foundation in chs. 1.1.5, 17.2.5, and 28.3 by arguing that the standard model of fixed-objective rational agents is unsafe at scale and must be replaced by uncertainty-over-objectives + assistance games + inverse reinforcement learning so that machines pursue our objectives rather than their own.

Why this book matters

Russell-Norvig (AIMA) is the single most-used AI textbook of the past three decades and the closest thing the field has to a shared technical ontology. For Yang’s AI-policy and AI-tools work, it provides three irreplaceable assets at once: (i) the formal vocabulary (Bellman equation, Bayesian network factorization, A* admissibility, VC dimension, MDP/POMDP, attention) used by every serious paper in the field; (ii) the routing table between subfields, so that someone arguing about NLP capability can locate themselves on the same map as someone arguing about RL safety; and (iii) the alignment programme — Russell’s value-alignment-via-assistance-games formulation, which is the strongest mainstream-academic articulation of the alignment problem and the technical complement to Nick Bostrom - Deep Utopia and Sebastian Mallaby - The Infinity Machine. The 4th edition’s pivot from “rational agent maximizes fixed objective” to “rational agent maintains uncertainty over the human objective” is the single most policy-portable move in modern AI textbooks. AIMA also subsumes Simon Prince - Understanding Deep Learning at the architectural sketch level (chs. 22, 25) while offering the breadth Prince deliberately omits — search, logic, planning, probability, decision theory, multiagent, robotics. Reading Prince for deep learning and AIMA for everything else is the natural complementary pairing.

Core argument

AIMA’s pedagogical claim, distilled across 28 chapters and ~1100 pages, is that AI is one field, unified by the rational-agent frame, and the technical layers from search through alignment are a single stack of generalizations. The textbook argues this through nine sequential moves:

  1. The rational-agent paradigm is load-bearing (ch. 1-2). An agent is rational iff for each percept history it selects an action that maximizes the expected value of its performance measure. PEAS specification and the seven environment-property axes (observable / agents / determinism / episode / static / discrete / known) give the reader a routing table to the rest of the book — every subsequent chapter relaxes one or more axes. The standard model — pursuing a fixed externally-supplied objective — pervades AI, control theory, OR, statistics, and economics.

  2. Search is the primitive computational lens (chs. 3-6). A search problem is the 6-tuple ⟨S,s0​,G,ACTIONS,RESULT,c⟩. All informed search is BEST-FIRST(f) with f=g+h giving A*; admissibility (h≤h∗) suffices for optimality, and consistency (h(n)≤c(n,a,n′)+h(n′)) is the triangle-inequality strengthening that lets graph-search be optimal too. Every later algorithm — CSPs, logical inference, planning, MDPs — is a generalization of this template.

  3. Logic gives declarative reasoning, with formal soundness/completeness guarantees (chs. 7-11). The KB / TELL / ASK architecture, propositional logic with resolution / DPLL, first-order logic with unification, knowledge representation with ontological commitments, and PDDL planning — all united by entailment ⊨ vs derivation ⊢, the soundness/completeness duality, and the qualification problem that motivates the shift to probability.

  4. Probability handles partial knowledge in a way logic cannot (chs. 12-14). The shift from logic to probability in the 1980s-90s is, by Russell’s own framing, the most consequential methodological transition in AI history. Bayes’ rule P(H∣E)=P(E∣H)P(H)/P(E) + Bayesian network factorization P(x1​,…,xn​)=∏i​P(xi​∣parents(Xi​)) + conditional independence (d-separation) + dynamic Bayes nets / HMMs / Kalman filters is the four-piece compression that made open-world AI tractable. Pearl’s causal extensions (ch. 13.5) reframe inference once more by distinguishing observational P(Y∣X=x) from interventional P(Y∣do(X=x)).

  5. Decision theory wires probability to action (ch. 15). The MEU principle a∗=argmaxa​∑s′​P(s′∣a,e)U(s′) + the von Neumann-Morgenstern representation theorem + the value-of-information formula are a complete formal closure of “what should a rational agent do”. Russell uses this to argue that any coherent agent must behave as if maximizing some utility function — making the question of alignment unavoidable rather than optional.

  6. Sequential decisions = MDPs and the Bellman equation (ch. 16). The MDP ⟨S,A,P(s′∣s,a),R(s,a,s′)⟩ with discounted utility ∑γtRt​ has optimal value satisfying

    U(s)=maxa​∑s′​P(s′∣s,a)[R(s,a,s′)+γU(s′)].

    The Bellman update is a γ-contraction in ∥⋅∥∞​, so value iteration converges geometrically. This is the technical core that the second half of the book builds on (RL, robotics, alignment).

  7. Learning fills in unknown P, R, or hypothesis class (chs. 19-22). Empirical risk minimization, decision trees with information gain, PAC theory and VC dimension, MLE / Bayesian / EM for probabilistic models, deep nets via backprop on differentiable computation graphs, CNN convolution and parameter sharing, transformer attention Attn(Q,K,V)=softmax(QK⊤/dk​​)V. The textbook treats these uniformly as ways of learning a rational-agent component — a hypothesis class, a probabilistic world model, a value function, or a policy.

  8. Reinforcement learning unifies the prior layers (ch. 23). RL = MDP with unknown P,R. Q-learning’s update Q(s,a)←Q(s,a)+α[r+γmaxa′​Q(s′,a′)−Q(s,a)] converges to Q∗ under standard conditions; DQN scales it via deep function approximation; policy-gradient methods ∇J=E[∇logπθ​(a∣s)Qπ(s,a)] give the modern actor-critic toolkit; inverse RL flips the problem to learn R from expert behaviour. RL is the algorithmic spine of AlphaGo, AlphaZero, RLHF, and modern robotics.

  9. The 4th edition revises its own foundation: alignment over fixed objectives (chs. 1.1.5, 17.2.5, 28.3). The standard model — fixed objective, agent optimizes — is unsafe when the objective is misspecified, because a sufficiently capable agent will achieve precisely the wrong thing (King Midas problem; specification gaming). Russell’s three principles: (i) the machine’s only objective is to maximize the realization of human preferences; (ii) the machine is uncertain about those preferences; (iii) human behavior is the source of evidence about them. This is operationalized via assistance games (a 2-player game with the AI’s reward parameter θ as a hidden variable observable through human action) and inverse reinforcement learning. The off-switch theorem (Hadfield-Menell et al. 2017): a sufficiently uncertain assistance-game agent has positive expected value of information from human input, hence will not disable its own off-switch.

The cumulative claim is that the formal layers from search through RL converge on the alignment question — and that the alignment question is not external to AI, it is what the rational-agent paradigm forces you to ask once you get capable enough to deploy.

Formal landmarks

A list of the load-bearing formal results, with chapter pointers, that a reader who claims fluency with this book must be able to write down from a blank page:

  • Rational agent: f(p)=argmaxa​E[U∣p,a] (ch. 2.2).
  • A* admissibility & consistency: h(n)≤h∗(n); h(n)≤c(n,a,n′)+h(n′) ⇒ A* graph-search is optimal (ch. 3.5.2).
  • Minimax + alpha-beta: bm/2 effective branching with optimal ordering (ch. 6.3).
  • Soundness / completeness: KB⊢i​α⇒KB⊨α; converse for resolution on FOL (ch. 7-9).
  • Bayes’ rule: P(H∣E)=P(E∣H)P(H)/P(E) (ch. 12.5).
  • Bayes-net factorization: P(x1​,…,xn​)=∏i​P(xi​∣parents(Xi​)) (ch. 13.2).
  • MEU + vNM theorem: any rational agent behaves as if maximizing some E[U] (ch. 15.2).
  • Bellman equation: U(s)=maxa​∑s′​P(s′∣s,a)[R+γU(s′)] (ch. 16.2).
  • Bellman contraction: ∥BU−BU′∥∞​≤γ∥U−U′∥∞​ (ch. 16.2.1).
  • Q-learning convergence: under standard Robbins-Monro + visit-each-pair conditions (ch. 23.3).
  • Policy-gradient theorem: ∇θ​J(θ)=E[∇θ​logπθ​(a∣s)Qπ(s,a)] (ch. 23.5).
  • Transformer attention: Attn(Q,K,V)=softmax(QK⊤/dk​​)V (ch. 25.4).
  • Off-switch / assistance-game theorem: an assistance-game agent uncertain over θ has positive VPI from human input, hence does not disable its off-switch (ch. 17.2.5).

Key concepts

  • Rational Agent (Russell-Norvig Definition) — the textbook’s organizing primitive; the formal commitment to “acting rationally” over the other three approaches.
  • Bayesian Network (Formal) — DAG + local CPTs whose product equals the joint distribution; conditional independence via d-separation.
  • Markov Decision Process (Formal) — the ⟨S,A,P,R⟩ tuple plus discounted utility; the formal substrate for RL and alignment.
  • Bellman Equation — the fixed-point equation for optimal utility; the contraction-mapping proof of value-iteration convergence.
  • Search Algorithm Taxonomy — BFS / DFS / IDS / UCS / Greedy / A* / IDA* unified by the evaluation function f(n).
  • Value Alignment Problem — Russell’s reformulation: standard model is unsafe, machines should be uncertain about human preferences (referenced in Nick Bostrom - Deep Utopia and Sebastian Mallaby - The Infinity Machine).

Best chapters/pages to actually read

Adjust by background. AIMA is modular by design: each part can be read after chs. 1-2 and a few of its specific prerequisites. For a policy reader with calculus + light linear algebra:

  • Chs. 1-2 (Introduction + Intelligent Agents) — mandatory. The rational-agent paradigm + PEAS + environment axes + standard-model critique (ch. 1.1.5) frame every subsequent chapter. Without this, the rest reads as a disconnected algorithm zoo.
  • Ch. 3 (Search) — mandatory. The search-problem 6-tuple and A* admissibility/consistency proof are the foundational formal result of the book. If you can’t write the Bellman equation later, you probably skipped this.
  • Ch. 5 (CSPs) — short and high-leverage; the first factored representation, with arc-consistency and backtracking heuristics that recur in SAT, planning, and probabilistic inference.
  • Ch. 6 (Adversarial Search) — read for minimax + alpha-beta + MCTS. UCB1’s exploit-explore tradeoff is the conceptual seed of bandit RL.
  • Chs. 7-8 (Logic) — read sections 7.1-7.5 + 8.1-8.3 for vocabulary; skim resolution/DPLL implementations unless you build SAT solvers. Soundness/completeness duality is the take-away.
  • Ch. 11 (Planning) — PDDL action schemas + the relaxed-plan heuristic + GraphPlan are conceptually clean; STRIPS/PDDL is the lingua franca for talking about goal-directed AI.
  • Ch. 12 (Probability) — mandatory. Bayes’ rule + conditional independence + the wumpus-world worked example. Anyone who claims AI literacy without ch. 12 is bluffing.
  • Ch. 13 (Bayesian Networks) — mandatory through 13.2 (semantics + d-separation). 13.3-13.4 (exact and approximate inference) only if you build inference engines. 13.5 (causal networks) is the single highest-leverage page-for-page section in the book for empirical-research-policy readers.
  • Ch. 14 (Temporal Models) — read 14.1-14.2 (filtering equation) for vocabulary; 14.3 (HMMs) and 14.4 (Kalman) only if you work in speech, biology, or controls.
  • Ch. 15 (Simple Decisions) — vNM theorem (ch. 15.2) + value of information (15.6). Both are quoted in policy contexts more than any other section of the book.
  • Ch. 16 (Complex Decisions) — mandatory. The MDP definition, Bellman equation, value-iteration contraction proof, and policy-iteration algorithm together are prerequisite for ~80% of the post-2010 AI literature. Read every page.
  • Ch. 17 (Multiagent) — read 17.2.5 (assistance games) explicitly, even if you skip game theory generally. It is the formal seed of Russell’s alignment programme.
  • Chs. 19, 21, 22 (Learning) — these can be substituted by Simon Prince - Understanding Deep Learning for a reader prioritizing modern depth on neural nets; AIMA’s coverage is broader but shallower per topic. Read AIMA ch. 19 sections 19.1, 19.5 (PAC + VC), 19.6 (linear/logistic) for foundations Prince does not cover, then defer to Prince for chs. 22 and 25.
  • Ch. 23 (Reinforcement Learning) — mandatory. TD-learning, Q-learning, DQN, policy gradient, inverse RL. This is the algorithmic spine of AlphaGo, RLHF, and most current AI-safety literature.
  • Chs. 24, 26, 27 — pick by domain: NLP (25 over 24), robotics, computer vision. Each is a gateway to its own literature; AIMA is not deep enough to be a primary source in any of these subfields.
  • Ch. 25 (Deep NLP) — read 25.1 (embeddings) + 25.4 (transformers) once carefully. Trace the attention computation on a 3-token toy by hand. State-of-the-art section is mid-2020 and dated; supplement with current papers.
  • Ch. 28 (Philosophy, Ethics, Safety) — mandatory if you are in policy. The three-principle reformulation (28.3) is the textbook’s bridge to Human Compatible; this chapter is the most-cited section of AIMA outside the technical core.
  • Ch. 29 (Future of AI) — short and worth the half-hour. The architectural prescription (29.2) is Russell’s bet on what general AI must look like to be safely deployable.

Critiques and limitations

The book has real limitations any reader importing its framings into research or policy should name explicitly.

  1. Breadth at the cost of depth on every individual topic. AIMA covers everything from A* to transformers in 28 chapters; for any specific subfield (deep learning, RL, NLP, vision, robotics) there is a deeper modern textbook (Simon Prince - Understanding Deep Learning for DL; Sutton-Barto for RL; Manning et al. for NLP). Use AIMA for breadth and inter-topic linkage; use specialized texts for depth.
  2. Field drift even in the 4th edition. Published 2021, the deep-learning chapters predate the post-ChatGPT / RLHF / scaling-law literature. Pretraining recipes, the RLHF pipeline (Ouyang et al. 2022, Bai et al. 2022), and the constitutional-AI / RLAIF programme are absent or marginal. The state-of-the-art sections of chs. 22, 25 are usefully historical but not current.
  3. The standard-model critique is uneven across chapters. Russell’s value-alignment programme is presented in chs. 1.1.5, 17.2.5, and 28.3, but most of the technical chapters (search, MDPs, RL) still operate in standard-model mode without flagging the safety implications. A reader who reads chs. 16 + 23 alone could finish thinking RL is solved without realizing Russell views fixed-objective optimization as the unsafe limiting case.
  4. No serious treatment of compute supply / economics. The book treats algorithms as primary and compute as a footnote, which is increasingly out of step with the empirical reality that capability scales with compute (cross-ref Chris Miller - Chip War and the Bitter-Lesson literature in Sebastian Mallaby - The Infinity Machine). A reader who learns AI from AIMA alone will under-weight the political economy of capability.
  5. Limited engagement with neuroscience / cognitive science. Russell explicitly bracketed cognitive science as out of scope (ch. 1.1.2), but the resulting silence on biological intelligence makes the textbook complementary to, rather than a substitute for, Max Bennett - A Brief History of Intelligence and Daniel Johnston - Foundations of Cellular Neurophysiology on the substrate side.
  6. Mathematical rigor is uneven. Some chapters (16, 17, 19) are formally tight; others (22, 25, 27) sketch results without proof. The exercises sets help but cannot fully compensate. For the formal-theory reader, AIMA is a survey, not a treatise.
  7. The alignment programme is Russell-flavored and not neutral. Chs. 1.1.5 / 17.2.5 / 28.3 reflect Russell’s specific position (assistance games, IRL, three principles) and treat alternative framings (mesa-optimization / inner alignment, scalable oversight, constitutional AI, mechanistic interpretability) only briefly. Pair with Christian’s The Alignment Problem and contemporary alignment literature for plurality.

Important examples / cases

The textbook leans on a small set of recurring worked examples that the reader should reproduce by hand to anchor the abstractions. The Romania route-finding map (ch. 3, Fig. 3.1 — Arad → Bucharest with straight-line distance heuristic) is the canonical A* example; tracing the search frontier through Sibiu, Fagaras, Pitesti, Rimnicu-Vilcea makes admissibility/consistency tangible. The vacuum world (ch. 2, ch. 3) appears across problem types — atomic, factored, partially observable — and is the textbook’s stress-test for environment axes. The wumpus world (chs. 7, 12) is the bridge from logical to probabilistic reasoning — the same domain encoded twice, once with propositional logic and once with conditional probability tables, demonstrating which axiomatic style fits which kind of uncertainty.

The 8-puzzle / 15-puzzle (ch. 3.6) is used to compare heuristics — misplaced-tiles vs Manhattan-distance vs disjoint-pattern-databases — making the dominance ordering on heuristics (h2​ dominates h1​ iff h2​(n)≥h1​(n) for all n) operational. The alarm / burglary / earthquake / John / Mary network (ch. 13.1, Fig. 13.2) is the canonical 5-node Bayes net; reproducing the joint P(B,E,A,J,M) and computing P(B∣J,M) by enumeration grounds the factorization formula.

The 4×3 grid world (ch. 16, Fig. 16.1) is the textbook’s MDP — stochastic moves with 0.8/0.1/0.1 transition probabilities, terminal states +1 and −1, transition reward −0.04. Tracing value iteration to convergence on this 11-state MDP is the single most-recommended hand exercise in the book; Russell shows (Fig. 16.7) how the policy becomes optimal at iteration 5 even though the utility values keep changing through iteration ~30. The Tetris MDP (ch. 16, Fig. 16.5, ∼1062 states) is the contrasting “real” MDP showing why factored / approximate methods are necessary at scale.

For learning, the restaurant-decision-tree example (ch. 19) carries the chapter’s information-gain machinery; the MNIST / ImageNet sequence (ch. 22) carries the deep-learning architectural progression; AlphaGo / AlphaZero (chs. 6, 23.7) is the worked-through case for MCTS + RL + deep nets in combination, and is the textbook’s single most-cited modern AI achievement. Atari DQN (Mnih et al. 2013/2015, ch. 23.4) is the worked-through case for deep RL and the conceptual proof that one architecture can master 49 games from raw pixels with no hand-coded features.

Links to other books

  • Modern complement to Simon Prince - Understanding Deep Learning — Prince does deep learning to ~500 pages of depth; AIMA does deep learning in 60 pages but covers everything else (search, logic, probability, decision theory, RL, robotics, vision, alignment) Prince omits. Read together: AIMA for breadth + cross-topic routing, Prince for the architectural details of chs. 22, 25.
  • Substrate-detail counterpart in Daniel Johnston - Foundations of Cellular Neurophysiology — Johnston explains real ion-channel dynamics; AIMA explains the abstract artificial-agent computations that ignore them. Both books on the same shelf clarifies which biological details modern AI has deliberately abstracted away.
  • Conceptual-history complement in Max Bennett - A Brief History of Intelligence — Bennett organizes intelligence as five evolutionary breakthroughs; AIMA organizes intelligence as algorithmic stack. Bennett’s “mentalizing” / “theory-of-mind” maps loosely onto AIMA’s multiagent + assistance-games chapters (17, 28).
  • Industrial-history alignment counterpart in Sebastian Mallaby - The Infinity Machine — Mallaby is the institutional history of DeepMind, OpenAI, Anthropic; AIMA is the technical content their researchers operate on. Reading both lets you locate Sutskever’s transformer-scaling bet, Hassabis’s pivot doctrine, and Amodei’s marginal-returns-to-intelligence in the formal vocabulary of MDP / Bellman / scaling laws.
  • Alignment-philosophy companion in Nick Bostrom - Deep Utopia — Bostrom asks what happens after alignment is solved; AIMA ch. 28 is the technical introduction to why solving it is hard. Russell’s three principles are the operational version of Bostrom’s “solved world” precondition.
  • Foundational-formalism predecessors in Marvin Lee Minsky - Computation Finite and Infinite Machines and Marvin Minsky and Seymour Papert - Perceptrons — Minsky’s Computation covers FSMs / Turing machines / universality (the substrate); Perceptrons covers single-layer neural-net limits. AIMA inherits both: the formal-language-theory content of Part III and the deep-net resolution of perceptron limits in Part V.
  • Mind-philosophy framing in Daniel C. Dennett - Brainstorms and Daniel C. Dennett - The Mind’s I — Dennett’s intentional-stance vocabulary helps interpret the “rational agent” abstraction without reifying agency where there is only optimization. Russell’s standard-model critique (ch. 1.1.5) reads as a Dennett-flavored argument that the intentional stance toward AI must be revised when the optimizer becomes powerful.
  • Decentralized-cognition counterpart in Marvin Minsky - The Society of Mind (forthcoming) and Marvin Minsky - The Emotion Machine — Minsky’s society-of-agents view is a competing architectural intuition to AIMA’s monolithic-rational-agent + learning-element model.
  • Embodiments-of-mind ancestor in Warren S. McCulloch - Embodiments of Mind — McCulloch’s 1943 neural-net formalism is the historical seed of AIMA’s chs. 22 and 25.

Open questions

How should an AI textbook in the LLM / agentic-AI era organize its material when foundation models are simultaneously a technique (in chs. 22 / 25) and an agent paradigm potentially replacing the rational-agent frame (chs. 1-2)? Russell’s three principles assume the agent has a coherent uncertainty-over-objectives representation; do current LLMs even support that abstraction, or does scaling-up RLHF produce something that is shaped by, but not literally implementing, the assistance-game framework? Should ch. 28’s value-alignment material come first in a 5th edition — given that policy-relevant readers form their intuitions about agent design while reading the technical chapters and may set those intuitions before encountering the alignment vocabulary? And: is the rational-agent paradigm itself the right central organizing concept for a field that is increasingly shaped by emergent capabilities of pretrained foundation models that nobody designed as rational agents?

Research uses

  • AI-policy and AI-tools technical reference. AIMA is the textbook to cite when you need to ground a policy claim in non-handwavy technical vocabulary — Bellman equation, conditional independence, policy gradient, transformer attention, value alignment. The page-for-page highest leverage for policy: chs. 1.1.5, 13.5 (causal nets), 16.2 (Bellman + value iteration), 17.2.5 (assistance games), 23.6 (inverse RL), 28.3 (three principles).
  • Course sequencing. If teaching policy or strategy audiences, build a 12-week mini-course around chs. 1-3, 12-13, 15-17, 19, 22 (sketch only), 23, 28. Skip the logic chapters (7-11) for non-technical audiences unless a specific application motivates them. For technical audiences pair AIMA with Simon Prince - Understanding Deep Learning for chs. 22-25 depth.
  • Capability-claim audits. When a memo or vendor claim asserts an AI system “reasons” / “plans” / “has values”, map the claim onto AIMA’s formal vocabulary: Is it doing search (ch. 3-4)? Probabilistic inference (ch. 13)? MDP solving (ch. 16)? RL (ch. 23)? Or is it next-token prediction in a transformer (ch. 25.4) interpreted as if it were one of the above? The textbook gives you the disambiguation tools to challenge every category-error in the discourse.
  • Alignment vocabulary in writing. When discussing AI safety in publications, use the assistance-game / IRL / off-switch-theorem framing from chs. 17.2.5 + 23.6 + 28.3 rather than informal “the AI might want to take over” framings. Russell’s formulation is the strongest mainstream-academic articulation and gives technical interlocutors a shared vocabulary.

What I would miss if I only read this note

Every algorithm pseudocode, every figure, every numerical worked example, the exercises that anchor abstract definitions in concrete computation, and the textbook’s full bibliographic notes that connect each chapter to the primary literature. You would miss the actual calculations on the 4×3 grid world (ch. 16, Fig. 16.7) that make the value-iteration contraction proof tangible — and the moment of seeing the policy become optimal at iteration 5 while the utility numbers keep moving until iteration ~30. You would miss the Romania-map worked example for A* and the wumpus-world dual encoding (logic in ch. 7, probability in ch. 12) that shows why the field shifted. You would miss Russell’s prose voice in the standard-model-critique sections (chs. 1.1.5, 28.3), which carries an argumentative weight that survey-style summary cannot reproduce; the chess-via-blackmail thought experiment (ch. 1) is the textbook’s most rhetorically effective single passage. You would miss the bibliographic notes that connect each chapter to the primary literature — particularly the historical notes for chs. 13 (Pearl), 16 (Bellman + Howard), and 23 (Sutton + Barto), which contextualize the formalisms as choices rather than inevitabilities. Finally, you would miss the experience of doing the exercises, which is where understanding actually consolidates — narrating the equations is not knowing them.

Retrieval pledge

Before claiming mastery of a chapter, reproduce its central equation(s) from a blank page and compute one worked example by hand. Specifically: A* on the Romania map (ch. 3); the joint P(B,E,A,J,M) and posterior P(B∣J,M) on the alarm Bayes net (ch. 13); value iteration on the 4×3 grid world to convergence (ch. 16); the Q-learning update on a 3-state MDP with 2 actions (ch. 23). Be able to write the Bellman equation, the Bayes-net factorization formula, and the transformer attention equation from a blank page — these three are the modern lingua franca, and not knowing any of them creates measurable analytic gaps. Read chs. 1.1.5, 17.2.5, and 28.3 together as a single argumentative arc — this is the most policy-portable through-line in the book and the one most likely to be cited in any serious AI-safety conversation.
