Wendy Smith and Marianne Lewis - Both-And Thinking

Read chapter-by-chapter → Deep dive of Both/And Thinking: Embracing Creative Tensions to Solve Your Toughest Problems (11 chapters)

One-sentence thesis

Most chronic personal, organizational, and societal problems are not dilemmas (binary choices among alternatives) but paradoxes (contradictory yet interdependent forces that persist over time), and the operational alternative to the or is a four-part interdependent tool kit — and thinking supported by Assumptions, Boundaries, Comfort, and Dynamism (ABCD) — that produces creative integrations (mules) and dynamic micro-shifts (tightrope walking) instead of pendulum swings.

Why this book matters

Smith and Lewis’s contribution is to convert “both/and” from a slogan into an operational system. The slogan has been around for decades — Mary Parker Follett in the 1920s, Charles Hampden-Turner in the 1980s, the Lao Tzu / Heraclitus convergence 2,500 years ago — and recent years have seen it claimed by everyone from Barclays to Yale to Barack Obama. The authors’ worry, which the book explicitly addresses, is that the slogan stays a slogan unless it is paired with structures, emotional practice, and adaptive routines that hold paradoxes in productive form under stress.

Most adjacent books in the decision-making and leadership shelf either treat tensions as dilemmas to be optimized (Dennett’s intuition pumps as dilemma-resolution heuristics; the negotiation literature on win-win) or as threats to be eliminated through sharper choice (the strategy literature on commitment, focus, positioning). Smith and Lewis make the harder claim: most consequential problems cannot be optimized once-and-for-all, because the underlying tension is interdependent and persistent. Their empirical evidence is concrete — a three-country survey of >3,000 workers (US, China, Israel) shows that employees in the engaging zone (high felt tension + paradox mindset) outperform on innovation, productivity, and job satisfaction as rated by managers. The Paradox Mindset Inventory is in the appendix and online.

The book sits in productive dialogue with Julia Galef - The Scout Mindset (Galef’s accuracy-motivation parallel; identity-held-lightly parallel), Andrew S. Grove - High Output Management and Andrew S. Grove - Only the Paranoid Survive (Grove’s operating discipline as paradox-management at managerial and strategic scale), Hamilton Helmer - 7 Powers (cornered-resource vs counter-positioning as a paradox), Frederick P. Brooks Jr. - The Mythical Man-Month (conceptual integrity vs participation), Reid Hoffman and Chris Yeh - Blitzscaling (counterintuitive scaling rules as both/and), and Philip E. Tetlock and Dan Gardner - Superforecasting (foxes are both/and thinkers). For practitioners — leaders, parents, mid-career professionals, anyone in a chronic dilemma — the worksheet in chapter 8 plus the SMALL model in chapter 9 give the book unusual operational reach.

Core argument

  1. Three-tier vocabulary: tensions, dilemmas, paradoxes. Tensions are the umbrella term for situations of opposing demands. Dilemmas are how tensions present at the surface — choices among alternatives. Paradoxes are the underlying structure: contradictory yet interdependent elements that exist simultaneously and persist over time. Most popular discourse operates at the dilemma layer, weighing pros and cons; Smith and Lewis’s diagnostic move is to push every chronic dilemma down to its underlying paradox before any decision is attempted.

  2. Three environmental drivers make paradoxes more salient now: change, scarcity, plurality. Faster change (the future arrives faster) intensifies today/tomorrow tensions. Scarcer resources (climate constraints, attention, time) intensify self/other and competition/cooperation tensions. More plurality (more voices, more identities, more stakeholders) intensifies global/local and unity/distinctiveness tensions. The convergence is “the perfect paradox storm.”

  3. Either/or thinking is a real failure mode with three archetypes. Sometimes either/or is the right response — for low-stakes (where to eat dinner) or terminal (ending a bad relationship) decisions. But on persistent, interdependent contradictions, either/or produces three reinforcing failure patterns: the rabbit hole (intensification — cognitive, emotional, behavioral traps lock one pole in until strength becomes liability; LEGO 1990s discipline taken to rigidity); the wrecking ball (overcorrection — radical swing creates a new and bigger rut; LEGO 2000s “creativity above all else” producing 157 colors and near-bankruptcy); and trench warfare (polarization — opposing groups dig in and reinforce each other’s stuckness; corporate-governance debates, Brexit, US political polarization). The S-curve dynamic (Charles Handy) and Icarus paradox (Danny Miller) are the formal models behind rabbit-hole intensification.

  4. Paradoxes come in four (overlapping) types. Performing paradoxes (why-questions: goals, outcomes, expectations — mission/market). Learning paradoxes (when-questions: time-based — exploit/explore). Belonging paradoxes (who-questions: identity, roles — past-self/future-self). Organizing paradoxes (how-questions: structure — centralization/decentralization). The taxonomy is heuristic, not partitioning — real cases are knotted (multiple types interreinforce in one situation) and nested (the same paradox recurs across individual, group, organizational, and societal levels).

  5. Both/and thinking produces two virtuous-cycle patterns. Mules (creative integrations) — Rothenberg’s Janusian thinking; Mary Parker Follett’s library window opened in the adjacent room; Roger Martin’s opposable mind. Crucially, mules are infertile: a creative integration solves a specific dilemma but does not dissolve the underlying paradox, which returns in new presenting forms. Tightrope walking (consistent inconsistency) — Philippe Petit between the Twin Towers; sailing; biking; Schneider’s expansion/constriction balance; small frequent shifts that accommodate both poles over time. Successful navigators use both patterns; Wendy’s IBM dissertation found that the most successful business-unit leaders mostly tightrope-walked with occasional mules.

  6. The Paradox System (ABCD) is four interdependent tool sets, not a sequence. Assumptions — shift from one truth to multiple truths (improv “Yes, and”; the blind people and the elephant), from scarcity to abundance (Bazerman’s mythical fixed pie; ecobricks; Adam Grant’s give-and-take), from controlling to coping (Heifetz, Grashow, and Linsky’s adaptive challenges; the dance-floor/balcony move). Boundaries — higher purpose (Frankl, Sinek, Sherif’s Robbers Cave superordinate goals), separating-and-connecting (Lloyd’s underwriters’ Mr. Rogers wardrobe change; Tushman and O’Reilly’s structural ambidexterity; Birkinshaw and Gibson’s contextual ambidexterity), and guardrails (Petriglieri’s couple contract; Dr. Seuss’s 50-word constraint that produced Green Eggs and Ham). Comfort — building in a pause (autonomic rehabilitation; “Keep Calm and Carry On”), accepting discomfort (Tara Brach’s radical acceptance; Wegner’s white-bear / ironic processing; the Buddhist two-arrows), broadening perspective (Fredrickson’s broaden-and-build; Naomi Rothman’s emotional ambivalence as the most paradox-aligned emotional posture). Dynamism — measured experimentation (IDEO’s rapid prototyping; Toyota’s Prius iterations; NCFDD’s grad-student experiment), preparing for serendipity (planned luck; Pasteur; the Honda effect; Marianne’s Cass deanship via her London-Business-School grilling), and learning to unlearn (Argyris’s double-loop; Adam Grant’s Think Again; Gore’s “breathing in, breathing out” metaphor for global/local).

  7. The system is itself paradoxical. The horizontal axis is heart vs mind (assumptions ↔ comfort); the vertical axis is stability vs change (boundaries ↔ dynamism). Cameron and Quinn’s slogan — “navigating paradoxes is paradoxical” — is the book’s most-repeated mantra. This is not just a clever framing: it’s a structural commitment that prevents the system from collapsing into either-pure-cognition or pure-emotion or pure-structure or pure-action.

  8. Two structural failure modes for organizations attempting both/and. False dichotomy — separating poles into different units without connection; innovation subunits that never link back to the core business decline into political conflict (some IBM business units that incubated innovation in isolation). False synergy — connecting poles through aspirational mission statements without structural protection for the weaker pole; the existing world’s inertia (sales quotas, R&D demands) crushes innovation, which becomes “off the side of the desk” work (the IBM business unit Smith calls out for posters and wallet cards but no structural separation).

  9. The five-step decision worksheet (chapter 8) operationalizes ABCD for individuals. Define the dilemma; surface the underlying paradox; reframe to a both/and question; analyze by separating (pros/cons of each pole) AND connecting (move up a level to overarching vision; move down a level to reinforcing synergies); choose between mule options (creative integration) and tightrope-walker options (consistent inconsistency). The Ella Franke case (a composite of recurring career-dilemma advisees) carries the chapter — she takes the new hospital head-of-development role but negotiates a one-day-per-week consulting transition with her old team for four weeks and brings a second rising star with her.

  10. The SMALL model (chapter 9) operationalizes ABCD for intergroup conflict. From Polarity Partnerships (Barry Johnson and Margaret Seidler’s work). Seeing (label poles in neutral or positive language); Mapping (greater purpose statement + deeper fear; upsides and downsides of each pole); Assessing (where on the polarity map does current reality sit?); Learning (which downsides dominate?); Leveraging (action plan that amplifies each pole’s upsides + early-warning indicators for downsides). The Charleston Police Department case under Chief Greg Mullen, working with Seidler before and after the 2015 Mother Emanuel AME Church shooting, is the chapter’s payoff — the Illumination Project ran 33 listening sessions in 2018 and generated 2,226 ideas.

  11. Eight leadership tasks (chapter 10) operationalize ABCD for organizations. Build context: link organizational tensions to higher purpose; build guardrails; diversify stakeholders; encourage experimentation. Invite people in: surface paradoxes; honor discomfort; build conflict-management skills; personalize paradoxes for employees. The Paul Polman / Unilever case (Sustainable Living Plan, 2009-2019, 300% shareholder return while halving environmental footprint) carries the chapter, with parallel applications from Netflix (Hastings’s culture deck), DDD (Hockenstein’s social enterprise), Shorefast (Cobb’s “global network of intensely local places”), and Gore (Kelly’s breathing metaphor).

  12. The book’s central honest claim is that paradoxes don’t go away. The repeated mantra throughout — “navigating paradoxes is paradoxical” — is also a reminder that the work is permanent. There is no final integration after which the tension dissolves. Mules are infertile. Tightrope walkers are always re-balancing. The leaders the book celebrates (Cobb, Polman, Mullen, Kelly, Perna, Hockenstein, Rockquemore) all keep the work going; none reach a stable end-state.

Argument map

Numbered prose outline:

  1. The diagnostic vocabulary — three tiers (tensions, dilemmas, paradoxes); three environmental drivers (change, scarcity, plurality); four paradox types (performing, learning, belonging, organizing); knotted and nested.
  2. The failure mode — vicious cycle of either/or, with three archetypes (rabbit hole = intensification, wrecking ball = overcorrection, trench warfare = polarization), each grounded in a formal model (S-curve / Icarus paradox / Pygmalion / escalating commitment / polarity map).
  3. The response — both/and thinking, with two virtuous-cycle patterns (mules = creative integration; tightrope walkers = consistent inconsistency), supported by the four-part Paradox System (ABCD), which is itself paradoxical.
  4. The four ABCD tools, one chapter each — Assumptions (mindset shifts in knowledge, resources, problem-solving); Boundaries (higher purpose, separate+connect, guardrails); Comfort (pause, accept, broaden); Dynamism (experiment, serendipity, unlearn).
  5. The two structural failure modes for organizational both/and — false dichotomy (separate without connect) and false synergy (connect without separate).
  6. The applications — five-step individual decision worksheet (ch. 8); SMALL model for intergroup conflict (ch. 9); eight leadership tasks for organizations (ch. 10).
  7. The central honest claim — paradoxes don’t dissolve. Navigation is permanent. The leaders the book celebrates are all still doing the work.

Key concepts

  • Both-And Thinking — the cognitive and behavioral disposition; the alternative to either/or thinking
  • Paradox System — the four-part ABCD tool kit
  • Vicious Cycle of Either-Or — the failure mode the system is designed to interrupt; three archetypes
  • Antifragility — adjacent design target from Taleb; antifragile systems, like both/and-navigated ones, gain from stress
  • Scout Mindset — Galef’s sister construct; accuracy-motivated reasoning is the cognitive parallel to paradox-mindset’s structural integration

Best chapters/pages to actually read

Read chapters 2 and 3 first — they are the analytical core. Chapter 2 is the diagnosis (the LEGO case as the canonical rabbit-hole-and-wrecking-ball story; the three-archetype taxonomy of vicious cycles; the S-curve and polarity-map formalisms). Chapter 3 is the prescription (mules and tightrope walkers as virtuous-cycle archetypes; the ABCD system introduced as itself paradoxical). Together they give the conceptual spine; the rest of the book elaborates.

Then read chapter 4 for the empirical anchor (Paradox Mindset Inventory, the four-zones 2x2, the three-country study, the “Yes, and” improv practice). The screen-time fight with Wendy’s husband is one of the book’s best personal examples of how either/or even captures couples who teach paradox theory for a living.

For the operational worksheet, read chapter 8 (Ella Franke’s career-decision case carries the five-step process). For intergroup work, read chapter 9 (the SMALL model and Charleston’s Illumination Project). For organizational leadership, read chapter 10 (Polman’s Unilever turnaround and the eight-task framework).

The chapters on Boundaries (5), Comfort (6), and Dynamism (7) are good but more case-heavy and less analytically dense than chapters 2, 3, 4 — read them after the spine, or read selectively for the cases (DDD, Charleston, Gore, IDEO/Honda).

Important examples / cases

  • Zita Cobb on Fogo Island, Newfoundland (chs. 1, 10) — eighth-generation islander returns after the cod collapse, founds Shorefast, builds the Fogo Island Inn around local woodworkers, quilters, foragers; “find new ways with old things”; the cauliflower as company symbol (florets as local communities, stem as global connection).
  • LEGO (ch. 2) — discipline rabbit-hole in the 1990s (a decade to add the color green) → innovation wrecking ball in the 2000s (157 colors, near bankruptcy) → bifocal recovery under Knudstorp; the eleven paradoxes posted on LEGO walls since the 1980s.
  • Jeremy Hockenstein and Digital Divide Data (chs. 4, 5, 10) — 2000 Hong Kong inflection; founds Cambodian social enterprise that’s nonprofit in the US and for-profit in Cambodia; “Neither. Both. Does it really matter?”; the early advisory-board “you’ll be bankrupt in three months” wakeup call; the thatched-hut-dream-vs-Battambang experiment.
  • Janet Perna at IBM Data Management (ch. 5) — Lou Gerstner’s three-horizon mandate; structural ambidexterity through California (mainframe) + Toronto (Java/Unix) split with senior-leadership integration; “There’s no grandstanding on this team.”
  • Wendy at Yale, first night (ch. 6) — the belonging paradox between her Florida-public-school past and Yale future; resolved through a knock on the door, suite-mates eating pizza at Yorkside.
  • Marianne’s career swings (chs. 1, 2, 4, 5, 7) — research → administration → integration; her own rabbit-hole (research-only) and wrecking-ball (administration-only) failure modes.
  • Greg Mullen and the Charleston Police Department / Mother Emanuel AME Church (ch. 9) — pre-existing polarity work with Margaret Seidler before the 2015 shooting; the SMALL model in action; the Illumination Project’s 33 sessions and 2,226 ideas.
  • Paul Polman at Unilever (ch. 10) — 2009-2019 turnaround through the Sustainable Living Plan; ended quarterly guidance; hired Jeff Seabright as initially-separate-then-embedded chief sustainability officer; replaced 70 of the top 100 leaders to install the paradox-mindset.
  • Honda’s US motorcycle market entry, 1960 (ch. 7) — the Boston Consulting Group’s clean strategic-brilliance story versus Pascale’s 1982 reinterview revealing dumb luck, oil leaks, and the Sears executive who spotted the lightweight bikes the Honda team was using for errands; the “Honda effect” as the design-vs-emergent strategy debate.
  • Mary Parker Follett’s library window (ch. 3) — open in the adjacent room rather than half-open the original window; the canonical mule.
  • Ella Franke’s career decision (ch. 8) — the worked example through the five-step worksheet; takes the new role + consulting transition + brings second rising star.

Critiques and limitations

The book is an exemplar of consultant-style frameworks: a memorable mnemonic (ABCD), a 2x2 (paradox mindset zones), a worksheet (chapter 8), a five-step model (SMALL), an eight-task leadership table — repeated case-by-case across the chapters with the recurring incantation that “navigating paradoxes is paradoxical.” The framework has the genuine analytical power of recognizing the three vicious-cycle archetypes and the empirical backing of the Paradox Mindset Inventory’s three-country study, but the chapters’ rhythm — case study → framework → personal vignette → quote from Lao Tzu or Heraclitus or Brené Brown — can feel formulaic by chapter 7. The book is most analytically alive in chapters 2-4 and most operationally useful in chapters 8-9; chapters 5-7 and 10 elaborate with case after case in a way that begins to dilute the spine.

A second criticism: case selection is winner-only. Cobb, Polman, Hockenstein, Mullen, Kelly, Rockquemore, Perna are all success stories selected because they applied something resembling both/and. We do not see the leaders who attempted similar moves and failed because of timing, market structure, regulatory shifts, or factors orthogonal to mindset. The implicit causal claim — that both/and thinking produces the success — is therefore weaker than the rhetoric suggests. The 3,000-person Paradox Mindset survey is real evidence, but cross-sectional and self-report-supplemented; it shows correlation, not causation, between paradox mindset and outcomes.

A third criticism: the boundary between genuinely-paradoxical-and-irreducible problems and merely-difficult-tradeoff problems is vague. The authors acknowledge that either/or thinking is sometimes correct (low-stakes or terminal decisions), but the diagnostic for which type a given problem is remains intuitive (“does the tension persist? does choosing one side eliminate the other’s pull?”). In practice, ambitious leaders may use both/and language to avoid making genuinely necessary tradeoffs — “we can do both X and Y” can be motivated reasoning dressed as paradox theory. Hochschild’s false synergy warning is right but underused.

A fourth criticism: the Charleston Illumination Project case is presented as a triumph of the SMALL model but the larger context — that US police-community relations have if anything deteriorated since 2015, with Floyd, Taylor, and others — sits awkwardly with the chapter’s framing. The authors acknowledge this in passing but the case is asked to carry more weight than it can. The chapter’s claim that SMALL works for national-political polarization is not really supported; the cases shown are facilitated workshops with willing participants, which most polarized national debates are not.

Finally, the book’s own genre conventions — first-name corporate leaders, Brené Brown quotes, Ted-talk-ready metaphors, repeated “navigating paradox is paradoxical” mantra — code it as practitioner-leadership-bestseller rather than scholarship. Readers wanting the original 2011 AMR paper’s tighter argumentation will need to go to the academic source; readers wanting deeper engagement with the philosophical lineage (Heraclitus, Lao Tzu, Hegel, Kierkegaard, Jung) will find it gestured at but not engaged with at depth.

Links to other books

  • Julia Galef - The Scout Mindset — sister volume on identity-light reasoning; Galef’s accuracy-motivation parallels Smith and Lewis’s paradox-motivation; “ideological Turing test” is a both/and practice
  • Andrew S. Grove - High Output Management — Grove’s operating discipline (one-on-ones, staff meetings, peer reviews) is implicit both/and at managerial scale; balancing autonomy and oversight is the canonical paradox of management
  • Andrew S. Grove - Only the Paranoid Survive — strategic-inflection-points are paradox events; Grove’s “constructive confrontation” + “Cassandras” + “let chaos reign” parallel comfort + dynamism
  • Hamilton Helmer - 7 Powers — cornered resource vs counter-positioning is a strategic paradox; Helmer’s “counter-positioning” of incumbents trapped in their own success is the Icarus paradox in strategy form
  • Frederick P. Brooks Jr. - The Mythical Man-Month — conceptual integrity vs participation is a paradox; Brooks’s surgical-team and chief-programmer-team proposals are early structural-ambidexterity attempts
  • Reid Hoffman and Chris Yeh - Blitzscaling — the counterintuitive scaling rules (“embrace chaos,” “tolerate bad management”) are paradoxical responses to the speed/quality tension
  • Philip E. Tetlock and Dan Gardner - Superforecasting — Tetlock’s foxes (multi-perspective integrators) are both/and thinkers; hedgehogs are either/or thinkers; the practical superiority of foxes on real-world forecasting is empirical support for the paradox-mindset claim
  • Nassim Nicholas Taleb - Incerto — antifragility is a structural cousin of both/and; positive convexity to volatility is what the paradox system aims for at the organizational level
  • Daniel C. Dennett - I’ve Been Thinking — Dennett’s intuition pumps are tools for surfacing hidden assumptions; the “Yes, and” assumption-shift in chapter 4 is a similar move

Research uses

For personal decision-making, the chapter-8 worksheet is the most directly portable artifact. Apply it to any persistent dilemma (career moves, relationship configurations, work-life arrangements, health-vs-pleasure tradeoffs). The crucial diagnostic question is whether the dilemma keeps recurring in different forms — if yes, it’s a paradox, and either/or framing will produce vicious cycles regardless of which side you choose.

For AI policy and governance research, the framework is unusually apt. Acceleration vs precaution, openness vs control, capability vs safety, scale vs interpretability — these are paradoxes, not tradeoffs. Either/or framings produce the rabbit-hole and wrecking-ball failure modes the book describes. The ABCD diagnostic is useful: which of the four tool sets is missing in current governance proposals? Most proposals have boundaries (regulatory structures) but no comfort (no honest reporting infrastructure), no dynamism (no iteration or unlearning), and contested assumptions (no shared mental model of multiple truths).

For organizational design, use ABCD as an audit framework on real teams. Innovation-stuck teams often have assumptions and dynamism but missing boundaries. Politically-stuck teams often have boundaries without comfort. Burned-out teams often have all four but missing tightrope-walking practices. The structural-ambidexterity vs contextual-ambidexterity distinction (chapter 5) is especially useful for choosing intervention design.

For career-strategy work, the book pairs naturally with portfolio-bets thinking (Career as Portfolio of Bets) and antifragility (Antifragility). A career navigated as a portfolio of bets, with explicit guardrails (couple contract, geographic constraints, financial floors), built-in experiments (side-projects, sabbaticals), and willingness to unlearn (career pivots) is operationalized both/and at the personal scale.

Open questions

  • How sharp is the boundary between paradoxes (genuinely irreducible) and difficult tradeoffs (where one side really is correct)? The book gives an intuitive heuristic but no operational test.
  • Is the empirical claim that “paradox mindset → engagement zone → better performance” causal or selection-driven? People with high baseline cognitive flexibility may both adopt paradox mindset and perform better for orthogonal reasons.
  • Does the SMALL model scale beyond facilitated workshops with willing participants? National-scale political polarization, where one or both sides reject the framing, is the harder test.
  • How much of the LEGO recovery, the Polman turnaround, the DDD success is specifically attributable to both/and thinking versus other factors (capital structure, founder-CEO continuity, fortunate market timing)?
  • Are there domains where either/or thinking outperforms both/and even on chronic problems? Some commitment-device literature suggests yes — relationships, religious vows, certain strategic positions benefit from precluding the very re-evaluation that both/and requires.
  • The book is published in 2022 in the wake of the Covid pandemic. How does the framework hold up against the post-pandemic backlash to many “both/and” arrangements (hybrid work, ESG investing, multi-stakeholder capitalism)?

What I would miss if I only read this note

I would miss the texture of the personal vignettes that anchor the framework — Wendy’s twin spitting up on her sweater at the coffee shop while she planned to open a daycare; her screen-time fight with her husband; her first-night-at-Yale belonging paradox resolved through pizza with a Korean-American Chicagoan and a Chicana from LA. I would miss Marianne’s concrete career-arc descriptions — the doctoral years with three small children and her husband as full-time parent; the burnout that led to the first sabbatical; the Cass deanship she got because of how she handled a 90-minute London Business School grilling. I would miss the LEGO middle-managers via Lotte Lüscher, the Charleston police-officer who told the Post and Courier he was shocked to hear citizens feared sitting next to a uniformed officer, and the Russell Maier ecobricks story (Igorot people in the Philippines have no word for trash, only ayyew — the value of repurposing).

I would also miss the way the book demonstrates its own thesis through its structure. It alternates Wendy and Marianne’s voices (“I (Wendy),” “I (Marianne)”); it is co-authored without a clear divider; it celebrates personal vulnerability while modeling academic rigor; it is by two women writing about leadership without making gender the explicit topic. The book is partly an instance of what it argues — the both/and is enacted in the writing as well as theorized in the chapters.

I would miss the small operational details that make the worksheets useful: Wendy’s “draw a black X on the back of your hand” reminder to listen before speaking in meetings; Marianne’s habit of pausing to ask “what are you afraid of?” when she sees Wendy slip into either/or; the prompt to ask a friend to brainstorm both/and options for your paradox because emotional defenses block one’s own. These small habits are what convert the framework from a slogan into a practice.
