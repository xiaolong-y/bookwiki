Acharya and Lee - The Cartel System of States

Read chapter-by-chapter → Deep dive of The Cartel System of States — An Economic Theory of International Politics (7 chapters)

One-sentence thesis

The modern territorial state system is best understood as an economic cartel — a self-enforcing, geographically segmented oligopoly in which states (the suppliers) collude to not compete for the same citizens (the consumers) in the market for governance, with the consequence that mutual recognition rather than institutional efficiency defines what counts as a state, that border stability emerges from cartel-maintenance dynamics rather than international law as such, that wars are essentially price-war episodes after which cooperation rapidly resumes, and that the system has proven surprisingly robust to the three threats — interstate conflict, opportunistic entry by would-be states, and the rise of mass democracy — that ought naively to have unraveled it.

Why this book matters

A 2024 Oxford monograph that supplies what international-relations theory has lacked: a single self-enforcing mechanism that explains both the origin and the persistence of the territorial state system, and that does so by importing industrial-organisation tools (Bertrand competition, repeated-game cartel theory, entry deterrence, brand loyalty) into the classical IR vocabulary. It is more rigorous than Spruyt’s institutional-selection account, more parsimonious than the Westphalian-norms ideational accounts, and more politically grounded than realist offense-defense balance, because it locates the system’s stability in the specific political-economy interest of the rulers being governed against — namely, citizens.

For Yang’s research uses, the book sits at an exceptionally productive position: cartel theory is portable to any sovereignty-recognition question (Taiwan, Palestine, Somaliland, the digital-yuan/cryptocurrency boundary, the EU as quasi-state), to any tax-competition question (corporate inversion, OECD/G20 minimum-tax negotiations, BEPS), and to any state-recognition question in IR scholarship. The framing is also a clean complement to Angela Huyue Zhang - High Wire (which tells the inside-China regulatory dynamics story) and to Meg E. Rithmire - Land Bargains and Chinese Capitalism / Meg Rithmire - Precarious Ties (which tell the subnational-China story); Acharya-Lee supplies the international-system context within which Chinese state-building must operate. The timing is especially useful: published just before the post-2022 Ukraine and post-2023 AI/IP-rivalry periods raised exactly the kind of abstract-governance-market questions the framework is designed to handle (ch. 7).

The book also offers the unusual combination of formal-theoretic rigour (the spatial Bertrand model in ch. 2 is fully specified) and dense historical case work (Anglo-Scottish border 1500–1640; Spanish fueros; French intendent fiscal data; Atacama Desert; Pakistan-Afghan Durand Line). For Yang’s qualitative-IR work and for evaluating other people’s claims about state-system change, this is the right primary reference for the next several years.

Core argument

  1. The market-for-governance abstraction. States supply a bundle of services (protection, dispute resolution, infrastructure, education, R&D, regulation) and citizens pay taxes for them; both supply and demand exist, so a “market for governance” exists in the loose-but-precise sense relevant to industrial organisation (ch. 1; ch. 2). The market has the potential to be competitive — a citizen near the border between two providers can in principle play the rulers off each other, extracting concessions in the form of lower taxes or greater autonomy. The classic illustration is the British Raj’s North-West Frontier, where competition with the Afghan ruler depressed taxes to the point of subsidy of local khans (ch. 1).
  2. The spatial structure of cost and value. The cost of providing governance rises with distance from a ruler’s administrative core (Boulding’s “loss of strength gradient”); the value extractable per citizen is bounded by v — the point at which marginal taxation triggers revolt, exit, or tax avoidance (the “reservation price”). The geography of the model places ruler A and ruler B at endpoints of a line; their cost curves c_A, c_B rise with distance; profit per location is v − c(loc) where positive (ch. 2). Where two rulers’ positive-profit zones overlap, they are potential competitors — and the losses from such competition (L_A, L_B) are what they want to avoid.
  3. The cartel agreement. The cooperative solution is for the rulers to set a boundary Y (where c_A = c_B, the joint-profit-maximising point) and agree not to compete across it. Each becomes a local monopolist over their share of the market and extracts the full rent v from their core. This is self-enforcing in repeated interaction via trigger strategies (cheating today triggers competitive future, more costly than the gain from cheating). Sustainability requires the discount factor to exceed both L_A/(L_A+L_B) and L_B/(L_A+L_B) — the cartel works when symmetric losses-from-competition are at stake (ch. 2).
  4. The historical origin of the cartel in early-modern Europe. In the Middle Ages, low v and steep c curves left a large buffer zone between rulers’ practical reach. Universal claims (papacy, Holy Roman Empire, Austriae est imperare orbi universo) were costless because non-enforceable. The Commercial Revolution raised v; the Military Revolution + bureaucratic state-building lowered c. As cost curves flattened and value rose, the buffer zone shrank and the contested zone [X, Z] widened. This generated real losses-from-competition for the first time and motivated the sovereignty agreements that became the cartel — first bilaterally (the Anglo-Scottish 1552 demarcation; Treaty of the Pyrenees 1659; Westphalia 1648), then via the multilateral Congress of Vienna (1815) and the Berlin Conference (1884–85) (ch. 3).
  5. Global expansion through colonial conquest and coerced opening. By 1900 the cartel had spread worldwide, mostly via European colonisation (Berlin Conference partition of Africa = pure cartel exercise, “straight lines reflecting limited awareness of local geographical and political realities”), with the few non-colonised East Asian polities forced into the cartel via the Treaty of Nanking (1842), Tientsin (1858), Kanagawa (1853), Kanghwa (1876). The expansion is geographically near-complete (only 1.6% of land contested today, per ch. 1) but functionally uneven: the Pakistan-Afghanistan Durand Line is an active competitive failure of the cartel; many African borders are stable precisely because they cross unprofitable terrain that no state cares to govern (ch. 4).
  6. Conflict as cartel revelation, not cartel collapse. When relative governance costs shift asymmetrically (e.g., c_B drops while c_A is unchanged), the joint-profit-max boundary moves. War serves as a credible signalling mechanism — only a state whose costs have actually dropped will bear the cost of war over the disputed territory (ch. 5, Atacama Desert as canonical illustration). After the war the cartel reconstitutes quickly: Denmark accepts loss of Malmö; Spain accepts loss of Portugal; Mexico accepts loss of Texas/California; Germany accepts loss of Sudetenland and Silesia (ch. 5). Wars are price-war episodes within an equilibrium of long-term cooperation.
  7. Entry deterrence as the canonical cartel function. The same logic that makes economic cartels suppress new entrants makes the state cartel suppress secession movements. Recognition is collectively withheld even when the entrant is institutionally capable (Confederacy 1861–65; Biafra 1967–70; Republic of the Rif 1921–26; Somaliland today). Recognition is extended when great-power interests align (Belgium 1830 — Britain wanted a buffer; decolonisation 1945–90 — US/USSR lacked colonial empires and benefited from smaller successor states). The model’s coordination problem (ch. 5, Fig. 9) is exactly the function international institutions like the UN and OAU have come to perform.
  8. Democracy is not a threat to the cartel — it is a re-instantiation. A naive prediction has democratic citizens demanding open competition and lower monopoly tax. But the redistributive median-voter analysis shows that in a democracy that redistributes the cartel rent, the median voter in the core area prefers the cartel + redistribution over open competition (ch. 5, Fig. 10). The cartel survives democratisation because the identity of the “ruler” shifts from autocrat to pivotal voter / dominant lobby / large donor — but the cartel-versus-citizen logic remains. Empirically: Catalonia pays 27% of Spanish taxes and 20% of taxes today; Spain has used force to prevent independence (2017 referendum repression). The US fought a civil war to preserve territorial integrity; democracies are if anything more hostile to secession than autocracies (because democracies fight wars more successfully, democratic peace reduces interstate revision, and democratic accountability raises domestic costs of concession).
  9. Nationalism is the cartel’s stabiliser. Investments in national identity create a discontinuous jump in governance cost at the border — own population is cheaper to govern, foreign population is harder. This makes the joint-profit-max boundary insensitive to small administrative-capacity shifts: borders persist even when the rival state has caught up technologically (ch. 6, Fig. 12). The Nepal-vs-Sikkim paired case is the cleanest illustration: Sikkim (no national-identity investment) was annexed by India in 1975; Nepal (with century-long national-identity investment via Gorkhapatra 1901, Radio Nepal 1951, standardised Nepali curriculum) was not, despite the “Indira doctrine” of Himalayan suzerainty.
  10. International institutions as cartel headquarters. The UN is “the headquarters of the cartel” — membership is sufficient and almost necessary for international recognition; only 4 UN members are contested by >1 other member (Israel, Palestine, North Korea, China) and only 3 non-members are recognised by >1 member (Taiwan, Western Sahara, Kosovo) (ch. 6). The UNSC’s response asymmetry — rapid mobilisation against Iraq’s invasion of Kuwait in 1991 (35-nation coalition); no mobilisation against Russian Crimea takeover — reveals that UN action tracks cartel-leader interest, not abstract norm violation, exactly as the model predicts.
  11. Globalisation transforms but does not abolish the cartel. The market-for-governance has shifted from physical territory to abstract policy space (trade, capital, IP, immigration, regulatory regimes). Apple’s “double Irish” / Jersey BEPS arrangements moved profits without moving production; OECD estimates 2015 tax-competition cost at $240B/year (ch. 7). The cartel’s response is managed trade (Stiglitz 2002 quoted: a real free-trade agreement would be “a few pages”; the TPP ran 6,000+ pages) and coordinated tax-floors (the 2021 OECD/G20 BEPS framework’s 15% minimum corporate tax, agreed by 136/140 members). The cartel adapts; it does not dissolve.
  12. Forward-looking implications. Four 21st-century challenges: (i) immigration differentials driven by governance-quality differentials; (ii) failed states generating cross-border externalities the cartel cannot easily resolve (Afghanistan, Somalia, Syria, Venezuela); (iii) power shifts between US and China, especially over intellectual property — the asset class developed countries depend on as they shift to knowledge economies (ch. 7); (iv) new modes of conflict — economic warfare (Russia-Ukraine sanctions; “akin to a declaration of war” per Putin), cyber-warfare, IP theft. These are not refutations of cartel theory; they are the current operating challenges of a cartel that has migrated from territorial to abstract space.

Argument map

The reasoning runs from a core abstraction (the market for governance) through a self-enforcement mechanism (the cartel agreement) to a structural prediction (mutual recognition replacing institutional efficiency as the criterion of statehood), and then applies the framework historically (origin in Europe; spread by colonisation), to threats (conflict, entry, democracy), to stabilisers (nationalism, international institutions), and finally to the contemporary abstract-market transformation (globalisation, IP, China rise).

Key concepts

  • Cartel of States — the central framing: states as a self-enforcing oligopoly of governance providers (newly created concept note).
  • Market for Governance — the supply-side abstraction Acharya-Lee build the model on (newly created concept note).
  • Skin in the Game — Taleb’s framework illuminates who actually pays when the cartel breaks: powerful mobile-capital holders extract concessions; immobile ordinary citizens absorb the monopoly tax. The cartel is a precise instance of asymmetric skin-in-the-game distribution.
  • Black Swan — entry events into the cartel (Belgium 1830; USSR dissolution; Eritrean/East Timorese independence) are rare, hard to forecast, and dominate the long-run shape of state count over time. The volatility-fragility logic of cartel dynamics shares structure with Taleb’s framing.
  • Heterarchy — modern governance markets exhibit lateral, multi-tier authority (national, EU, WTO, OECD, treaty-based) that resembles the heterarchical structure McCulloch named, not a clean strict-hierarchy.
  • Antifragility — the cartel exhibits a kind of antifragility: shocks (wars, secession movements, democratic transitions) have repeatedly strengthened it rather than weakened it, by triggering institutional adaptations (UN, OAU, managed trade).

Best chapters/pages to actually read

  • Ch. 1 (Introduction) — sets up the cartel framing through the Neche/Gretna and Point Roberts illustrations. Read first; the rest of the book applies the framing developed here. The Krasner (1993) quotation on “extruding out of universal alternatives to the sovereign state” anchors the historical claim, and the foreshadowing of chs. 5–7 is unusually direct for an academic introduction.
  • Ch. 2 (The Cartel Model) — the formal-theoretic chapter. The spatial Bertrand model, the trigger-strategy condition δ > L_A/(L_A+L_B), the discussion of independent vs interdependent cooperation in the three-state case (Belgium 1914 as illustration), and the comparison with rival theories (Westphalia hypothesis, Jervis offense-defense balance, Spruyt institutional selection, Alesina-Spolaore voting model). This is the chapter that makes the book novel; without it the book would be a competent IR essay.
  • Ch. 3 (The Cartel Emerges) — the historical-origin chapter. The Anglo-Scottish border (1500–1640) walk-through is the densest case. The quantitative French intendent fiscal data (Fig. 7, Mallet 1789 / Bonney 1999) showing 12.05× revenue growth in border provinces vs 3.81× in non-border provinces is the cleanest empirical anchor for the model’s prediction.
  • Ch. 5 (Challenges to the Cartel) — handles the three threats (conflict, entry, democracy) with one model extension each. The Atacama Desert case for conflict, the Rif/Somaliland/Belgium triplet for entry, the Catalonia case for democratic survival. The Fig. 10 redistributive-vs-competitive policy analysis is the key formal move.
  • Ch. 6 (Resilience of the Cartel) — Nepal vs Sikkim is the best-paired case study in the book. Read this section even if skimming the rest of the chapter. The UN-as-cartel-headquarters analysis (Iraq-Kuwait vs Crimea response asymmetry) is the cleanest prediction-test.
  • Ch. 7 (The Cartel Today) — most relevant for current research. The managed-trade analysis, the BEPS / Apple-Ireland material, the US-China IP / power-shift section, and the closing thought experiment about a single world government as logical-coherence test of the theory. Skip the section on emigration if you’ve read Stiglitz-on-globalisation already.
  • Skip: ch. 4’s Africa and Durand Line cases are well-written but theoretically secondary if you’ve internalised chs. 2–3. Save them for a second read or use them as worked examples.

Important examples / cases

  • Anglo-Scottish border (ch. 3) — the densest case study. 1552 five-man commission demarcates Scot’s Dyke; March Law jury composition with Scottish/English balance; “law books of the border” periodically synchronised; 16th-century English diplomats’ explicit elite-vs-borderer framing (“the less privy the Borderers be made to the division hereof, the more likely it is that the thing will take place”). Demonstrates all three steps of cartel emergence — mutual recognition, cooperative demarcation, cooperative enforcement.
  • French intendent fiscal data (ch. 3, Fig. 7) — Mallet 1789 / Bonney 1999. 1600–1695 nominal revenue growth: 3.81× non-border provinces, 6.20× formerly-border-now-interior, 12.05× border provinces. The cleanest quantitative anchor in the book.
  • Atacama Desert (ch. 5) — Saltpeter War 1879–82. Three claimants (Chile/Peru/Bolivia); no conflict 1820s–1860s when desert worthless; nitrate boom raises v; Chile’s superior army wins, consolidates 1884/1903. Bolivian renegotiation attempt unanimously dismissed by ICJ in 2018 — cartel stability ratified judicially long after.
  • Republic of the Rif vs Belgium 1830 (ch. 5) — paired case for entry deterrence. Rif had institutional substance (currency, army, schools); Belgium did not. Rif crushed by joint Franco-Spanish action (¼ million troops, poison gas). Belgium recognised because Britain wanted a French buffer. Recognition tracks great-power interest, not entrant capability.
  • Nepal vs Sikkim (ch. 6) — paired case for nationalism. Sikkim absorbed 1975 (“referendum” with 99.55% allegedly favouring incorporation). Nepal not absorbed despite “Indira doctrine,” because Nepal had inculcated national identity via Gorkhapatra (1901), Radio Nepal (1951), standardised curriculum (1950s), UN membership (1955). Bollywood-actor-disparagement riots in 1999/2000 demonstrate the cost-to-India of any annexation attempt would be prohibitive.
  • Apple-Ireland BEPS (ch. 7) — the canonical contemporary example. “Double Irish” and “Green Jersey” arrangements moved profits to low-tax Ireland; a single 300BIPtransferraisedIrishGDPby26.3240B/year tax-competition loss. 60 Fortune 500 firms paid no tax on $79B profit.
  • Pakistan-Afghan Durand Line / FATA (ch. 4) — clearest case of competitive cartel failure. NWFP 1932 expenditure 36M rupees, revenue 8M; Central Provinces 7× population but 3× cost. FATA: 600 arms factories (2017); free electricity; no taxes; Frontier Crimes Regulation in force until 2018. Both Pakistan and Afghanistan compete for Pashtun loyalty exactly as medieval English/Scottish states competed for surnames.
  • UN response asymmetry: Iraq-Kuwait vs Russia-Crimea (ch. 6) — Iraq invasion of Kuwait condemned by UNSC in 4 days; sanctions imposed; force authorised in 3 months; 35-nation coalition led by US drove Iraq out. Russian Crimea annexation 2014: no comparable response, because Russia is a permanent UNSC member. Cartel theory predicts this exactly: enforcement tracks cartel-leader interest, not norm violation.

Critiques and limitations

  • Falsifiability concerns. The cartel model with cost-curve geometry, value-bound v, and trigger-strategy enforcement has many degrees of freedom. The book successfully post-hoc rationalises observed outcomes (border stability, failed-state recognition, war-followed-by-cooperation) but predictions are scarce; few cases would clearly refute the framework. The historical-origin claim (the system originated as a cartel) is more falsifiable than the operating-as-if claim, but the book’s strongest evidence is for the latter. A skeptic could read the book as a clever description-with-vocabulary rather than a positive theory; this is a real weakness for a framework that explicitly claims to compete with realism, institutionalism, and constructivism on theoretical grounds.
  • Identification weakness in the historical chapters. The French intendent fiscal data (Fig. 7) is striking, but the inferred mechanism (border treaties → fiscal capture from elites) competes with confounders (military pressure, dynastic centralisation, secular bureaucratic professionalisation, the Sun King’s personal ambition). Without a control group or counterfactual identification, the cartel-mechanism interpretation is one among several. Acharya-Lee acknowledge this implicitly through their notes on alternative explanations but don’t address it head-on. A natural-experiment design (varying treaty timing across paired regions) would strengthen the empirical claim materially.
  • The “ruler” elision. The model’s central actor — the “ruler” — is treated as a unitary profit-maximising agent, then in ch. 5 reinterpreted as the median voter or pivotal lobby in democracies. This rescues the framework but at the cost of changing the actor’s identity mid-argument. A more disciplined treatment would specify when the median-voter assumption is empirically supported (when redistribution is actually balanced-budget, when periphery vs core voters can be cleanly identified) and when it is not. Caplan’s Bryan Caplan - The Myth of the Rational Voter is the natural companion here: rational ignorance plus systemic biases mean the median voter often does not recognise the cartel-rent-redistribution bargain, raising doubt about the chapter’s predicted equilibrium.
  • Underweighting non-cartel governance forms. The framework treats non-state governance (Mafia, insurgent groups, merchant associations, Migdal-style local elites) as orthogonal to the main argument (footnote at ch. 2). But in many cases — drug-trafficking corridors, platform governance, Web3 / decentralised-finance jurisdictions, gig-economy labour relations — these alternative providers are exactly the “competitors” whose suppression is the cartel’s raison d’être. A more integrated treatment would help; in particular, Web3 / DAO governance is a non-territorial entrant whose suppression by the cartel (China’s mining ban; US securities enforcement against decentralised exchanges; EU’s MiCA framework) is precisely what cartel theory predicts but the book never analyses directly.
  • The IP / power-shift section is thin. Ch. 7’s discussion of US-China IP rivalry is the weakest section of the book, mostly because of citation availability at writing time. Krugman’s 1979 North-South product cycle is invoked, but the framework doesn’t integrate cleanly with cartel theory — IP is a non-rival good with strong returns to imitation, which differs from territorial governance in ways the cartel model doesn’t fully accommodate. The Acharya-Lee analysis of why China would refuse to accept US IP norms is correct as far as it goes, but it under-models the bargaining dynamics: Chinese acceptance of IP norms is partial and conditional, exchanged for market access and technology-transfer concessions, in ways the simple cartel-vs-entrant framing doesn’t fully capture.
  • No engagement with public-choice / regulatory-capture literature. Bryan Caplan’s Bryan Caplan - The Myth of the Rational Voter, Mancur Olson’s collective-action work, and the rent-seeking literature would all be productive complements; the cartel-vs-citizens framing has obvious affinity with public-choice traditions but the book under-engages them. Olson’s The Rise and Decline of Nations (1982) anticipates much of the cartel-collective-action mechanism the book develops; the absence of citation is striking.
  • Scope limitation — the abstract-market transformation may exceed the framework. Acharya-Lee note that as governance migrates from territory to mobile capital + IP + human capital, the original spatial Bertrand model loses its primary intuition (geography). They handle this by reinterpreting the line segment as policy space, but this is a strained reinterpretation: in policy space there is no “loss of strength gradient” with distance. The framework may need substantial modification to cover the next 30 years — perhaps a multi-dimensional discrete-policy-space model, perhaps a hybrid spatial-network model. The book gestures at this but does not deliver the formal extension.
  • Authorial voice is muted. Compared to Stiglitz, Zhang, or Rithmire, the prose is dry-academic. The case material is rich but the reader has to do interpretive work to feel the force of the argument. This is not a critique of accuracy, just of pedagogy: the book under-leverages its own findings rhetorically. A more polemical revised edition (in the spirit of Stiglitz’s Globalization and Its Discontents Revisited) could reach a much wider audience without sacrificing the formal-theoretic contribution; this is a missed opportunity given the importance of the framework.
  • Limited treatment of the EU as quasi-cartel-member. The book closes ch. 7 with a brief discussion of the EU as “alternative blueprint” but does not formally extend cartel theory to handle quasi-state entities. The EU’s customs union, Schengen area, monetary union, and emerging fiscal capacity are exactly the kind of intra-cartel-coordination the framework should illuminate, but Acharya-Lee leave this for future work. For Yang’s policy interests, this is the most consequential under-development of the framework.

Links to other books

  • State-cartel context for Angela Huyue Zhang - High Wire — Zhang focuses on the internal dynamics of Chinese tech regulation; Acharya-Lee supplies the external context (the international cartel within which Chinese state-building must operate). Reading Zhang’s analysis of the post-2022 Chinese rebalancing toward technological self-sufficiency through the cartel lens — China asserting cartel-leader status in the digital governance market — sharpens both books. Direct cross-reference exists: Zhang’s note explicitly cites Acharya and Lee - The Cartel System of States.
  • Subnational complement to Meg E. Rithmire - Land Bargains and Chinese Capitalism and Meg Rithmire - Precarious Ties — Rithmire on city-level land bargains and firm-state precarious ties supplies the vertical bottom of Chinese political economy; Zhang on national regulatory architecture supplies the middle; Acharya-Lee on international cartel supplies the top. The three together give the full vertical of how Chinese statehood actually operates, from real-estate developers to UNSC posture.
  • Methodological and ideological complement to Joseph E. Stiglitz - Globalization and Its Discontents Revisited — Stiglitz argues globalisation has been managed badly by IMF/World Bank technocrats with weak feedback; Acharya-Lee argues the same outcome — managed trade rather than free trade — is the predicted equilibrium of the cartel framework. The two books reach the same descriptive conclusion via different theoretical routes; reading them together gives both a normative and a positive account of why the world economy looks as it does. Acharya-Lee directly quote Stiglitz on “managed trade” and the TPP’s 6,000-page length (ch. 7).
  • Public-choice foundation from Bryan Caplan - The Myth of the Rational Voter — Caplan establishes that median-voter outcomes can systematically diverge from welfare-maximisation. Acharya-Lee’s ch. 5 democratic-political-economy extension assumes balanced-budget redistribution and median-voter pivot; Caplan’s framework would deepen the analysis of when these assumptions hold and when they don’t.
  • Methodological cousin to Gary Goertz and James Mahoney - A Tale of Two Cultures — Acharya-Lee combine formal-theoretic modelling (ch. 2’s Bertrand competition) with within-case process-tracing (ch. 3’s Anglo-Scottish, ch. 5’s Atacama, ch. 6’s Nepal-Sikkim). Goertz-Mahoney describe exactly this two-cultures hybrid as the most productive form of contemporary IR/comparative-politics research. Acharya-Lee is a worked exhibit of what high-quality combined-methods political economy looks like.
  • Geopolitical context for Chris Miller - Chip War — Miller documents the US-China semiconductor rivalry; Acharya-Lee supplies the cartel-theoretic grammar for understanding why the US is willing to bear the costs of export controls (it is reasserting cartel-leader prerogatives over the IP-sub-market) and why China is willing to bear the costs of self-sufficiency drives (it is positioning to enter the cartel as a co-leader rather than fringe member). The two books together explain the IP/semiconductor-policy dimension of the abstract governance market.
  • Implicit Talebian framing — Acharya-Lee’s framework invokes Black Swan structurally without naming Taleb: cartel entry events (Belgium 1830; USSR dissolution; Eritrea/East Timor) are rare, hard-to-predict, and dominate the long-run shape of state count over time. The framework’s treatment of war as revelation mechanism shares structure with Antifragility: shocks have repeatedly strengthened the cartel by triggering institutional adaptations (League of Nations, UN, OAU, OECD). Reading Nassim Nicholas Taleb - Incerto alongside makes both deeper.

Open questions

  • Will the cartel survive the migration from territorial to abstract governance markets? Acharya-Lee answer “yes, by adaptation” — but the original spatial-Bertrand model relied on the loss-of-strength gradient, which has no clear analogue in IP / capital / cyber space. A 2030+ revision of the framework may need substantially different formal foundations. The most likely route is multi-dimensional policy-space competition, but this loses the geometric clarity of the original spatial model.
  • Is managed trade a stable cartel equilibrium or a transitional regime? The 2021 OECD/G20 BEPS framework is an unprecedented case of cartel-leaders coordinating against capital mobility. Whether this kind of coordination will hold or fracture (under nationalist populist pressure, US-China rivalry, AI-policy divergence) is empirically open and central for predicting the 2030–2040 governance-market structure.
  • How does AI-governance map onto the cartel framework? AI compute infrastructure is geographically concentrated (Taiwan, US, S Korea, Netherlands per Chris Miller - Chip War) but AI capabilities are non-rival once trained. The cartel logic applies cleanly to compute (export controls = cartel-leader move) but breaks for capabilities. A formal extension that treats training-stage IP and inference-stage services differently would be valuable.
  • What does opportunistic entry by a non-state network look like in this framework? The Islamic State explicitly rejected the sovereignty norm; cryptocurrency networks implicitly do. The framework treats these as coordination failures in entry deterrence, but a positive theory of non-state network entry would help interpret blockchain regulation, DAO governance, and platform-state competition.
  • How does the framework handle the EU? Acharya-Lee close ch. 7 with the EU as “alternative blueprint” — quasi-state without statehood. Whether the EU stays in this transitional position, evolves into a proper cartel member (federal Europe), or fragments back to nation-states is a live empirical question the book leaves open. The Brexit datum is informative: the cartel’s pull is strong enough that exit costs remain large even when domestic politics favour exit.
  • Can democratic citizens learn to want cartel-breaking? The redistributive median-voter argument predicts citizens prefer cartel + redistribution. But this assumes citizens accurately perceive the bargain. If the welfare cost of cartel monopoly becomes legible (post-pandemic immigration debates, post-tech-platform regulatory awareness), democratic preferences could shift. This is the long-run political question hidden inside the framework.
  • Does the framework predict regulatory convergence or divergence on climate? Climate is a negative externality the cartel must coordinate over (per ch. 7’s closing thought experiment). The Paris-Kyoto-Copenhagen sequence shows partial coordination; the question is whether the cartel can sustain action without the kind of distinct-territorial-rents that motivated earlier cooperation. The framework predicts cartel coordination occurs only when cartel-leader interests align — and on climate, alignment is partial and contested.
  • What is the framework’s prediction for cartel-internal dispute mechanisms? The book mentions ICJ, WTO dispute resolution, the OECD’s coordinating role — but the cartel’s internal-dispute infrastructure is treated as exogenous. Whether the cartel’s internal disputes (e.g., US-China trade-war-via-WTO-blocking-of-appellate-body) are themselves cartel-defection events or cartel-strengthening rituals is a question the framework leaves open.

Research uses

  • Yang’s blockchain-regulation work: cartel theory supplies the right vocabulary for analysing why states have responded to cryptocurrency the way they have. DCEP (digital yuan) and central-bank digital currencies are cartel-defensive innovations — preserving the territorial monopoly over the medium of governance payment. Private cryptocurrencies (Bitcoin, Ethereum stablecoins) are opportunistic entrants that the cartel must either co-opt (regulated stablecoins, bank-issued tokens) or suppress (China’s 2017/2021 mining bans). The cartel framework predicts that cross-border CBDC interoperability protocols (mBridge, Project Agorá) will be governed by cartel-leader interests — China, US, EU, BIS — rather than by efficiency criteria. Useful explanatory frame, especially for the question of why crypto regulation has converged on similar features (stablecoin reserve requirements, exchange licensing, tax disclosure, anti-money-laundering) across otherwise very different jurisdictions: the cartel coordination mechanism is doing real work even where the political-ideological framings differ.
  • China-policy thinking: the framework is portable to Taiwan recognition (cartel maintenance — UN refusal to admit Taiwan is exactly the entry-deterrence prediction), South China Sea claims (cartel asymmetric-cost-shift dynamics), Belt and Road (China asserting cartel-co-leader position), AIIB/parallel-institution moves (institutional bid for cartel-co-leadership). Useful for evaluating Chinese state-action without lapsing into either alarm or apology — Chinese behaviour is intelligible as cartel-position-seeking by an emerging great power, neither uniquely revisionist nor purely defensive. The framework also illuminates why China invests heavily in nationalism (ch. 6’s mechanism — discontinuous cost jumps at borders) and why the post-2022 technological-self-sufficiency vector is exactly what cartel theory predicts an emerging-power-IP-importer would do.
  • Lobbying-network research with In Song Kim: the cartel framework reframes lobbying as the intra-cartel-member mechanism by which citizens (workers, farmers, capital holders) compete to shape the cartel-leader’s posture in international negotiations. Yang’s existing horizontal lobbying-network models could be extended to vertical cartel-cross-tier dynamics — modelling how domestic lobbying coalitions translate into international-cartel positions on trade, IP, immigration, environmental policy. Empirically: the persistence of farm subsidies in developed countries (despite their welfare cost and trade-distortion effects) is a clean test case for the framework — these subsidies persist because farm interests are decisive in cartel-leader politics, not because of efficiency.
  • AI-policy comparative analysis: each major AI jurisdiction (US sectoral + judicial, EU AI Act, China cyber-administration model, UK light-touch) reflects a different cartel-leader posture. The framework supplies the right unit of analysis: not “what AI rules does each country have” but “how does each country position relative to cartel-leader prerogatives over the AI-compute and AI-capabilities markets.” This is a more analytically tractable comparative frame than rights-based or risk-based taxonomies. The framework also predicts that AI-policy convergence will track cartel-leader coordination cycles — heavy convergence when US-China-EU interests align, divergence when they don’t.
  • Goldman quant work / macro views: managed-trade equilibrium has direct implications for cross-border tax-avoidance trades, BEPS-driven corporate restructuring, and tax-haven-jurisdiction macro positioning. The framework supplies a structural account of why these arbitrages persist (cartel adaptation, not regulatory failure) and when they should compress (when cartel-leader coordination tightens, as in the 2021 OECD agreement). Useful for relative-value trades on Ireland-vs-Cayman / Bermuda-vs-Jersey corporate-domicile spreads, for sovereign-CDS pricing on weak-cartel-member states, and for currency basis-swap analysis where capital-control regimes vary by cartel position.
  • Self-defence against secondary literature: Acharya-Lee is now the right benchmark for evaluating IR / comparative-politics journalism and policy commentary on state-system change. Pieces that don’t engage cartel logic (defaulting to Westphalian-norms or realist-balance-of-power framings) are operating with a partial frame. Useful self-discipline — the frame applies to coverage of UNGA votes, ICJ cases, sanctions regimes, BRICS-expansion politics, post-Brexit EU-UK relations, all the way to corporate-tax journalism.
  • Methodological example for combined-methods research: Acharya-Lee is one of the cleanest worked examples of formal model + within-case process tracing in contemporary political science. For Yang’s qualitative-IR work and any plan to combine modelling with case studies, the book is an explicit template — the model in ch. 2 is fully specified; the case studies in chs. 3–5 illustrate the model’s mechanism rather than re-deriving it. This division of labour between model and case is more disciplined than typical IR work and worth emulating.

What I would miss if I only read this note

The case-study density. Acharya-Lee’s argumentative force comes from the cumulative weight of historically specific cases: the 1518 Scottish privy council quotation about the Armstrongs being “in the Debatable landis, and agreit [in agreement] with Ingland”; the 1551 council minutes about “the great and heavy crimes committed upon our sovereign lady’s poor lieges by thieves”; the equivalente tax in Bourbon Valencia explicitly designed to equalise burdens with Castile; the 84% / 1.25% noble-vs-crown land split in pre-reduktion Livland; the Chilean state’s mining-tax negotiation with Bolivia in 1874 followed by Bolivia’s 10-cent-tax breach in 1878 that triggered war; Senators Schumer and Casey’s 2012 Ex-PATRIOT Act proposing 30% capital-gains tax on US citizenship-renouncers; the 1779 France–Austrian Netherlands border treaty mentioning a specific ditch separating two villages; the Phoebus/Great Lightbulb cartel granting GE “North America, Cuba, and the West Indies” while the French Compagnie General received “France, Greece, and joint rights in Bulgaria, Romania, Yugoslavia, and Turkey.” These cases do work on the reader that aggregated bullets cannot — they show, repeatedly, that the framework’s predicted mechanism actually operated in archival reality, and that the rulers and diplomats involved were aware of it.

The note also compresses the formal apparatus of ch. 2 — the Bertrand competition, the discount-factor condition (the literal δ > L_A/(L_A+L_B) and δ > L_B/(L_A+L_B) derivation), the multiple-equilibria structure of the three-state game, the geometric construction of Y and the loss-from-competition shaded areas, the Fig. 4-vs-Fig. 5 contrast that powers the historical-origin chapter. Re-deriving the model for oneself, even on a napkin, is the only way to develop the trained intuition for what the framework predicts in a new case (e.g., when applied to AI-compute or cryptocurrency or platform-governance). The compressed bullet-list version of the model loses this trainability — the reader who hasn’t sat with the figures cannot easily extend the framework to novel domains.

Equally, the note compresses Acharya-Lee’s explicit critique of rival theories. The book’s section-by-section dismantling of the Westphalia hypothesis (Ruggie, Philpott, Nexon, Osiander), the Jervis offense-defense balance, Spruyt’s institutional selection, and the Alesina-Spolaore voting-derived borders is itself a useful introduction to the contemporary theory of the state system. Without engaging the comparative critique, the positive contribution of cartel theory is harder to see clearly — what makes the cartel framework new is precisely what it absorbs from each rival theory while supplying the missing self-enforcement mechanism. The book functions as a structured tour of the field; the note can only point back to it.

Finally, the note compresses the quantitative figures — the French intendent fiscal data (Fig. 7), the treaty-mentions-of-borders running average (Fig. 6), the diplomatic-equality-by-dyad time series (Bayer 2006), the agriculture-vs-services-vs-manufacturing US GDP shares (Fig. 13), the net-migration-vs-governance-quality scatter (Fig. 14), the post-1850 number-of-states trajectory (Fig. 11). For a reader trying to internalise the framework as a working analytical tool rather than a remembered set of bullet-points, the empirical anchors matter. Specific numbers stay with the reader in ways that the verbal summary does not — “12.05× revenue growth in border provinces” is more memorable than “border provinces grew faster than non-border ones.”
