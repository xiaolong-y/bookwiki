Maurice Obstfeld and Kenneth Rogoff - Foundations of International Macroeconomics

One-sentence thesis

A graduate-grade open-economy macroeconomics textbook organised around a single load-bearing methodological commitment — that every problem in international finance can be addressed within a unified microfounded intertemporal framework — from which the authors derive the intertemporal-trade view of the current account (Ch. 1, CAt​=St​−It​ with the current account interpreted as the change in net foreign assets), the infinite-horizon Euler-equation / permanent-income current account (Ch. 2, u′(Ct​)=(1+r)βEt​u′(Ct+1​) with consumption smoothing as the canonical mechanism), the OLG breakdown of Ricardian equivalence (Ch. 3, government deficits cause current-account deficits when they redistribute across cohorts), the Harrod-Balassa-Samuelson real-exchange-rate result (Ch. 4, p^​=(μLN​/μLT​)A^T​−A^N​), the complete-markets pricing kernel and equity-premium puzzle (Ch. 5), the sovereign-risk debt-pricing equilibrium 1+r∗=(1−q)(1+r) (Ch. 6), the stochastic neoclassical and endogenous growth models with international convergence (Ch. 7), the Cagan-style asset-price representation of the exchange rate under flexible prices and target-zone smooth-pasting (Ch. 8), the Mundell-Fleming-Dornbusch overshooting model with monetary-policy credibility (Ch. 9), and the capstone Obstfeld-Rogoff (1995) “redux” two-country sticky-price general-equilibrium model with closed-form welfare results dUR=m^W/θ (Ch. 10) — sequencing the reader from real-side intertemporal trade through OLG fiscal-policy interactions, multi-good real-exchange-rate determination, complete-markets asset pricing, capital-market frictions, growth dynamics, monetary determination of nominal exchange rates, sticky-price macro-stabilization theory, and microfounded welfare analysis of international monetary policy, on the wager that one methodological axiom (microfoundations) plus one organizing object (the intertemporal budget constraint) plus one organizing equation (the Euler equation in its many guises) generates a self-consistent treatment of every current-account, exchange-rate, sovereign-debt, and monetary-policy-spillover question that an open-economy macroeconomist can be asked.

Why this book matters

This is the canonical first-edition (MIT Press, 1996) of Obstfeld and Rogoff’s Foundations of International Macroeconomics — the dominant graduate textbook in international macroeconomics for the past three decades and the work that defined what “open-economy macro” means in modern PhD pedagogy. Maurice Obstfeld (Class of 1958 Professor of Economics, UC Berkeley; IMF Economic Counsellor and Director of Research 2015–2018; CEA member 2014–2015) and Kenneth Rogoff (Maurits C. Boas Chair of International Economics, Harvard; IMF Chief Economist 2001–2003; co-author of This Time Is Different with Carmen Reinhart) wrote this textbook in their early-1990s prime as research economists, and the book is unusually dense with their own unpublished or simultaneously-published research — most notably the redux model of chapter 10, published as Obstfeld-Rogoff Journal of Political Economy 1995. The textbook is the canonical companion to Romer’s Advanced Macroeconomics (closed-economy macro) at the PhD-coursework level, and the prerequisite for any serious work in international finance, sovereign debt, currency-crisis modelling, or open-economy monetary policy.

For a research-oriented reader (quant-finance interview prep, sovereign-debt research, blockchain-economics modelling on cross-border capital flows, China political-economy modelling, international-trade-and-policy work, IMF/World Bank policy analysis), this textbook serves three functions distinct from a applied policy text like Krugman-Obstfeld-Melitz International Economics (the undergraduate companion) or Reinhart-Rogoff This Time Is Different: it provides (i) a shared dialect for discussing current-account dynamics, exchange-rate determination, and sovereign-debt pricing with academic and IMF-staff counterparties; (ii) a complete formal-analytical apparatus (Euler equations, transversality conditions, intertemporal budget constraints, Cagan-Sargent-Wallace asset-price representations, complete-markets pricing kernels, monopolistic-competition CES aggregators, log-linear approximations) that subsequent papers assume the reader has internalized; and (iii) a baseline curriculum-shape for graduate international macro instruction, against which to judge later additions to the canon (Galí-Monacelli 2005 small-open-economy New Keynesian; Schmitt-Grohé-Uribe Open Economy Macroeconomics 2017; Vegh Open Economy Macroeconomics in Developing Countries 2013).

The 1996 timing matters: the textbook anticipates, but does not yet absorb, the 1997–98 Asian financial crisis, the 1998 LTCM collapse, the 2001 Argentine default, the 2007–09 global financial crisis, or the 2010–12 European sovereign-debt crisis. As a result, the chapter-6 sovereign-risk and chapter-8 speculative-attack apparatus is prophetic rather than retrospective — readers in 2026 can assess how much of the post-1996 crisis sequence was already in the model versus genuinely surprising. The redux model of chapter 10 has been substantially extended (LCP-vs-PCP; financial frictions; ZLB) but its core structure and welfare apparatus remain the reference point for every subsequent New Open-Economy Macroeconomics paper.

Core argument

The argument is methodological (every open-economy macroeconomics question should be addressed within a unified microfounded intertemporal framework) rather than a single substantive claim about how the world works. It proceeds:

  1. The current account is fundamentally an intertemporal phenomenon (Ch. 1, Ch. 2). The identity CAt​=St​−It​=Bt+1​−Bt​ recasts the current account as the change in net foreign assets, not as the trade balance. Combined with the intertemporal budget constraint and the Euler equation, this delivers testable predictions: temporary income shocks should produce current-account surpluses (consumption smoothing), permanent shocks should leave the current account unchanged, anticipated future income shocks should produce current-account deficits today. An unbalanced current account is not a pathology — it is the welfare-maximizing response to differences between the autarky and world interest rates.

  2. Microfoundations are non-negotiable (Preface, throughout). “With little exception, nothing appears in the book that is not based on explicit microfoundations” (Preface). The textbook’s polemical move against the “classic [open-economy macro] literature from twenty and thirty years ago” is that ad-hoc Keynesian models cannot be relied on for welfare analysis or for cross-equation restrictions — only models with optimizing agents and explicit budget constraints support the comparative-dynamics and counterfactual exercises policymakers actually need. Chapter 10’s welfare-analysis-of-monetary-policy is the textbook’s clearest demonstration of why this matters.

  3. Ricardian equivalence holds in the representative-agent benchmark; OLG breaks it (Ch. 3). Lump-sum tax-vs-debt timing is irrelevant when the representative consumer internalizes future tax liabilities (Ch. 1–2 framework). With overlapping generations, however, public debt redistributes from unborn-to-current cohorts and does cause current-account deficits. The chapter resolves the apparent contradiction between Ricardian-equivalence theory and the strong empirical “twin-deficit” correlation of the 1980s by isolating the demographic-redistribution channel.

  4. Real exchange rates are pinned down by sectoral productivity differentials in the long run (Ch. 4). The Harrod-Balassa-Samuelson result p^​=(μLN​/μLT​)A^T​−A^N​ is the textbook’s most empirically successful real-exchange-rate prediction: countries with relatively higher tradables-vs-nontradables productivity growth experience real appreciation. This is the long-run anchor that any short-run sticky-price model (Ch. 9–10) must converge to.

  5. Asset pricing in international markets is governed by the consumption-CAPM (Ch. 5). Vt​=Et​{βu′(Ct+1​)/u′(Ct​)Xt+1​} — the same pricing kernel as closed-economy asset pricing, with the international twist that complete markets predict perfectly correlated cross-country consumption growth (empirically false) and that two-fund separation predicts world-market-cap-weighted equity portfolios (empirically false: home bias). The textbook’s empirical-puzzle inventory (equity premium, home bias, Backus-Kehoe-Kydland anomaly) anchors the post-1996 international-finance research agenda.

  6. Capital-market imperfections explain why the perfect-markets benchmark fails (Ch. 6). Sovereign risk (without third-party enforcement, debt is sustainable only via reputation / sanctions / output-cost-of-default) and asymmetric information (hidden actions, hidden types) limit international borrowing in measurable ways. The Bulow-Rogoff (1989) reputation-alone-cannot-sustain-debt critique and the debt-buyback paradox are the textbook’s contributions to formal sovereign-debt theory; the chapter is the reference for every subsequent sovereign-default paper.

  7. Growth in open economies is shaped by capital mobility, technology diffusion, and convergence dynamics (Ch. 7). The neoclassical Solow-Swan model with international capital mobility predicts near-instant convergence of capital-labor ratios across countries; observed slow convergence (≈ 2%/year) is evidence for chapter-6-type frictions plus immobile factors (human capital, technology). The endogenous-growth literature (AK, Romer 1990) provides an alternative in which permanent saving differences support permanent growth differences.

  8. The exchange rate is a forward-looking asset price (Ch. 8). Cagan money demand combined with PPP and uncovered interest parity yields et​= discounted present value of expected future fundamentals. This forward-looking representation organizes everything from monetary models of hyperinflation (Cagan original case) to target zones (Krugman 1991 smooth-pasting) to speculative attacks (Krugman 1979 first-generation crisis model).

  9. Sticky prices are necessary to explain short-run monetary non-neutrality and exchange-rate overshooting (Ch. 9). The Mundell-Fleming-Dornbusch model — modernized but not yet microfounded — delivers Dornbusch (1976) overshooting: an unanticipated permanent monetary expansion overshoots the long-run nominal exchange rate. Combined with monetary-policy credibility analysis (Kydland-Prescott / Barro-Gordon), this delivers the analytical foundation for inflation-targeting and central-bank-independence institutions.

  10. The redux model unifies sticky prices, monopolistic competition, and intertemporal optimization (Ch. 10). The capstone two-country general-equilibrium sticky-price model with monopolistically-competitive producers delivers the closed-form welfare result dUR=m^W/θ: in the symmetric monopoly-distortion-only case, monetary expansion anywhere benefits all countries equally, regardless of origin. Monetary policy is not a beggar-thy-neighbor zero-sum game in this benchmark — though the conclusion reverses when distorting income taxes or other frictions dominate. The chapter founded the New Open-Economy Macroeconomics literature.

  11. Cumulative claim. A reader who completes the textbook has the formal apparatus to model any current-account, exchange-rate, asset-pricing, sovereign-debt, monetary-policy, or growth question that arises in international macroeconomics, and the empirical literacy to know which of the textbook’s results survive contact with the data versus which remain puzzles. The textbook’s real claim is that this apparatus reduces — under one methodological commitment plus one organizing object — to a coherent body of theory rather than the “scattered field” the preface diagnoses.

Formal landmarks

The textbook’s six load-bearing formal results, traceable to specific chapters and equations:

  1. Intertemporal budget constraint (Ch. 1.1, eq. 2; Ch. 2 generalization): ∑t​(1+r)−t(Ct​+Gt​+It​)=(1+r)B0​+∑t​(1+r)−tYt​ with transversality limT​(1+r)−TBT+1​=0.

  2. Euler equation, deterministic and stochastic forms (Ch. 1.1, eq. 3; Ch. 2): u′(Ct​)=(1+r)βEt​u′(Ct+1​). Underwrites permanent-income consumption smoothing, the consumption-based real interest rate, and the consumption-CAPM pricing kernel.

  3. Current-account identity (Ch. 1.1): CAt​=Bt+1​−Bt​=Yt​+rt​Bt​−Ct​−Gt​−It​=St​−It​.

  4. Real interest rate parity / Fisher parity (Ch. 4, Ch. 8): in the absence of arbitrage and risk premia, expected real returns on real bonds are equalized internationally; in nominal form, uncovered interest parity 1+i=(1+i∗)E{E+1​}/E.

  5. Two-country general-equilibrium redux model (Ch. 10): closed-form welfare result dUR=m^W/θ under symmetric monopoly distortion only; the benchmark for international monetary-policy welfare analysis.

  6. Harrod-Balassa-Samuelson result (Ch. 4): p^​=(μLN​/μLT​)A^T​−A^N​ — the textbook’s most empirically successful long-run real-exchange-rate prediction.

Key concepts

  • Intertemporal Budget Constraint — Ch. 1.1 eq. (2), Ch. 2 generalization. The single most foundational object in the textbook; every later chapter is some elaboration.
  • Euler Equation (International Macro) — Ch. 1.1 eq. (3); Ch. 2 stochastic version. The textbook’s canonical first-order condition; appears in dozens of guises (consumption smoothing, asset pricing, sovereign-debt repayment, monetary-policy credibility).
  • Current-Account Identity — Ch. 1.1; Ch. 2.2 dynamics. CA=S−I, recasting the current account as the change in net foreign assets and as an intertemporal-trade phenomenon.
  • Redux Model (Obstfeld-Rogoff) — Ch. 10. Two-country general-equilibrium sticky-price model with monopolistic competition and the closed-form welfare result dUR=m^W/θ.
  • Real Interest Rate Parity — Ch. 4 + Ch. 8. The combined real / nominal version of the law-of-one-price for international assets; the no-arbitrage benchmark against which all asset-pricing puzzles are measured.
  • Capital Asset Pricing Model (CAPM) — referenced via the consumption-CAPM in Ch. 5, with international extensions.
  • Sticky Economy — referenced via Ch. 9–10 sticky-price machinery.
  • Beggar-thy-neighbor — Ch. 10’s redux model challenges the standard intuition: in the symmetric monopoly-distortion benchmark, monetary spillovers are positive, not negative.

Key arguments created from this book

  • Microfoundations are non-negotiable for international macroeconomics — the textbook’s distinctive doctrinal claim, candidate for an argument note.
  • The current account is the change in net foreign assets, not the trade balance — the methodological move underlying the entire framework.
  • Monetary policy spillovers are not zero-sum (in the monopoly-distortion-only benchmark) — the redux model’s signature welfare result.
  • Ricardian equivalence holds in the representative-agent benchmark; OLG demography breaks it — the formal resolution of the twin-deficits debate.

Best chapters/pages to actually read

For a researcher returning to international macro, the highest-leverage chapters are:

  • Chapter 1 (pp. 1–58) — Intertemporal Trade and the Current Account. The conceptual setup; read in one sitting. Skipping this chapter to “get to the formulas” is the single most common mistake; the chapter’s CA=S−I identity and Euler-equation development are the conceptual scaffold for everything that follows.
  • Chapter 2 (pp. 59–128) — Dynamics of Small Open Economies. The infinite-horizon generalization with permanent-income consumption rule and the stochastic Euler equation. The chapter that turns chapter 1’s two-period toy into a workhorse model.
  • Chapter 4 (pp. 199–268) — Real Exchange Rate and Terms of Trade. Harrod-Balassa-Samuelson, real-exchange-rate determination, GDP-vs-GNP diagrams. Highest-leverage chapter for any cross-country price-level or real-exchange-rate question.
  • Chapter 5 (pp. 269–348) — Uncertainty and International Financial Markets. The textbook’s longest chapter and the densest in asset-pricing results. Equity premium, home bias, complete-markets risk-sharing, intragenerational risk-sharing. Highest-leverage chapter for international asset pricing or sovereign-bond pricing.
  • Chapter 6 (pp. 349–428) — Imperfections in International Capital Markets. Sovereign risk, hidden information, moral hazard, debt buybacks. Highest-leverage chapter for sovereign-debt research or emerging-market currency-crisis modelling.
  • Chapter 8 (pp. 513–604) — Money and Exchange Rates under Flexible Prices. Cagan model, monetary exchange-rate model, target zones, speculative attacks. The chapter on which all currency-crisis literature builds.
  • Chapter 10 (pp. 659–714) — Redux Model. The textbook’s signature contribution and the foundation of New Open-Economy Macroeconomics. Read with chapter 9 (Mundell-Fleming-Dornbusch) as a paired diptych.
  • Skip on first pass: the Marshall-Lerner stability appendix (1A), the dynamic-programming and difference-equation supplements at the end of the book (read on need-basis), the target-zone speculative-attack continuous-time machinery (Ch. 8.5–8.6) unless directly relevant. Chapter 7’s growth content is largely standard Solow / Romer; readers familiar with these can skim.

Important examples / cases

  • Joseph in the Bible / Egypt grain consumption-smoothing (Ch. 1) — second-millennium-BCE policy of Pharaoh storing 20% of bumper-year grain for famine years, used to illustrate consumption smoothing under perceived high default risk on foreign loans (Babylonian interest rates 20–33% per year on grain loans, but enforcement weak under military-political conditions).
  • Norway’s 1973–94 saving-investment-current-account dynamics (Ch. 1.2 Figure 1.2) — North Sea oil development decade. Huge current-account deficits to −14% of GDP in 1977, then surpluses through 1985 repaying the debt. Illustrates how investment-driven deficits can be efficient.
  • Japan post-1950 real-exchange-rate appreciation (Ch. 4.2 Figure 4.5) — Penn World Table data showing Japan’s relative price level more than doubling vs other industrial countries 1950–92, traceable to Harrod-Balassa-Samuelson tradables-vs-nontradables productivity differentials.
  • Feldstein-Horioka 1980 cross-country saving-investment correlation (Ch. 3 application) — slope coefficients ≈0.9 (1960–74) and ≈0.6 (1982–91) in OECD samples. Apparent rejection of capital mobility resolved (per Taylor 1994) by demographic-and-growth controls.
  • US 1950–90 consumption-volatility data and equity premium puzzle (Ch. 5.4) — Var(consumption growth)≈0.000708 means Lucas (1987) calibration of welfare cost of consumption variability gives only ≈0.3% of permanent consumption with risk aversion ρ=10; equity premium of ≈6% requires implausibly high ρ to rationalize.
  • Bolivia 1988 debt buyback at 11¢/$1 (Ch. 6 application) — illustrates Bulow-Rogoff buyback paradox: residual creditors’ price bid up after announcement; sovereign captures less than half of apparent surplus.
  • East Asian growth miracles (Korea, Taiwan, Hong Kong, Singapore) (Ch. 7.3) — Young (1995) decomposition shows factor accumulation (rising K/L, labor-force participation), not TFP, drives most growth. Krugman (1994) extrapolation: transitional, not permanent, growth-rate divergence.
  • Cagan hyperinflations (Ch. 8.2) — Germany 1921–23, Hungary 1945–46, Argentina, Brazil. Empirical anchors for the seignorage / inflation-Laffer-curve analysis.
  • Krugman (1979) speculative-attack timing (Ch. 8.6) — closed-form T=(R0​−Rˉ)/μ; matches first-generation currency-crisis models for Mexico 1976, Argentina 1981, Israel 1983.

Critiques and limitations

The textbook has the characteristic limitations of any 1996-vintage graduate macro textbook, plus a few specific to its theory-first authorial perspective:

  1. No financial frictions in the post-2008 sense. The textbook’s chapter-6 frictions are sovereign-risk and asymmetric-information; it lacks Bernanke-Gertler-Gilchrist financial accelerator, intermediary-balance-sheet constraints (Brunnermeier-Sannikov, He-Krishnamurthy), or shadow-banking / repo-market plumbing (Gorton, Adrian-Shin) that became central post-GFC. A 2026 reader of Roger Lowenstein - When Genius Failed or post-2008 sovereign-debt literature would find the chapter-6 framework incomplete.

  2. No zero-lower-bound treatment. The textbook’s monetary models all assume the nominal interest rate is interior. Japan’s 1990s liquidity trap and the 2008–2015 ZLB period in advanced economies require the Krugman (1998) / Eggertsson-Woodford (2003) extensions that postdate the textbook. The redux model’s welfare result dUR=m^W/θ implicitly assumes the central bank is unconstrained.

  3. Local-currency pricing vs producer-currency pricing. Chapter 10’s redux model assumes producer-currency pricing (PCP) — exchange-rate movements pass through fully to import prices. Subsequent New Open-Economy Macroeconomics literature (Devereux-Engel 2003; Gopinath et al. 2010) showed local-currency pricing (LCP) — exchange-rate movements are absorbed by exporter margins — better fits the data. The chapter foreshadows this but does not develop it.

  4. No sovereign-debt-restructuring institutional detail. The chapter-6 sovereign-default analysis is highly stylized; the actual mechanics of Brady deals, Paris Club, London Club, Argentine 2001-05 holdouts, and the Greek 2012 PSI / OSI distinctions are absent. Reinhart-Rogoff (2009) and Sturzenegger-Zettelmeyer (2007) treatments are necessary supplements.

  5. No twin / triple crises analysis. The 1997 Asian financial crisis (currency-banking-corporate triple crisis) and the 2010–12 European sovereign-banking doom-loop both involve interactions between sovereign risk (Ch. 6), exchange-rate-regime choice (Ch. 9), and bank-balance-sheet fragility that the textbook’s compartmentalized chapter structure cannot easily integrate.

  6. No global imbalances framework. The 2000s “global savings glut” debate (Bernanke 2005), the post-2008 unwinding, and the China-vs-US bilateral-imbalance discussion all developed after 1996. The textbook’s framework can be applied to these questions but the textbook itself does not.

  7. No exchange-rate-disconnect explanation. The Meese-Rogoff (1983) random-walk-beats-structural-models result is acknowledged in chapter 9.3 as a puzzle without resolution; subsequent literature (Engel-West 2005; Gourinchas-Rey 2007 valuation channel) has refined but not resolved this.

  8. No capital-flow-management / macroprudential treatment. The 2010s literature on capital controls (Farhi-Werning, Korinek), macroprudential regulation, and the “international monetary system” (Rey 2013 global financial cycle; Miranda-Agrippino-Rey 2020) is absent. The textbook’s frictionless / monopoly-distortion benchmark cannot rationalize macroprudential policy.

  9. No central-bank balance-sheet / unconventional-monetary-policy treatment. QE, forward guidance, yield-curve control, and FX swap lines are post-2008 instruments the textbook predates.

  10. U.S. / advanced-economy bias in examples. Most empirical applications draw on OECD data; emerging-market and developing-economy specifics (informal sectors, dual-currency regimes, financial-repression-driven seignorage) are sparse. Vegh’s Open Economy Macroeconomics in Developing Countries (2013) is the natural complement.

  11. No environmental / climate macroeconomics. Carbon-budget constraints, climate-disaster pricing, and stranded-asset-driven sovereign risk are absent. A 2026 reader interested in climate-finance applications must import this content.

  12. The book is unusually long and dense. 800+ pages of tightly-argued mathematical exposition. Self-study without an instructor is feasible but demanding; the asterisked sections marked “advanced” are not optional for serious graduate work.

Links to other books

  • Foundational microeconomics prerequisite: Daron Acemoglu - Microeconomics — Acemoglu et al supply the price-theory, mean-variance utility, monopolistic-competition CES aggregator, and information-economics machinery (adverse selection, moral hazard, signaling, principal-agent) that Obstfeld-Rogoff’s chapters 5, 6, and 10 take as given. Reading them sequentially: Acemoglu Ch. 16 develops adverse selection / signaling / moral hazard rigorously; Obstfeld-Rogoff Ch. 6 applies the same apparatus to sovereign-debt and international-lending problems. The pairing is the canonical price-theory → international-macro sequence.

  • Corporate-finance complement: Jonathan Berk and Peter DeMarzo - Corporate Finance — Berk-DeMarzo’s Law-of-One-Price / no-arbitrage scaffolding (Ch. 3, Ch. 21) is the formal backbone of asset-pricing applications in Obstfeld-Rogoff’s Ch. 5 (consumption-CAPM) and Ch. 8 (covered interest parity). Berk-DeMarzo’s Ch. 31 (international corporate finance) explicitly uses the covered-interest-parity formula F=S(1+rd​)/(1+rf​) that Obstfeld-Rogoff develops in Ch. 8.7. Reading the two together: Berk-DeMarzo gives the firm-level apparatus; Obstfeld-Rogoff gives the country-level / general-equilibrium apparatus. The pairing is the canonical finance + open-macro diptych.

  • Institutional-critique counterpoint: Joseph E. Stiglitz - Globalization and Its Discontents Revisited — Stiglitz’s IMF-conditionality critique builds on his Nobel-winning information-economics work — the same apparatus that underlies Obstfeld-Rogoff Ch. 6’s hidden-information / moral-hazard sections. Reading them together is productive: Obstfeld-Rogoff provides the formal optimization framework that IMF programs implicitly assume; Stiglitz argues the framework is misapplied (capital-account liberalization as adverse-selection trap; conditionality as failed principal-agent contracting). The Asian financial crisis of 1997 is the case where Stiglitz says the textbook’s framework was ideologically deployed against the data.

  • Empirical-RCT counterpoint: Abhijit V. Banerjee - Good Economics for Hard Times — Banerjee-Duflo’s empirical-development methodology stresses RCT identification of causal effects in specific contexts; Obstfeld-Rogoff stresses unified theoretical frameworks. The two represent the orthogonal poles of post-1990 economics — calibrated structural models vs causally-identified reduced-form effects. Reading both: Obstfeld-Rogoff gives the structural model; Banerjee-Duflo questions whether structural-model parameters are stable across contexts. Banerjee-Duflo Ch. 2 on migration is a particularly direct empirical engagement with chapter 1.5’s labor-mobility framework.

  • Microfinance / micro-development complement: Abhijit V. Banerjee - Poor Economics — provides the household-level evidence on consumption smoothing, savings constraints, and credit-market frictions that international macro’s representative-agent approach abstracts from. Reading Banerjee-Duflo’s chapters on insurance and savings alongside Obstfeld-Rogoff Ch. 2 (permanent-income consumption smoothing) and Ch. 6 (capital-market imperfections) shows how aggregated frictions emerge from individual-household constraints.

  • Developmental-state model: Lee Kuan Yew - From Third World to First — Singapore’s policy choices on capital flows, exchange-rate management (managed float against a trade-weighted basket), foreign-reserves accumulation, and forced-saving (CPF) are direct applied open-economy macroeconomics. Reading Lee Kuan Yew alongside Obstfeld-Rogoff: the Singapore case is the textbook’s chapter 7 East Asian growth-miracle cell with chapter 8’s exchange-rate-regime-choice problem solved deliberately by a long-tenured policy maker. The contrast with Obstfeld-Rogoff’s representative-democracy-policymaker assumption is stark.

  • Voter-rationality counterpoint: Bryan Caplan - The Myth of the Rational Voter — Caplan argues voters are systematically biased on economics (against-trade, against-foreigners, anti-market). Obstfeld-Rogoff’s Ch. 1 gains-from-trade theorem and Ch. 9 optimal-currency-area analysis assume welfare-maximizing policymakers; Caplan’s argument should make a reader uncomfortable about that assumption. The textbook’s redux model produces a clean welfare result — but if voters reject the Pareto-improving monetary policy (because they anti-foreigner-bias against benefiting Foreign citizens equally), the model’s normative force erodes.

  • Capital-allocation-and-VC application: Sebastian Mallaby - The Power Law — Mallaby’s history of venture capital is direct applied corporate finance using Berk-DeMarzo’s apparatus, but the Asian and emerging-market venture-capital-flow patterns it documents are direct applied chapter-7 international-capital-mobility / chapter-6 capital-market-imperfections cases. Reading both: the textbook gives the framework; Mallaby gives the cases of why VC flows tend to remain home-biased even when the textbook’s frictionless benchmark predicts perfect cross-border allocation.

  • Note on companion textbooks not currently in this vault: Krugman-Obstfeld-Melitz International Economics (the undergraduate companion, with Obstfeld as co-author); Romer Advanced Macroeconomics (closed-economy graduate companion); Schmitt-Grohé-Uribe Open Economy Macroeconomics (2017, the post-GFC update); Vegh Open Economy Macroeconomics in Developing Countries (2013, the developing-country companion); Reinhart-Rogoff This Time Is Different (the historical-data companion to chapter 6 sovereign-debt content); Galí Monetary Policy, Inflation, and the Business Cycle (the closed-economy New Keynesian DSGE companion to chapter 10). Cross-link only after they are ingested.

Open questions

  • Has the redux model’s monopoly-distortion-only benchmark survived 30 years of empirical and theoretical work? The textbook’s signature welfare result dUR=m^W/θ is highly sensitive to which distortion is operative. Subsequent literature (Devereux-Engel 2003; Corsetti-Pesenti 2005; Gali-Monacelli 2005) has explored alternative friction configurations with substantially different welfare conclusions.

  • What is the right replacement for the textbook’s frictionless capital-market benchmark in a post-2008 world? The chapter-6 sovereign-risk / hidden-information frictions are a partial answer; the financial-intermediary / shadow-banking / global-financial-cycle frictions of the post-GFC literature are not in the textbook.

  • Is the exchange rate really a forward-looking asset price? Chapter 8’s clean asset-pricing representation is theoretically elegant but Meese-Rogoff (1983) showed structural exchange-rate models cannot beat random-walk forecasts at short horizons. Engel-West (2005) and Gourinchas-Rey (2007) have refined the puzzle without fully resolving it.

  • How should the textbook’s framework be extended to a multipolar / fragmenting global economy? The 2020s policy environment — US-China decoupling, sanctions regimes, dollar-dependence weaponization, CBDC and stablecoin payment-system fragmentation — is an environment in which the textbook’s “world capital market” abstraction may be losing analytical traction.

  • Should the redux model be re-derived under local-currency pricing? Most subsequent NOEM literature uses LCP rather than the textbook’s PCP. A pedagogically-modernized version of the textbook would foreground this.

  • What is the right relationship between international macro and the climate / AI / digital-asset frontiers? Carbon-budget constraints on long-run intertemporal choices, AI-driven productivity asymmetries, and crypto-asset-driven cross-border-payment innovations are all areas where the textbook’s framework can be applied but where the textbook itself offers no guidance.

Research uses

This textbook serves five distinct research-prep functions for a reader with my (Yang’s) profile:

  • Quant-finance interview prep on macro / FX / rates desks. Chapters 1, 2, 4, 5, 8, 10 cover the core international-macro and FX content that quant-finance interviews ask: current-account / saving-investment identity, permanent-income consumption smoothing, real-exchange-rate determinants (Balassa-Samuelson, PPP), uncovered interest parity, monetary models of the exchange rate, sticky-price overshooting. Useful as a structured interview-prep map; pair with Jonathan Berk and Peter DeMarzo - Corporate Finance for the corporate-finance content and Roger Lowenstein - When Genius Failed for derivatives-failure cases that test the textbook’s frictionless benchmarks.

  • Blockchain-economics and cross-border-payments research. Chapter 8’s monetary models of exchange-rate determination and chapter 6’s sovereign-risk-and-information-asymmetry framework supply the analytical vocabulary for stablecoin design (asset-backed stablecoins as fiat-pegged claims subject to reserve-management problems), cross-border-CBDC architecture (the central-bank-balance-sheet implications of multi-CBDC corridors are direct chapter-8-type problems), and DeFi-protocol governance (multi-jurisdiction sovereign risk applied to validator-staked protocols). The textbook’s frictionless-capital-market benchmark is the foil against which crypto-friction-laden alternatives must justify themselves.

  • AI-policy and global-AI-investment-flow research. Chapter 7’s growth-and-convergence machinery applied to AI-compute capex (where US-China bifurcation creates a new chapter-3-type cross-country asset-allocation problem) and chapter 10’s redux welfare framework applied to AI-driven monetary-policy spillovers (when AI reduces the cost of arbitrage, what happens to monetary transmission?) are direct applications. Pair with Sebastian Mallaby - The Power Law for AI-firm capital-formation cases.

  • China political-economy modelling. Chapter 6’s sovereign-risk machinery applied to RMB-denominated debt-issuance experiments and Chinese local-government-financing-vehicle credit risk; chapter 8’s exchange-rate-regime analysis applied to the managed-float / capital-control combination China runs; chapter 10’s redux framework applied to PBoC monetary-policy spillover analysis. Pair with Angela Huyue Zhang - High Wire (regulatory state) and Meg E. Rithmire - Land Bargains and Chinese Capitalism (land-finance fiscal channel that the textbook’s framework can be adapted to).

  • Lobbying-network / corporate-political-strategy research. Chapter 9’s optimal-currency-area framework supplies the analytical apparatus for currency-coalition formation (e.g. the political economy of the euro, of dollarization, of regional currency arrangements like the Gulf Cooperation Council debate); chapter 6’s sovereign-debt material supplies the apparatus for IMF-conditionality bargaining (which is itself a multi-actor political-economy negotiation among creditor governments, the IMF staff, and the debtor government). Cross-link to In Song Kim’s lobbying-network research on industry-coalition formation around trade and exchange-rate policy.

What I would miss if I only read this note

A graduate-grade international macroeconomics textbook is largely derivations, end-of-chapter problems, and applications-to-data, not propositions — and this note can replicate none of these. Specifically, I would miss: (a) the cumulative reps from end-of-chapter problems that build automatic reflexes for Euler-equation manipulation, intertemporal-budget-constraint algebra, log-linearization around steady states, transversality-condition application, comparative-dynamics under unanticipated shocks, complete-markets state-pricing, target-zone smooth-pasting, monetary-model present-value calculation, and redux-model welfare-decomposition algebra — fluency that note-reading alone does not produce; (b) the Application boxes scattered through every chapter (Joseph in Egypt; Norway oil; Japan post-1950 real-exchange-rate appreciation; Feldstein-Horioka; equity-premium puzzle; Bolivia debt buyback; East Asian growth decomposition; Cagan hyperinflations) that anchor the abstract theory in named historical and empirical cases; (c) the Notation Guide and Symbol Glossary (pp. 781+) that tracks the textbook’s careful notational discipline across 800 pages and is itself a curated map of which symbols carry which meanings in modern open-economy macro; (d) the mathematical-supplements appendices — the difference-equation primer (Supplement to Ch. 2C), the dynamic-programming primer, the maximum-principle / continuous-time-optimization primer (Supplement to Ch. 8A) — which are the field’s best brief references for the mathematical machinery and which a reader will want to consult repeatedly; (e) the figure-and-table apparatus — the saving-investment diagrams, the indifference-curve / current-account graphical derivations, the production possibilities frontiers, the GDP-vs-GNP diagrams, the target-zone S-curves, the Dornbusch overshooting time paths, the demand-curve / monopolistic-competition CES geometry — which is how the field actually communicates and which note prose flattens; (f) the footnote-and-source-trail to the original research-frontier papers (Fisher 1930; Cagan 1956; Mundell 1963; Dornbusch 1976; Lucas 1982; Eaton-Gersovitz 1981; Krugman 1979 / 1991; Obstfeld-Rogoff 1995) that lets a curious reader go from textbook example to source paper to follow-up literature to current-frontier replication; (g) the References section (pp. 755+) with hundreds of citations to the international-finance literature, itself a curated bibliography of the field’s foundational works through 1996; (h) the author’s voices — Obstfeld’s careful empirical engagement (Application boxes, especially in chapters 1–7) and Rogoff’s dry-witted methodological-and-historical asides (e.g. the Joseph-in-Egypt application, the seignorage box’s “the United States Treasury recently estimated that the cost of printing a paper one dollar bill is 3.8 cents”), which humanize the propositions and which bare summary erases entirely; and (i) the detailed table of contents (pp. v–xi) — every section heading, sub-heading, application title, and box label across the 10 main chapters — which is a finer-grained map than the chapter-by-chapter table above and which lets a reader looking for a specific topic (e.g. “stochastic Cagan model”, “Krugman target zones”, “Bulow-Rogoff debt buyback paradox”, “redux welfare with distorting taxes”) jump directly to the relevant pages.
