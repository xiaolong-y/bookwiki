Joel Mokyr - A Culture of Growth

Read chapter-by-chapter → Deep dive of A Culture of Growth: The Origins of the Modern Economy (18 chapters)

One-sentence thesis

Sustained economic growth began in Western Europe rather than elsewhere because, in the centuries between Columbus and Newton, a transnational community of intellectuals — the Republic of Letters — emerged within a uniquely fragmented political environment, transformed elite cultural beliefs about Useful Knowledge and the manipulation of nature, and made the application of science to production into a shared, virtuous, and self-reinforcing project that broke the historical pattern of one-off technological progress fizzling into stagnation.

Why this book matters

Most accounts of the Industrial Revolution explain what changed in Britain after 1760 — the spinning jenny, the steam engine, the puddled-iron process, the cotton mill — and where the change happened first. Mokyr’s book confronts the much harder question of why this happened in Western Europe at all, and why now. His answer is that the standard candidates — institutions, geography, demography, accumulated capital, even Smithian commercialization — are not enough to explain the surge of technological creativity that distinguished modern growth from every previous boomlet of innovation. What is needed, he argues, is a careful account of how elite cultural beliefs changed in the two centuries between roughly 1500 and 1700, before the Industrial Revolution itself was visible.

This book matters for the BookWiki growth shelf because it sits upstream of Robert J. Gordon - The Rise and Fall of American Growth in the causal chain. Gordon traces what happened to the standard of living during the Special Century (1870–1970); Mokyr explains how the cultural and institutional preconditions for sustained innovation were assembled in the centuries before. It complements Anton Howes - Arts and Minds (whose Society of Arts is the practical-improvement institution that crystallized Mokyr’s “Industrial Enlightenment”) and provides the historical backbone for the philosophical argument in David Deutsch - The Beginning of Infinity (whose defense of the Enlightenment as a tradition of good explanations is essentially the Mokyr thesis abstracted to a meta-philosophy of progress).

It is also methodologically important. Mokyr is one of the few economic historians who takes culture seriously and refuses the lazy alternative of treating it as exogenous “national character.” His framework — culture as a menu of beliefs, values, and preferences from which individuals choose under historically variable transmission biases, with cultural entrepreneurs reshaping the menus that others face — is portable beyond the specific case of European industrialization. It applies to every comparative-development question and to most of the long-run historical comparisons in Will and Ariel Durant - The Lessons of History.

Core argument

Mokyr’s argument unfolds in eight numbered moves.

  1. The puzzle has two parts, and they have a single solution. The historical riddle is that Douglass-North-style institutions (property rights, contracts, low rent-seeking) explain Smithian growth — better markets and allocations — but cannot explain the surge of technological creativity that produced sustained per-capita income growth after 1750. The economic riddle is that useful knowledge is a non-rivalrous quasi-public good that should be chronically under-produced. Both riddles dissolve when one attends to the cultural and institutional changes between 1500 and 1700 that solved the appropriability problem and shifted elite incentives toward the production of knowledge for material progress.

  2. Culture must be defined precisely or it explains nothing. Mokyr borrows Boyd and Richerson’s definition: a set of beliefs, values, and preferences, capable of affecting behavior, socially transmitted, shared by some subset of society. Beliefs are positive (factual) statements; values are normative statements about social relations; preferences are normative statements about consumption and personal life. Culture is decomposable into discrete elements, and individuals choose elements from menus. This is not the “culture as fog” of cultural studies — it is a tractable analytical object.

  3. Cultural change is evolutionary but not biologically Darwinian. Mokyr deploys generalized Darwinism — variation, inheritability, superfecundity — but rejects the strict neo-Darwinian synthesis (Weismann barrier, random mutation, particulate inheritance) as misleading for cultural evolution. Cultural inheritance is quasi-Lamarckian: acquired traits pass on, mutations are not strictly random, and inheritance is rarely particulate. Memes are “purely imaginary and not all that useful.” The unit of selection is the cultural element, not its carrier.

  4. Choice-based cultural evolution is governed by transmission biases. Drawing on Richerson and Boyd, Mokyr taxonomizes the biases by which individuals select cultural variants: content-based, direct (authority), consistency, model-based, rhetorical, frequency-dependent, rationalization, coercion, and salient-events. These biases are not parameters but historically variable. The early modern transformation in Europe is best read as a change in biases — content bias strengthened by new instruments, direct bias shifted away from ancient authorities toward credentialled contributors, rhetorical bias transformed by the printing press and vernacular publication.

  5. Cultural entrepreneurs are the agents of large-scale cultural change. A small class of agents not only chooses cultural traits for themselves but actively expands the menu others face. Bacon and Newton are Mokyr’s archetypes — not because they were the most important thinkers by some objective standard, but because Enlightenment Europe itself elected them as the founding cultural entrepreneurs of its age. Successful cultural entrepreneurship requires latent demand, synthesizing prior tendencies, layered disciples, and an open-enough institutional environment. The entrepreneurs coordinate as much as they innovate — they create focal points for previously diffuse beliefs.

  6. Bacon and Newton were complementary cultural entrepreneurs. Bacon’s contribution was a coherent intellectual program: useful knowledge as the goal of inquiry, experimental method, savant-fabricant collaboration, and open distribution of knowledge. He created no science and was a poor scientist, but his synthesis gave focus to the Royal Society and through it to two centuries of European intellectual life. Newton’s contribution was demonstrative — Principia killed the ancients, fused mathematics with experiment, and showed that nature could be reduced to elegant universal laws. Newton’s status (knighted, master of the mint, buried like a king in Westminster Abbey) provided model-based bias for talented young people: science was a path to fame and fortune, shifting the allocation of upper-tail human capital.

  7. The Republic of Letters was the institutional substrate that made cultural entrepreneurship cumulative. A small (1,200 to 12,000) transnational community of European scholars, the Republic of Letters solved the appropriability problem by substituting reputation for exclusion. Members placed propositional knowledge in the public domain in exchange for credit, peer recognition, and patronage. The community enforced four implicit rules — freedom of entry, contestability, transnationality, and open science — through a community-managed knowledge commons in Elinor Ostrom’s sense. Its effectiveness depended on Europe’s combination of political fragmentation with intellectual unity (Latin lingua franca, shared classical heritage). Fragmentation prevented coordinated suppression of heretics; community transcended borders to ensure that exiled scholars found welcoming courts.

  8. The China comparison vindicates the institutional argument. China by 1700 was not backward — markets were integrated, literacy was high, books were cheap, civil service was meritocratic, “sprouts of capitalism” existed in mining and textiles. Yet sustained growth did not happen there. The differences Mokyr identifies are: top-down state control of education vs. European decentralization; coordinated suppression of heterodoxy possible in a unified empire; backward-looking kaozheng scholarship that recovered ancient sages rather than challenging them; the unbridged gap between savants and fabricants; and the defensive sinification of foreign knowledge (Mei Wending’s claim that European mathematics was “derivative from the Chinese”). China had cultural entrepreneurs and a market for ideas — the Ming reformer Xu Guangqi resembled Bacon in many respects — but the institutional substrate selected for tradition-respecting scholarship rather than canon-shredding innovation.

The book closes with Cardwell’s Law: technology in any economy crystallizes at some point; progress slows and fizzles. Breaking out of Cardwell’s Law requires both pluralism-and-competition and a coordination mechanism for distributing knowledge. Most societies achieved at most one. Europe in 1500–1700 uniquely combined them. The Industrial Enlightenment was the result.

Argument map

The argument runs from cultural change and institutional substrate (both maturing 1500–1700) through the joint emergence of Bacon’s Baconian program and Newton’s demonstrative physics, into the Industrial Enlightenment and finally into sustained growth. The China branch is the negative comparison case: similar literacy and markets, missing institutional configuration.

  1. Cultural shifts (1500–1700): belief in progress; loss of reverence for ancients; anthropocentric religion; openness to foreign useful knowledge.
  2. Institutional substrate (1500–1700): political fragmentation; Republic of Letters as transnational community; open-science norms; reputation-as-reward.
  3. Cultural entrepreneurs (Bacon and Newton) reshape menus; their disciples coordinate diffuse beliefs into focal-point doctrines.
  4. Industrial Enlightenment synthesizes savants and fabricants, producing self-reinforcing useful knowledge.
  5. Industrial Revolution and sustained growth follow, escaping Cardwell’s Law.
  6. Negative case: China has cultural entrepreneurs (Xu Guangqi) and a market for ideas, but the institutional substrate selects backward-looking scholarship.

Key concepts

  • Useful Knowledge — the propositional+prescriptive knowledge that compounds across generations and is the engine of modern growth.
  • Republic of Letters — the transnational scholarly community that solved the appropriability problem of useful knowledge by substituting reputation for exclusion.
  • Cultural Entrepreneur — agents who reshape the cultural menus others face; Bacon and Newton are the archetypes.
  • Common Knowledge — useful contrast: Mokyr’s “useful knowledge” is propositional+prescriptive; common knowledge in the Schelling sense is the meta-knowledge that makes coordination possible.
  • Special Century — Gordon’s term for 1870–1970, the downstream consequence of the cultural shift Mokyr traces.
  • Headwinds to Growth — Gordon’s framework for why the Special Century may be unrepeatable; Mokyr’s framework helps specify what cultural conditions a future century would need.

Best chapters/pages to actually read

For first reading, focus on chapters 6, 7, 8, 11, and 12. Chapter 6 introduces the cultural-entrepreneur concept, which is the conceptual hinge of the entire book and the most portable contribution Mokyr makes to social science. Chapter 7 (Bacon) and chapter 8 (Newton) are the case studies that ground the abstraction; chapter 7 in particular is the more important because Bacon’s program (rather than Newton’s mathematics) is what shaped Enlightenment institutional culture. Chapter 11 (Fragmentation, Competition, and Cultural Change) lays out the institutional argument about Europe’s political pluralism. Chapter 12 (Competition and the Republic of Letters) is the institutional core — read this carefully for Mokyr’s framing of the Republic as a community-managed commons solving the appropriability problem of knowledge.

For second reading and the comparative-history payoff, add chapters 16 and 17 (China). The China comparison is where the institutional argument is tested against a sophisticated, literate, market-integrated society that did not industrialize. Xu Guangqi’s near-counterpart-to-Bacon trajectory in late Ming China is the single most striking case study in the book for how cultural entrepreneurs need an institutional substrate to succeed. Chapter 14 (battle of the books) is essential if you want the cultural-history substance behind the abstract concept “belief in progress.” Chapters 1–5 are the methodological scaffolding — read them once carefully but don’t return for re-reading; they exist to make the rest of the book defensible to economists. The epilogue is short, programmatic, and worth reading after the body.

Important examples / cases

  • Bacon’s New Atlantis “merchants of light” — the imagined twelve fellows of Salomon’s House whose function was to sail abroad and return with books, abstracts, and “patterns of experiments.” Mokyr reads this as the prefiguration of European openness to foreign useful knowledge.
  • Hartlib’s “invisible college” — the network of intellectuals around Samuel Hartlib that, after Bacon’s death in 1626, became the institutional precursor to the Royal Society. Hartlib’s role as “intelligencer” (information node) embodies the architecture of the Republic of Letters.
  • Voltaire’s 1738 Élements de la Philosophie de Newton — the coup de grâce in the French Cartesian-vs-Newtonian battle. Demonstrates the cultural entrepreneur dynamic at work: Voltaire was not himself a physicist, but his rhetorical popularization tipped the cultural balance for an entire intellectual public.
  • Dollond and the achromatic lens — a self-taught silk-weaver was commissioned by the Royal Society to test Newton’s claim that achromatic lenses were impossible. Dollond eventually overturned Newton, vindicating the principle of contestability that defined the Republic of Letters.
  • Tommaso Campanella — heretic monk who spent twenty-seven years in a Neapolitan jail, escaped to France via French ambassador’s intervention, and was honored at the court of Louis XIII. The case Mokyr uses to dramatize Europe’s coordination failure for forces of conservatism.
  • Mei Wending and Kangxi — the Chinese mathematician who convinced the Kangxi emperor that European astronomy was “derivative from the Chinese.” Mokyr’s emblem of the defensive sinification that prevented Chinese intellectuals from confronting their own cultural canon.
  • Xu Guangqi — late Ming deputy prime minister who collaborated with Matteo Ricci on translating Euclid, reformed the Chinese calendar using Tycho Brahe and Kepler, converted to Christianity, and wrote a 700,000-character agricultural treatise advocating experimentation. The single most striking case in the book of a Chinese cultural entrepreneur whose project was structurally analogous to Bacon’s but whose institutional substrate prevented self-reinforcing success.
  • Newcomen’s 1712 Dudley Castle engine — Mokyr’s “hopeful monstrosity” example demonstrating that cultural and technological evolution permit discrete leaps, not just gradualist accumulation.
  • The Royal Society’s History of Trades project — the attempt to catalog all artisanal practices in the kingdom (dyeing, candlemaking, tanning, brewing) in Sprat’s terms, “to reduce the gap between best-practice and average-practice techniques.” A direct embodiment of Bacon’s program.

Critiques and limitations

The book has three substantial limitations worth taking seriously.

First, the elite-culture focus risks understating mass-cultural transmission. Mokyr is explicit that he is studying “upper-tail human capital” — perhaps a few thousand people across Europe. This is methodologically defensible but means the book has little to say about how the cultural shift propagated downward to the artisans, merchants, and workers who actually built the Industrial Revolution. Anton Howes’s Arts and Minds argues that an “improving mentality” had to spread through the middling classes for industrialization to take off. Mokyr’s framework can accommodate this but does not develop it.

Second, the Bacon-Newton choice as paradigmatic cultural entrepreneurs is partly an artifact of who eighteenth-century Europe canonized. Mokyr defends the choice on exactly these grounds — what matters is whom the contemporary market for ideas elected — but a critic could argue that this is circular: the framework defines successful cultural entrepreneurs as the ones whose followers won, which makes it hard to test against unsuccessful would-be entrepreneurs (Paracelsus? Bruno? Spinoza? Comenius?). The framework’s predictive power is weak; its explanatory power is largely retrospective.

Third, the China-vs-Europe comparison, while careful, still depends heavily on the work of Western sinologists (Cohen, Elman, Needham, Pomeranz, Brook, Bray) and on a particular reading of kaozheng scholarship. A scholar working primarily in Chinese sources might construct a different account in which Wang Yangming and the Mohist revival were further along the path to a Chinese Enlightenment than Mokyr allows, and in which the Manchu suppression was a contingent political event rather than a structural revelation. Mokyr’s response — that even acknowledging contingency, the configuration of institutions and culture differed between Europe and China — is reasonable but not fully decisive.

A subtler critique is that Mokyr’s “culture matters” claim is weaker than his synthesis suggests. He concedes that culture and institutions co-evolve, that timing involves substantial lags, that no single belief is necessary or sufficient, and that the cultural shift is best understood as a configuration. This makes the framework hard to falsify and arguably consistent with materialist or institutionalist explanations that would pick different proximate causes from the same configuration.

Links to other books

  • Anton Howes - Arts and Minds — Howes’ Society of Arts is the practical-improvement institution that crystallized Mokyr’s “Industrial Enlightenment” in eighteenth-century Britain. Mokyr supplies the cultural framework; Howes supplies the institutional case study and emphasizes the middling-class “improving mentality” that propagated downward from elite culture.
  • Robert J. Gordon - The Rise and Fall of American Growth — Gordon’s Special Century (1870–1970) is the downstream consequence of the cultural transformation Mokyr traces. Read together, the books provide a 500-year arc: Mokyr explains how the cultural and institutional capacity for sustained innovation was assembled; Gordon explains what the resulting innovations did to standards of living and why the Special Century may be unrepeatable.
  • David Deutsch - The Beginning of Infinity — Deutsch’s defense of the Enlightenment as a tradition of “good explanations” (and his critique of static societies like Sparta) is essentially the Mokyr thesis abstracted to a meta-philosophy of progress. Where Mokyr is the historian, Deutsch is the philosopher of the same phenomenon.
  • Will and Ariel Durant - The Lessons of History — the Durants’ civilizational arc complements Mokyr’s case: their generalizations about civilizational rise and fall, the role of religion, the dialectic of conservatism and innovation, and the role of contingency apply directly to Mokyr’s narrower European case. Mokyr’s framework gives the Durants’ grand history a tractable causal mechanism.
  • Hamilton Helmer - 7 Powers — Helmer’s “Cornered Resource” — a body of asymmetric assets that confers persistent strategic advantage — fits Mokyr’s account of useful knowledge as the West’s compounding edge for two centuries. The fit is slightly forced (Helmer is writing about firms, Mokyr about civilizations), but the shared insight is that durable advantage comes from cumulative know-how that is costly to replicate without the supporting institutional substrate.

Research uses

For research on the economics of innovation, Mokyr’s framework supplies the cultural and institutional preconditions that pure incentive-based accounts (patents, prizes, market structure) cannot generate by themselves. Researchers studying why some regions or industries innovate more than others should attend to the configuration of cultural openness, institutional pluralism, and transnational community — not just to the local incentives.

For research on AI and computing strategy, the analogy is direct. The contemporary moment looks more like 1660 than like 1860: a technically sophisticated elite, fragmented institutions, an open scientific culture under stress from commercial enclosure, and uncertainty about whether the institutional substrate (open publication, peer review, transnational scholarly community) will survive. The Mokyr framework asks: which contemporary institutions play the role of the Republic of Letters, and what threats to their open-science norms most endanger sustained progress?

For research on comparative development, Mokyr’s China-vs-Europe comparison is a template. Any explanation of why country X did not industrialize when Y did should specify the configuration of cultural elements (especially attitudes toward useful knowledge, openness to foreign ideas, reverence for ancestors) plus the institutional substrate (degree of fragmentation vs centralization, transnational vs local scholarly networks). Pure-institutional and pure-cultural explanations are both inadequate; the question is the joint configuration.

For research on cultural evolution itself, Mokyr’s careful refusal of strict biological isomorphism while preserving the analytical machinery of generalized Darwinism is a model worth studying. His critique of memes and his preference for “cultural elements” as the unit of selection have implications for any social-science use of evolutionary metaphors.

Open questions

  • Is Mokyr’s framework predictive at all, or only retrospective? Could it have predicted, in 1600, that the Industrial Revolution would happen in Britain rather than the Netherlands or Saxony or Northern Italy?
  • How much of Britain’s 18th-century lead is due to Puritanism specifically (Merton thesis, defended by Mokyr) versus to political contingencies (the Civil War’s outcome, the Glorious Revolution, the Act of Union with Scotland)?
  • Is the Republic of Letters genuinely necessary for sustained growth, or could other institutional arrangements — a more centralized state-led research apparatus, a commercial knowledge market — produce equivalent outcomes? The Mokyr epilogue suggests not, but the case is largely intuitive.
  • How does Mokyr’s framework apply to the Chinese reform-era growth miracle (1980–present)? Is this “catching up” within a borrowed institutional framework, or has China generated its own version of useful-knowledge culture?
  • Are we currently weakening the institutional preconditions for sustained growth (open publication, scholarly mobility, transnational community) faster than we are extending them? Mokyr does not address contemporary policy directly.
  • What is the relationship between the cultural shift Mokyr describes and the demographic shift (fertility decline, marriage patterns) that demographers like Hajnal have argued was a precondition for European modernization?

What I would miss if I only read this note

I would miss Mokyr’s historiographic generosity. The book is unusually careful in citing scholarly debates, naming the alternatives Mokyr considered and rejected, and acknowledging where his synthesis depends on the work of others (Cohen on the Scientific Revolution, Cardwell on the law of stagnation, Boyd-Richerson on cultural evolution, Greif on moral entrepreneurship, McCloskey on bourgeois virtues, Margaret Jacob on industrial culture, Slack on improvement). This makes the book read less like a polemic and more like a synthesis — and it makes the cultural-evolution framework portable in ways that a more aggressive book would not be.

I would also miss the texture of the cultural-entrepreneur case studies. Bacon’s actual rhetoric — “is truth ever barren? Shall he not be able thereby to produce worthy effects, and to endow the life of man with infinite commodities?” — and Newton’s intellectual autobiography (the Cambridge years, the alchemy notebooks, the heterodox theology, the celebrity at the mint) carry the argument in ways that a summary cannot. The same is true for Xu Guangqi: knowing the size of the Nongzheng quanshu and the breadth of Xu’s career in late Ming officialdom turns “China had cultural entrepreneurs but they couldn’t compound” from an abstract claim into a vivid contrast.

I would miss Mokyr’s intellectual modesty. The closing line of the epilogue — “It is a tale that will be told, and retold many times, and surely the arguments I have advanced here will be challenged and questioned by many others. That, in the end, is what illustrates the glory of a well functioning market for ideas” — is the kind of line that earns its sentiment by being embedded in 350 pages of careful argument. The book practices what it preaches.
